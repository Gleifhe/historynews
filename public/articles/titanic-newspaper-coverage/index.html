'ALL SAVED!' — The Shocking Lies Newspapers Printed Before the Truth About Titanic Emerged

How rural American newspapers covered the Titanic disaster, from wildly inaccurate early reports to the grim corrections days later.

Historical Date: April 15, 1912 Primary Source: Chronicling America (LOC)

The Biggest Ship in the World

The RMS Titanic was the largest, most luxurious ship ever built. When it set sail from Southampton, England, on April 10, 1912, it was carrying over 2,200 passengers and crew on its maiden voyage to New York City.

The ship was so big and so modern that many people believed it was unsinkable. Newspapers had called it “practically unsinkable” before it even launched. Some passengers reportedly said they felt safer on the Titanic than in their own homes.

They were wrong.

11:40 PM — Iceberg

On the night of April 14, 1912, the Titanic was steaming through the North Atlantic at high speed. The sea was calm. The sky was clear. There was no moon.

At 11:40 p.m., a lookout spotted a massive iceberg directly ahead. The ship tried to turn, but it was too late. The iceberg scraped along the right side of the hull, popping rivets and opening gashes below the waterline. Within minutes, seawater was flooding into the lower compartments.

The Titanic’s designer, Thomas Andrews, inspected the damage and delivered the terrible news: the ship had about two hours to live.

Not Enough Lifeboats

The Titanic carried only 20 lifeboats — enough for about 1,178 people. But there were over 2,200 people on board. Even worse, many of the lifeboats were launched only half full because the crew was poorly trained and passengers didn’t believe the ship was actually sinking.

Women and children were supposed to go first. In first class, most women and children survived. In third class — the poorest passengers, many of them immigrants — the survival rate was much lower. Some third-class passengers were locked behind gates that kept them in the lower decks.

At 2:20 a.m. on April 15, the Titanic broke apart and sank. About 1,500 people died in the freezing water.

The Newspapers Got It Wrong

Here’s where the newspaper story gets fascinating. In 1912, there was no television, no radio news, and no internet. News traveled by telegraph, and telegraph signals could be garbled or misunderstood.

On April 15, many newspapers printed their first editions based on incomplete information. Some of the early headlines were shockingly wrong:

  • “ALL SAVED FROM TITANIC AFTER COLLISION” — The Evening Sun, New York
  • “TITANIC’S PASSENGERS ARE TRANSFERRED TO OTHER LINERS” — Christian Science Monitor
  • “ALL TITANIC PASSENGERS SAFE” — The Daily Mail

These papers reported that the Titanic had been damaged but that rescue ships had saved everyone. They were completely wrong.

Small-Town Papers Tell the Real Story

Chronicling America, the Library of Congress’s free database of historic newspapers, lets you read how small-town papers across America covered the Titanic disaster. Many of these papers got their news a day or two late, which actually helped — by the time they published, the truth had come out.

Papers in small towns in Kansas, Nebraska, and Oregon printed the real death toll alongside heartbreaking stories of survivors. Local papers were especially interested in whether anyone from their town or state had been on board.

The Corrections Were Devastating

By April 17, every newspaper in America had published the truth: more than 1,500 people were dead. The headlines shifted from hope to horror:

  • “1,500 PERISH WHEN TITANIC SINKS”
  • “ONLY 705 SAVED OUT OF 2,200”
  • “WOMEN AND CHILDREN DIED IN STEERAGE”

The contrast between the early “all saved” headlines and the final death toll became a cautionary tale about the dangers of rushing to publish unverified news.

Lessons That Changed the World

The Titanic disaster led to major changes:

  • More lifeboats: Ships were required to carry enough lifeboats for everyone on board
  • 24-hour radio watch: Ships were required to keep their radios on at all times (a nearby ship, the Californian, had turned off its radio and never heard Titanic’s distress calls)
  • The International Ice Patrol: A new organization was created to track icebergs in the North Atlantic

The Titanic sank over 100 years ago, but its story still fascinates people today. Thanks to Chronicling America, you can read the same newspaper stories that Americans read in 1912 — and see how the truth slowly emerged from the confusion.
