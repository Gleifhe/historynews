STORM THE FORTRESS! How an Angry Mob With Muskets Tore Down a Prison — and Launched the French Revolution

Contemporary illustrations, maps, and eyewitness accounts reconstruct the hours-long siege that became the symbol of the French Revolution.

Historical Date: July 14, 1789 Primary Source: Wikipedia / Wikimedia Commons

A Country Ready to Explode

France in 1789 was a country on the edge. The government was bankrupt. The weather had destroyed crops. Bread prices had skyrocketed. Ordinary people were starving.

Meanwhile, King Louis XVI and Queen Marie Antoinette lived in spectacular luxury at the Palace of Versailles. The nobility and the clergy paid almost no taxes. All the burden fell on the common people — the farmers, shopkeepers, and workers who made up 98% of the population.

People were angry. And on July 14, 1789, that anger exploded.

What Was the Bastille?

The Bastille was a massive medieval fortress in the heart of Paris. It had been built in the 1300s as a military stronghold, with eight towers, walls up to 10 feet thick, and a moat.

By 1789, it was mainly used as a prison — but not an ordinary prison. The Bastille was where the King could lock up anyone he wanted, without trial, using documents called lettres de cachet. You could be thrown into the Bastille and forgotten.

For ordinary French people, the Bastille was the ultimate symbol of royal tyranny. It represented everything they hated about the monarchy: absolute power, injustice, and the idea that the King was above the law.

The Morning of July 14

In the days before July 14, tensions in Paris had been rising. The King had fired a popular minister. Troops were gathering around the city. Rumors spread that the army was going to attack the people.

On the morning of July 14, a crowd of Parisians — mostly working-class men and women — began gathering outside the Bastille. They had two goals:

  1. Get the gunpowder stored inside the fortress (they had already gotten muskets from a military warehouse but had no ammunition)
  2. Free any political prisoners being held inside

The crowd sent a delegation to the Bastille’s commander, Bernard-René de Launay, asking him to surrender the fortress and hand over the gunpowder. De Launay stalled for time.

The Siege

Around 1:30 p.m., the crowd lost patience. Some people managed to get into the outer courtyard by cutting the drawbridge chains. De Launay’s soldiers opened fire.

The fighting was chaotic and bloody:

  • The crowd had muskets but little ammunition and no training
  • The garrison had cannons and a strong defensive position, but only about 114 soldiers
  • Reinforcements arrived for the crowd — soldiers from the French Guards who had defected to the people’s side, bringing cannons

The battle lasted about four hours. By 5:00 p.m., the crowd was threatening to blow up the fortress with captured cannons. De Launay finally surrendered.

The Aftermath Was Brutal

When the crowd surged into the Bastille, they found it was almost empty. There were only seven prisoners inside — mostly people held for minor offenses or mental illness. There were no dramatic political prisoners to free.

But what happened next was violent:

  • De Launay was killed by the crowd. His head was cut off and stuck on a pike — a grim practice that would become common during the Revolution
  • Several of his soldiers were also killed
  • About 100 people in the crowd had been killed during the fighting

A Symbol Bigger Than the Facts

The Bastille itself was disappointing — a mostly empty fortress with a handful of prisoners. But as a symbol, it was everything.

The storming of the Bastille proved that ordinary people could stand up to royal power and win. It showed that the army couldn’t always be counted on to defend the King — some soldiers had switched sides.

News of the Bastille’s fall spread across France within days. In towns and villages everywhere, people rose up against local authorities. The feudal system — where nobles had absolute power over peasants — began to collapse.

What Came Next

The French Revolution that began on July 14, 1789, transformed France and the world:

  • The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (August 1789) established principles of human rights and equality
  • The monarchy was abolished in 1792
  • King Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette were executed by guillotine in 1793
  • The Reign of Terror (1793-1794) saw thousands executed as the Revolution turned violent and paranoid
  • Napoleon Bonaparte eventually seized power in 1799, ending the Revolution but spreading its ideals across Europe through conquest

July 14: France’s National Day

Today, July 14 is France’s national holiday — their equivalent of America’s July 4th. Every year, military parades march down the Champs-Élysées in Paris, fireworks light up the Eiffel Tower, and the French celebrate the day their revolution began.

The paintings, illustrations, and maps of the Bastille’s storming — many available through Wikimedia Commons — capture the raw energy of a moment when ordinary people decided they’d had enough. The Bastille was torn down brick by brick in the months that followed. Today, nothing remains of the fortress except a faint outline in the pavement of the Place de la Bastille.

But the idea it inspired — that power belongs to the people, not to kings — changed the world forever.
