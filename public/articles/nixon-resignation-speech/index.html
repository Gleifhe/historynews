'I SHALL RESIGN': Watching Nixon's Final Speech — The Night 110 Million Americans Saw a President Quit on Live TV

A close reading of Nixon's televised resignation speech — what he said, what he didn't say, and how the nation reacted.

Historical Date: August 8, 1974 Primary Source: C-SPAN Video Library

The Night a President Quit

On the evening of August 8, 1974, about 110 million Americans gathered around their television sets. They were about to witness something that had never happened before in American history: a president resigning from office.

Richard Nixon sat behind his desk in the Oval Office. The cameras focused on his face. At 9:01 p.m. Eastern Time, he began to speak:

“Good evening. This is the 37th time I have spoken to you from this office, where so many decisions have been made that shaped the history of this Nation. Each time I have done so to discuss with you some matter that I believe affected the national interest. In all the decisions I have made in my public life, I have always tried to do what was best for the Nation.”

Then came the words no American president had ever spoken:

“Therefore, I shall resign the Presidency effective at noon tomorrow.”

How Did It Come to This?

The story starts two years earlier, on June 17, 1972. Five men were caught breaking into the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate building in Washington, D.C. They were trying to plant listening devices and photograph documents.

At first, the break-in seemed like a small story. Nixon’s press secretary called it a “third-rate burglary attempt.” Nixon himself denied any involvement.

But two young reporters at the Washington Post — Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein — kept digging. With the help of a secret source known as “Deep Throat” (later revealed to be FBI Deputy Director Mark Felt), they uncovered a web of crimes that reached all the way to the President.

The investigation revealed that Nixon’s campaign and White House had:

  • Ordered the Watergate break-in
  • Paid the burglars hush money
  • Used the FBI and CIA to cover up the crime
  • Kept secret enemies lists
  • Used the IRS to harass political opponents
  • Secretly recorded conversations in the Oval Office

The Tapes That Ended a Presidency

The most damaging evidence was Nixon’s own voice. It turned out that Nixon had installed a recording system in the Oval Office that automatically recorded every conversation. When investigators learned about the tapes, they demanded them.

Nixon fought for months to keep the tapes secret. The case went all the way to the Supreme Court, which ruled unanimously that Nixon had to turn them over.

The tapes were devastating. They showed Nixon personally directing the cover-up, ordering aides to lie, and obstructing justice. One recording — the “smoking gun tape” from June 23, 1972 — showed Nixon ordering the CIA to block the FBI’s investigation, just six days after the break-in.

The Speech

When Nixon finally spoke to the nation on August 8, the speech was remarkable for what it did NOT contain:

  • He never admitted guilt. He said he was resigning because he had lost his “political base” in Congress — not because he had committed crimes.
  • He never apologized. He expressed regret about “any injuries that may have been done” — a passive construction that avoided taking personal responsibility.
  • He defended his record. Much of the speech was about his foreign policy achievements, especially opening relations with China.

The most emotional moment came near the end, when Nixon’s voice broke slightly:

“To leave office before my term is completed is abhorrent to every instinct in my body. But as President, I must put the interest of America first.”

The Reaction

Americans were divided:

  • Some felt justice had been served: Nixon had abused his power and tried to cover it up
  • Some felt sorry for him: Nixon looked exhausted and broken on television
  • Some felt the system had worked: The Constitution’s checks and balances had held

The next morning, Nixon said an emotional, rambling farewell to his staff. He was visibly crying. He walked across the White House lawn, climbed the steps to the Marine One helicopter, turned, and gave his famous two-armed “victory” wave. Then he was gone.

At noon on August 9, Vice President Gerald Ford was sworn in as the 38th President. His first words were: “Our long national nightmare is over.”

Why It Still Matters

The Watergate scandal and Nixon’s resignation established important precedents:

  • No one is above the law — not even the President
  • A free press is essential — Woodward and Bernstein’s reporting drove the investigation
  • The system of checks and balances works — Congress, the courts, and the press all played their roles

C-SPAN’s Video Library has the complete, unedited footage of Nixon’s resignation speech. Watching it today, you can still feel the weight of the moment — a president who reached the highest office in the land, and lost it because he couldn’t admit the truth.
