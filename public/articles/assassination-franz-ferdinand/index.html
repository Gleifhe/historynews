WRONG TURN! A Driver's Mistake Put an Assassin Face-to-Face With an Archduke — and Started a World War

How a wrong turn by a driver put Archduke Franz Ferdinand's car directly in front of his assassin in Sarajevo.

Historical Date: June 28, 1914 Primary Source: History.com

The Most Consequential Murder in History

On June 28, 1914, a 19-year-old student named Gavrilo Princip shot and killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary and his wife, Sophie, in the city of Sarajevo.

That single act of violence started a chain reaction that led to World War I — a war that killed over 17 million people, destroyed four empires, redrew the map of the world, and set the stage for World War II, the Holocaust, and the Cold War.

All because a driver made a wrong turn.

A Tangled Web of Alliances

To understand why one assassination could start a world war, you need to understand Europe in 1914.

The major European powers had formed two competing groups of alliances:

  • The Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy
  • The Triple Entente: France, Russia, and Britain

These alliances were like a row of dominoes. If one country went to war, its allies were obligated to join. And their enemies’ allies would join on the other side. A single conflict between two countries could drag in all of them.

The Assassins Were Amateurs

The assassination plot was organized by a Serbian nationalist group called the Black Hand. They were angry because Austria-Hungary controlled Bosnia, which had a large Serbian population. They wanted Bosnia to be part of Serbia.

The Black Hand recruited several young men, including Gavrilo Princip, and armed them with pistols and bombs. The plan was crude: the assassins would position themselves along the route of the Archduke’s motorcade through Sarajevo and attack when they got a chance.

On the morning of June 28, the Archduke’s motorcade drove through Sarajevo. One of the assassins threw a bomb at the Archduke’s car. The bomb bounced off the back of the car and exploded under the next vehicle, wounding several people.

The Archduke was shaken but unhurt. The other assassins along the route lost their nerve and didn’t act. The motorcade sped to City Hall.

The assassination attempt had failed.

The Wrong Turn

After the ceremony at City Hall, the Archduke decided to visit the officers who had been wounded by the bomb. His driver set off through the city.

But nobody told the driver about the change in route. He followed the original plan, turning onto Franz Josef Street. When the Archduke’s advisor realized the mistake, he shouted at the driver to stop and turn around.

The driver stopped the car — right in front of Moritz Schiller’s delicatessen. Standing on the sidewalk, just five feet away, was Gavrilo Princip.

Princip couldn’t believe his luck. He had given up after the failed assassination attempt and wandered to the area. Now the Archduke’s car was stopped directly in front of him.

Princip pulled out his pistol and fired twice. The first shot hit Sophie in the stomach. The second hit the Archduke in the neck. Both died within minutes.

The Dominoes Fall

What happened next was a cascade of ultimatums, mobilizations, and declarations of war:

  1. Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia for the assassination and issued an ultimatum with demands so harsh they were designed to be rejected
  2. Serbia rejected some demands. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia (July 28)
  3. Russia mobilized to defend Serbia, its ally
  4. Germany declared war on Russia (August 1) and then on France (August 3)
  5. Germany invaded Belgium to attack France, which brought Britain into the war (August 4)

In just five weeks, a single murder in a Balkan city had ignited a war that would engulf the entire continent.

The War Nobody Wanted (But Nobody Stopped)

One of the most tragic aspects of World War I is that most of the leaders involved didn’t actually want a major war. They expected short, limited conflicts. Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany told his troops they’d be “home before the leaves fall.”

Instead, the war lasted four years and three months. Entire generations of young men were slaughtered in the trenches. New weapons — machine guns, poison gas, tanks, and airplanes — made the killing more efficient than ever before.

Why It Still Matters

The assassination of Franz Ferdinand is a reminder that small events can have enormous consequences. A wrong turn by a driver, a teenager with a pistol, a tangled web of alliances — and the world was plunged into the most destructive war it had ever seen.

History.com’s coverage, combined with photographs from the Library of Congress and academic sources, tells the story of how a single moment of violence in a small European city changed the course of the 20th century — and the world we live in today.
