LIFTOFF! Two Bicycle Mechanics From Ohio Just Invented the Airplane in a 12-Second Flight

High-resolution images of the original Wright Flyer, the brothers' notebooks, and their telegram home reconstruct the birth of aviation.

Historical Date: December 17, 1903 Primary Source: Smithsonian Open Access

Two Brothers With a Dream

Wilbur and Orville Wright were not scientists. They were not engineers. They didn’t have college degrees. They ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio.

But they were obsessed with flying.

Since they were children, the Wright brothers had been fascinated by the idea of flight. They read everything they could find about birds, gliders, and the failed attempts of other inventors. They studied, they tinkered, and they built.

In 1899, they started seriously working on the problem. By 1903, they had solved it.

What Made Them Different

Lots of people were trying to build flying machines in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Most of them focused on building more powerful engines to force their machines into the air. The problem was that once these machines got airborne, the pilots couldn’t control them — and they crashed.

The Wright brothers took a completely different approach. They focused on control first. How do you steer a machine that’s floating in the air? How do you keep it from tipping over?

They studied how birds turned by twisting their wingtips. They built wind tunnels in their bicycle shop to test different wing shapes. They created a system called “wing warping” that let the pilot twist the wings to turn and balance the aircraft.

It was brilliant, simple, and original. And it worked.

Kitty Hawk, North Carolina

The Wright brothers chose Kitty Hawk, on the Outer Banks of North Carolina, for their flight tests. The area had strong, steady winds and soft sandy ground for landings (and crashes). They traveled there from Ohio every fall, camping in tents on the dunes.

In 1900, 1901, and 1902, they tested gliders — unpowered aircraft that rode the wind. Each year they learned more, fixed problems, and improved their designs.

By 1903, they were ready to add an engine.

The Machine

The 1903 Wright Flyer was an incredible piece of engineering made from spruce wood, muslin fabric, and bicycle chains. It weighed about 605 pounds without a pilot. It had a wingspan of 40 feet and 4 inches.

The brothers couldn’t find an engine light enough and powerful enough, so they built their own — a 12-horsepower gasoline engine that weighed only about 170 pounds. They also designed and carved their own propellers, which were essentially rotating wings.

The original Flyer is now at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum. Through Smithsonian Open Access, you can view high-resolution photographs of every detail of this remarkable machine.

December 17, 1903

On the morning of December 17, 1903, conditions were finally right. The wind was blowing at about 27 miles per hour. Five local men from a nearby lifesaving station came to watch and help.

Orville lay flat on the lower wing. The engine sputtered to life. The Flyer rolled down a 60-foot launching rail and lifted into the air.

The first flight lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet — shorter than the wingspan of a modern Boeing 747.

But it was real, powered, sustained, controlled flight. For the first time in history, a human being had flown a heavier-than-air machine.

They made four flights that day. The longest, piloted by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered 852 feet.

“Success Four Flights”

After the flights, Orville sent a telegram to their father back in Dayton:

“Success four flights Thursday morning all against twenty-one mile wind started from level with engine power alone average speed through air thirty-one miles longest 57 seconds inform press home Christmas.”

The telegram — now preserved at the Smithsonian — is one of the most understated announcements of one of the greatest achievements in human history.

The World Barely Noticed

Here’s the strangest part: almost nobody cared. The Dayton newspapers didn’t think the story was important enough for the front page. Most of the press either ignored the flights or got the details wrong.

It took years before the world fully understood what the Wright brothers had done. By 1908, when they did public demonstrations in the U.S. and France, people were finally amazed.

From 12 Seconds to the Moon

The speed of progress after the Wright brothers is staggering:

  • 1903: First flight — 12 seconds, 120 feet
  • 1927: Lindbergh flies across the Atlantic — 33.5 hours
  • 1947: Chuck Yeager breaks the sound barrier
  • 1969: Neil Armstrong walks on the moon — just 66 years after Kitty Hawk

Two bicycle mechanics from Ohio started it all. Their plane, their notebooks, and their photographs — all available through the Smithsonian and the Library of Congress — tell the story of how human flight began.
