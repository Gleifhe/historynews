TRAMPLED FOR FREEDOM: The Suffragette Who Threw Herself in Front of the King's Horse

How British papers — sympathetic and hostile — framed suffragette Emily Davison's death at the 1913 Epsom Derby.

Historical Date: June 4, 1913 Primary Source: British Newspaper Archive

The Race That Changed History

June 4, 1913. The Epsom Derby — the most famous horse race in England. Over 500,000 people lined the track. King George V’s horse, Anmer, was running. Newsreel cameras were rolling.

As the horses thundered around the final bend, a woman ducked under the railing and stepped onto the track. She walked directly into the path of the King’s horse.

The collision was caught on film. The horse hit her at full speed. She was thrown into the air and crashed to the ground. The jockey was also thrown but survived. Emily Wilding Davison, 40 years old, never regained consciousness. She died four days later.

Who Was Emily Davison?

Emily Davison was not an ordinary woman. She had studied at Oxford University and earned first-class honors in English — though Oxford did not grant degrees to women at the time. She later earned a degree from the University of London in 1908. She was a teacher, a writer, and a passionate believer in women’s right to vote.

In 1913, women in Britain could not vote. They couldn’t run for office. In the eyes of the law, they were second-class citizens. A growing movement of women — called suffragettes — were fighting to change that.

Davison had been fighting for years. She had been arrested nine times. She had gone on hunger strikes in prison and been force-fed 49 times — a painful and humiliating procedure where guards held her down and pushed a tube through her nose into her stomach.

Was It Suicide or Protest?

Nobody knows for sure what Davison intended when she stepped onto that track. Some people think she was trying to grab the horse’s bridle and attach a suffragette banner to it. She had a suffragette flag pinned inside her coat. She also had a return train ticket in her pocket — suggesting she planned to go home afterward.

Others believe she deliberately sacrificed herself to bring attention to the cause. Whether she meant to die or not, her death turned her into a martyr for women’s rights.

The Newspapers Were Divided

The British Newspaper Archive shows how dramatically different papers covered Davison’s death:

Sympathetic papers called her a hero and a martyr:

  • “A BRAVE WOMAN’S SACRIFICE FOR THE CAUSE”
  • “SHE DIED THAT WOMEN MIGHT BE FREE”

Hostile papers called her a lunatic and a troublemaker:

  • “SUFFRAGETTE MADNESS AT THE DERBY”
  • “WILD WOMAN’S FOOLISH ACT ENDANGERS JOCKEY AND HORSE”

Some papers worried more about the horse and jockey than about Davison. The King and Queen sent messages asking about the jockey’s health — but said nothing about the dying woman.

A Funeral That Became a Protest

Davison’s funeral on June 14, 1913, was one of the largest suffragette demonstrations ever. Thousands of women dressed in white marched through London. They carried banners that read “FIGHT ON AND GOD WILL GIVE THE VICTORY.”

The procession stretched for miles. Crowds lined the streets — some respectful, others jeering. Police were everywhere, worried about trouble. But the march was peaceful and dignified.

Did Her Death Make a Difference?

In the short term, Davison’s death actually hurt the suffragette movement. Some people who had been sympathetic turned against the suffragettes, saying their tactics had gone too far.

But in the long term, her sacrifice kept the issue of women’s voting rights in the public eye. When World War I started in 1914, women took over factory jobs, farming, and other work while men went to fight. Their contributions during the war made it impossible to deny them the vote any longer.

In 1918, British women over 30 won the right to vote. In 1928, all women over 21 could vote — the same age as men.

Emily Davison never saw it happen. But she helped make it possible.
