'I WAS FOLLOWING ORDERS' — The Trial Where the World Said That's No Excuse for Murder

An analysis of the Nuremberg Charter and key trial transcripts that established the precedent that 'following orders' is not a legal defense.

Historical Date: November 20, 1945 Primary Source: Avalon Project (Yale Law School)

The World Demands Justice

World War II ended in Europe on May 8, 1945. As Allied soldiers liberated concentration camps across Germany and Poland, the full horror of the Holocaust became clear. Six million Jewish people had been murdered. Millions more — Roma, disabled people, political prisoners, and others — had also been killed.

The question was: what should be done with the people responsible?

Some leaders, including British Prime Minister Winston Churchill, initially wanted to simply execute the top Nazi leaders. No trial, no lawyers, no delay. Just a firing squad.

But others argued that the rule of law mattered — even for the worst criminals in history. If the Allies just executed people without trials, they would be acting no better than the Nazis themselves.

In the end, the decision was made to put the Nazi leaders on trial. It would be the first international war crimes trial in history.

The Nuremberg Charter

The legal framework for the trials was created in a document called the Charter of the International Military Tribunal, preserved in the Avalon Project at Yale Law School.

The charter established three types of crimes:

  1. Crimes against peace: Planning and waging aggressive war
  2. War crimes: Violating the laws of war (torture, killing prisoners, etc.)
  3. Crimes against humanity: Murder, extermination, enslavement, and persecution of civilian populations

The third category — crimes against humanity — was entirely new in international law. It established the principle that there are some things so terrible that they are crimes against all of humanity, no matter what a country’s own laws say.

The Trial Begins

The trial opened on November 20, 1945, in the Palace of Justice in Nuremberg, Germany. Twenty-two of the most important surviving Nazi leaders sat in the defendants’ dock.

The defendants included:

  • Hermann Göring: Commander of the Luftwaffe (air force) and Hitler’s designated successor
  • Rudolf Hess: Hitler’s deputy
  • Albert Speer: Hitler’s architect and Minister of Armaments
  • Julius Streicher: Publisher of viciously antisemitic propaganda

The prosecution team was made up of lawyers from four countries: the United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union. American prosecutor Robert H. Jackson delivered the opening statement, which is considered one of the greatest legal speeches in history:

“The privilege of opening the first trial in history for crimes against the peace of the world imposes a grave responsibility. The wrongs which we seek to condemn and punish have been so calculated, so malignant, and so devastating, that civilization cannot tolerate their being ignored, because it cannot survive their being repeated.”

“I Was Just Following Orders”

Almost every defendant used the same defense: “I was just following orders.” They claimed they were soldiers and officials who had no choice but to obey Hitler. They weren’t responsible for what happened — Hitler was.

The tribunal rejected this argument. The judges ruled that following orders is not a defense when the orders are clearly criminal. Every person has a moral duty to refuse orders that involve mass murder and torture.

This principle — known as the “Nuremberg defense” — became a cornerstone of international law. It means that soldiers, police officers, and government officials everywhere can be held personally responsible for crimes against humanity, even if they were ordered to commit them by their superiors.

The Verdicts

After 218 days of proceedings, the verdicts were announced on October 1, 1946:

  • 12 defendants were sentenced to death by hanging (including Göring, who killed himself with a cyanide capsule the night before his execution)
  • 3 defendants were acquitted
  • 7 defendants received prison sentences ranging from 10 years to life

The Legacy

The Nuremberg Trials didn’t just punish Nazi criminals — they created the foundation for international justice:

  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) was directly influenced by the Nuremberg principles
  • The Geneva Conventions (1949) strengthened the laws of war
  • The International Criminal Court (established in 2002) is the permanent successor to the Nuremberg tribunal, with the power to try individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity

The Avalon Project at Yale Law School has digitized the complete trial transcripts, the charter, and the judgments. They remain essential reading for anyone interested in law, history, or human rights.

The Nuremberg Trials proved that even in the aftermath of the worst crimes in human history, justice — not revenge — could prevail.
