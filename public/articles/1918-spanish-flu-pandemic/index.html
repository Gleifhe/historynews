SOUND FAMILIAR? In 1918 America Fought Over Masks, Lockdowns, and 'Personal Freedom' — During a Plague

Public health journals from 1918-1920 reveal how cities that acted early survived the Spanish Flu better than those that didn't.

Historical Date: 1918-1920 Primary Source: JSTOR

The Deadliest Pandemic in Modern History

In the spring of 1918, a strange new illness started spreading. It began with a fever and a cough — like a regular flu. But this flu was different. It killed fast, and it killed people who were usually healthy: young adults in their 20s and 30s.

Over the next two years, the flu spread to every corner of the world. It infected about 500 million people — one-third of the entire world’s population. Between 50 and 100 million people died. That’s more people than died in all of World War I.

It was called the Spanish Flu, though it probably didn’t start in Spain. (Spain was neutral during WWI and didn’t censor its press, so Spanish newspapers were the first to report on the illness openly. Other countries were hiding their outbreaks to keep wartime morale high.)

Masks, Closures, and Angry Citizens

When the flu hit American cities in the fall of 1918, local governments had to make hard choices. They used many of the same tools that would be used again during COVID-19 over a hundred years later:

  • Mask mandates: Cities like San Francisco required everyone to wear cloth masks in public
  • Business closures: Theaters, saloons, churches, and schools were shut down
  • Bans on public gatherings: Parades, funerals, and sporting events were canceled
  • Quarantine orders: Sick people were told to stay home

And just like in 2020, many people pushed back. In San Francisco, a group called the Anti-Mask League held meetings protesting the mask mandate. They argued it violated their personal freedom and that masks didn’t work.

The Tale of Two Cities

Academic articles in JSTOR tell a fascinating story about two cities that took very different approaches: St. Louis and Philadelphia.

St. Louis acted fast. When the first cases appeared in October 1918, the city immediately closed schools, theaters, and churches. Public gatherings were banned. The city’s health commissioner, Dr. Max Starkloff, was aggressive and decisive.

Philadelphia waited. Despite warnings, the city went ahead with a massive Liberty Loan parade on September 28, 1918. About 200,000 people crowded together to watch. Within days, the city’s hospitals were overwhelmed. Within weeks, over 12,000 Philadelphians were dead.

The results were dramatic. At its peak, St. Louis had only one-eighth the mortality rate of Philadelphia. The lesson was clear: cities that acted early and aggressively saved lives. Cities that delayed paid a terrible price.

“Open the Bars!” — Pressure to Reopen

Even in cities that locked down, there was constant pressure to reopen. Business owners complained about lost income. Workers couldn’t afford to stay home. After a few weeks, many cities gave in and lifted restrictions — only to see cases spike again.

San Francisco is a perfect example. The city imposed a mask mandate in October 1918, and cases dropped. Thinking the worst was over, the city lifted the mandate in November. Cases immediately surged again, and the city had to reimpose the mandate in January 1919.

The Forgotten Pandemic

Despite killing tens of millions of people, the 1918 flu was largely forgotten for decades. It happened during World War I, and the war overshadowed everything. There were no memorials, no national days of remembrance. Most history books barely mentioned it.

That changed during COVID-19, when historians and public health experts dusted off the 1918 records and found eerie parallels: the same debates about masks, the same arguments about personal freedom vs. public safety, the same mistakes of reopening too early.

What the Journals Tell Us

The public health journals preserved in JSTOR provide a scientific record of the 1918 pandemic. They include:

  • Detailed statistics on infection and death rates in different cities
  • Debates among doctors about how the flu spread
  • Reports on experimental treatments (most of which didn’t work)
  • Analysis of which public health measures were most effective

Reading these papers today, it’s striking how much doctors in 1918 got right — and how often their advice was ignored by politicians and the public.

A Warning From the Past

The 1918 flu pandemic is a reminder that new diseases can appear without warning and spread around the world in weeks. The choices that leaders and ordinary people make during a pandemic — whether to act fast or delay, whether to follow science or ignore it — can mean the difference between thousands of lives saved and thousands of lives lost.

The academic record, preserved in JSTOR and other archives, ensures that the lessons of 1918 are available for future generations. The question is whether we’ll learn from them.
