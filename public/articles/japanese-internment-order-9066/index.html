LOCKED AWAY: How One Signature Imprisoned 120,000 Americans Who Did Nothing Wrong

FDR's Executive Order 9066, the internal memos debating its legality, and the decades-long fight for reparations.

Historical Date: February 19, 1942 Primary Source: National Archives (NARA)

A Stroke of a Pen

On February 19, 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt sat at his desk and signed a piece of paper. It was called Executive Order 9066. It was only one page long. But it would ruin the lives of 120,000 people.

The order gave the military the power to remove anyone it considered a threat from certain areas along the West Coast. It didn’t mention Japanese Americans by name. But everyone knew who it was aimed at.

Fear After Pearl Harbor

Just two months earlier, on December 7, 1941, Japan had attacked Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. The surprise bombing killed over 2,400 Americans and pulled the United States into World War II.

After the attack, many Americans were scared. Some people worried that Japanese Americans living on the West Coast might be secretly helping Japan. There was no evidence that this was true — but fear doesn’t need evidence.

Politicians, newspapers, and military leaders pushed for action. General John DeWitt, who was in charge of West Coast defense, said, “A Jap’s a Jap” — meaning he didn’t think it mattered whether Japanese Americans were loyal citizens or not.

Forced to Leave Everything Behind

Starting in the spring of 1942, Japanese American families received orders to report to assembly centers. They were given just days to pack. They could only bring what they could carry.

Families had to sell their homes, businesses, cars, and belongings for pennies on the dollar — or simply abandon them. Farmers left crops in the fields. Store owners locked their doors for the last time. Children were pulled out of school.

One famous photograph from the Library of Congress shows a store in Oakland with a large sign in the window: “I AM AN AMERICAN.” The store’s owner, a University of California graduate, had put up the sign after Pearl Harbor. It didn’t save him. He was sent to a camp anyway.

Life in the Camps

About 120,000 people — most of them American citizens — were sent to 10 internment camps scattered across remote areas of the western United States. The camps were in deserts, swamps, and other harsh locations.

Families lived in cramped barracks made of tar paper and wood. They shared bathrooms and mess halls. Armed guards watched them from towers surrounded by barbed wire.

Despite everything, the people in the camps tried to create normal lives. They set up schools, started newspapers, grew gardens, and organized sports leagues. Some even formed jazz bands.

Fighting for a Country That Locked Them Up

Here’s one of the most incredible parts of this story: even while their families were behind barbed wire, thousands of young Japanese American men volunteered to fight for the United States.

The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, made up almost entirely of Japanese Americans, became the most decorated unit in U.S. military history for its size and length of service. They fought in Europe and earned over 18,000 individual awards, including 21 Medals of Honor.

A Long Wait for Justice

The camps closed in 1945 when the war ended. But for decades, the U.S. government never apologized or admitted it had been wrong.

That finally changed in 1988, when President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act. The government officially apologized and paid $20,000 to each surviving person who had been imprisoned. Reagan said the internment was based on “race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership.”

The Document That Started It All

The original Executive Order 9066 is held at the National Archives in Washington, D.C. It’s a simple typed document with Roosevelt’s signature at the bottom. Looking at it today, it’s hard to believe that such an ordinary-looking piece of paper caused so much suffering.

The lesson of Executive Order 9066 is clear: fear can make a country do terrible things to its own people. And it reminds us that the rights promised in the Constitution must be protected — especially when people are scared.
