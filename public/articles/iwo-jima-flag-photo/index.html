CAPTURED IN A CLICK: The Untold Story Behind History's Most Famous War Photograph — and the Tragic Fates of the Men in It

The full story behind Joe Rosenthal's iconic Iwo Jima photograph — including the lesser-known first flag raising and the men's fates.

Historical Date: February 23, 1945 Primary Source: AP Images

The Most Famous Photo Ever Taken

You’ve seen it a thousand times. Six Marines straining to push an American flag up on a makeshift pole atop a rocky hill. The flag catches the wind. The figures lean together in a pyramid of effort. It is, by some measures, the most reproduced photograph in the history of photography.

But the story behind that picture is far more complicated — and far more heartbreaking — than most people know.

Why Iwo Jima Mattered

Iwo Jima is a tiny volcanic island in the Pacific Ocean, about 760 miles south of Tokyo. It’s only 8 square miles — roughly the size of a small town. But in February 1945, it was one of the most important pieces of real estate in the world.

The Japanese had built airfields on Iwo Jima that allowed them to intercept American bombers heading to Japan. If the Americans could capture the island, they could use it as an emergency landing strip for damaged B-29 bombers and as a base for fighter escorts.

The problem was that the Japanese had spent years turning Iwo Jima into a fortress. Over 21,000 Japanese soldiers were dug into a network of tunnels, bunkers, and caves. Many of the defensive positions were underground, invisible from the surface.

The Bloodiest Battle in Marine Corps History

The Marines landed on Iwo Jima on February 19, 1945. What was expected to last five days turned into 35 days of savage fighting.

The Japanese didn’t defend the beaches. Instead, they waited until the Marines were packed tightly on the sand and then opened fire from hidden positions. It was a massacre. In the first 48 hours, over 2,400 Americans were casualties.

Over the course of the battle:

  • 6,800 Marines were killed
  • 19,000 more were wounded
  • Of the 21,000 Japanese defenders, only 216 survived — the rest fought to the death

The First Flag — The One Nobody Remembers

On the morning of February 23, 1945 — four days into the battle — a patrol of Marines fought their way to the top of Mount Suribachi, the 546-foot volcanic peak at the island’s southern tip.

They carried a small American flag and raised it on a pipe at the summit. Sergeant Louis Lowery, a Marine photographer, took pictures. Marines on the beaches below saw the flag and cheered. Ships offshore blew their horns.

But the flag was small — too small to be seen clearly from a distance. And the battalion commander wanted it as a souvenir. So a second, larger flag was sent up the mountain.

The Second Flag — The Famous One

Later that afternoon, five Marines and one Navy corpsman raised the second, larger flag. Associated Press photographer Joe Rosenthal was on the summit. He almost missed the shot. He was adjusting his camera when someone shouted that they were putting up the new flag. Rosenthal quickly swung his camera around and clicked the shutter.

He didn’t even know if he’d gotten a good picture. He continued shooting other photos and then sent his film back to the AP for processing. When editors saw the flag-raising image, they immediately knew it was special. Within 48 hours, the photograph was on the front page of nearly every newspaper in America.

The Men in the Photo

For decades, the identities of the six men in the photograph were debated. The commonly accepted identities are:

  • Ira Hayes — A Pima Native American from Arizona
  • Harlon Block — A high school football star from Texas (killed on Iwo Jima on March 1)
  • Rene Gagnon — From New Hampshire
  • Franklin Sousley — From Kentucky (killed on Iwo Jima on March 21)
  • Michael Strank — An immigrant from Czechoslovakia (killed on Iwo Jima on March 1)
  • John Bradley — A Navy corpsman from Wisconsin

Of the six men, three were killed on Iwo Jima before the battle ended. They never knew the photograph had made them famous.

The Survivors’ Burden

The three survivors — Hayes, Gagnon, and Bradley — were brought home and used in a nationwide war bond tour. They were paraded around the country as heroes, attending rallies and dinners to help raise money for the war effort.

It was agonizing for them. Ira Hayes, especially, struggled deeply. He didn’t consider himself a hero — he had just helped put up a replacement flag. His real heroes were the friends who died beside him. Hayes descended into alcoholism and died at age 32 in 1955.

John Bradley rarely spoke about the photograph for the rest of his life. He told his son that the real heroes of Iwo Jima “didn’t come back.”

A Photograph That Became a Symbol

Joe Rosenthal’s photograph won the Pulitzer Prize and became the model for the Marine Corps War Memorial in Arlington, Virginia. It remains one of the most powerful images ever created.

The Library of Congress holds prints of the photograph and related images. The original negative is held by the Associated Press.

The photo captures something universal: the struggle, the teamwork, the cost of freedom. But behind the image are six real men — three of whom gave their lives on a tiny island in the Pacific, and three who carried the weight of that photograph for the rest of their days.
