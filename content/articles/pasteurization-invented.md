In the 1860s, no one knew why people got sick. The most popular theory was that diseases were caused by "bad air" — invisible, foul-smelling vapors that rose from swamps and garbage. Doctors did not wash their hands before surgery. Milk was sold without refrigeration and often carried deadly bacteria. Children died by the thousands from diseases that we now know are entirely preventable.

Louis Pasteur, a French chemist and biologist, was about to change all of that. In 1864, French wine producers came to Pasteur with a problem. Their wine kept spoiling, costing them enormous amounts of money. Pasteur studied the wine under a microscope and discovered that tiny living organisms — microbes — were contaminating it and causing it to go bad. He then demonstrated that heating the wine to a specific temperature for a set period of time killed these microbes without ruining the taste. This simple process became known as pasteurization.

But Pasteur's true genius was in realizing that what was true for wine was true for disease. If tiny organisms could spoil wine, then tiny organisms could also make people sick. This idea — known as the germ theory of disease — was revolutionary. It overturned centuries of medical thinking and laid the foundation for modern medicine as we know it.

Pasteur spent the rest of his career proving and expanding this theory. He showed that pasteurization could make milk safe to drink, which was especially important for children. Before pasteurization, contaminated milk was one of the leading causes of infant death. Thousands of babies and young children died every year from tuberculosis, typhoid fever, and other diseases transmitted through raw milk. Pasteurization virtually eliminated these deaths.

Pasteur did not stop there. In 1881, he developed a vaccine for anthrax, a deadly disease that killed livestock and sometimes humans. Then, in 1885, he achieved perhaps his most dramatic triumph: the development of a vaccine for rabies. His first human patient was Joseph Meister, a nine-year-old boy who had been severely bitten by a rabid dog. Without treatment, the boy would have died an agonizing death. Pasteur's vaccine saved his life, and the news made Pasteur a hero around the world.

Pasteur's work on germ theory directly inspired the development of vaccines that would later lead to the [eradication of smallpox](/articles/smallpox-eradication/) — one of the greatest achievements in human history. His understanding of how diseases spread also proved crucial during the [1918 Spanish Flu pandemic](/articles/1918-spanish-flu-pandemic/), when public health measures based on germ theory helped communities respond to the crisis.

The Institut Pasteur, founded in Paris in 1887, continues his work today as one of the world's leading research institutions for infectious diseases. Pasteur himself died in 1895, but his legacy is measured in the hundreds of millions of lives saved by his discoveries.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Pasteur's story reminds us that asking the right question is often more important than having the right answer. While everyone else accepted that disease came from bad air, Pasteur asked a different question: What if disease comes from something we cannot see? That simple shift in thinking changed the world.

In our daily lives, we often accept the way things are without questioning whether there might be a better explanation or a better way. Pasteur teaches us to be curious, to challenge assumptions, and to look for evidence rather than accepting popular beliefs at face value.

His journey also shows the power of applying knowledge across different fields. Pasteur was a chemist who solved problems in biology and medicine. He was not limited by the boundaries of his original training. This reminds us that skills and knowledge from one area of life can often be applied to solve problems in completely different areas.

## How This Connects to 2026

Pasteurization remains one of the most important food safety measures in the world. In 2026, virtually all commercially sold milk, juice, and many other beverages are pasteurized before reaching consumers. The process has been refined and improved, but the basic principle is exactly what Pasteur discovered over 160 years ago.

Food safety has become even more important as global supply chains grow longer and more complex. New pasteurization techniques, including high-pressure processing and ultraviolet treatment, are being developed to make food safer while preserving more nutrients and flavor. These innovations build directly on Pasteur's original insight.

Germ theory — Pasteur's greatest contribution — remains the foundation of how we fight infectious disease. In 2026, the world continues to apply his principles in the fight against emerging infections, antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and potential pandemic threats. Every time you wash your hands, take an antibiotic, receive a vaccination, or drink a glass of pasteurized milk, you are benefiting from the work of Louis Pasteur. His simple but profound idea — that invisible germs cause disease, and that we can stop them — continues to save lives every single day.
