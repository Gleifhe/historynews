On March 1, 1781, Maryland became the last of the thirteen states to ratify the Articles of Confederation, making them the official governing framework of the United States. It had taken over three years since Congress proposed them, and the delay foreshadowed the problems ahead. The Articles created a government that was, by design, almost powerless. Within six years, it would be clear that the experiment had failed.

## Fear of Power

The Articles of Confederation were shaped by one overriding fear: tyranny. The colonists had just fought a war to free themselves from a powerful central authority — the British Crown and Parliament. They had no intention of creating a new one. The result was a national government that was more like a diplomatic alliance than a sovereign state.

Under the Articles, there was no president. There was no federal court system. There was a single legislative body — a unicameral Congress — in which each state, regardless of population, had one vote. Congress could declare war, negotiate treaties, and manage relations with Native American nations. But it could not levy taxes, regulate commerce between states, or enforce its own laws.

Crucially, any amendment to the Articles required the unanimous consent of all thirteen states. This made reform virtually impossible.

## The Government That Could Not Govern

The weaknesses became apparent almost immediately. Congress could request money from the states to fund the government and pay war debts, but states routinely refused or underpaid. By the mid-1780s, the national government was essentially broke.

Trade disputes between states grew heated. States imposed tariffs on each other's goods. New York taxed goods coming from New Jersey and Connecticut. Some states printed their own paper money, creating economic chaos. Creditors lost faith in American financial instruments.

Foreign policy was hobbled. The British refused to evacuate military posts in the Northwest Territory, partly because they did not take the American government seriously. Spain closed the Mississippi River to American navigation. Without the power to raise an army or compel state cooperation, Congress could do little about either problem.

## Shays' Rebellion

The crisis came to a head in 1786 when Daniel Shays, a Revolutionary War veteran, led an armed rebellion of indebted farmers in western Massachusetts. The rebels, many of whom had fought for independence and now faced foreclosure, attempted to seize a federal armory in Springfield. Massachusetts eventually put down the rebellion, but the national government's inability to help — it could not raise troops or funds — alarmed leaders across the country.

George Washington, who had retired to Mount Vernon, was horrified. "If government cannot check these disorders," he wrote, "what security has a man for life, liberty, or property?"

The crisis prompted the call for the [Constitutional Convention](/articles/constitutional-convention/) in Philadelphia in 1787, where the Articles would be replaced entirely.

## A Necessary Failure

The Articles of Confederation are often dismissed as a mistake, but they served an important purpose. They held the states together during the Revolution and its immediate aftermath. They established precedents for western expansion, including the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, which created a framework for admitting new states. And their failures provided the essential lesson that made the Constitution possible: a government without adequate power is no government at all.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Articles of Confederation remind us that the fear of giving too much authority can be just as dangerous as giving too much. The Founders were so afraid of centralized power that they created a government that could not function. The same dynamic plays out in organizations, teams, and relationships. If you are so afraid of control that you refuse to establish clear authority, accountability, and structure, the result is not freedom — it is dysfunction. Balance matters.
