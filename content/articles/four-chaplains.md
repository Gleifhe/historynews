## A Torpedo in the Dark

It was just past midnight on February 3, 1943. The USAT Dorchester, a converted luxury liner now packed with 902 American soldiers and civilian workers, was plowing through the freezing waters of the North Atlantic, roughly 150 miles off the coast of Greenland. The men aboard were headed to an American base in Greenland, part of the vast logistical network that kept Allied forces supplied during World War II. Most of them were asleep below decks.

The water temperature was 34 degrees Fahrenheit. The air was colder. Three Coast Guard cutters — the Escanaba, the Comanche, and the Tampa — escorted the Dorchester through the darkness, but the convoy was in one of the most dangerous stretches of ocean in the world. German U-boats prowled the North Atlantic in deadly wolf packs, and Allied shipping losses in early 1943 were staggering.

At 12:55 a.m., a torpedo fired by the German submarine U-223 struck the Dorchester's starboard side, below the waterline. The explosion ripped through the engine room and killed many of the men sleeping in the holds below. The ship's power failed. The lights went out. In the pitch-black chaos below decks, hundreds of men scrambled for the ladders.

The Dorchester began listing almost immediately. It would sink in approximately 20 minutes.

## Order in the Chaos

Among the 902 people aboard were four U.S. Army chaplains: Lieutenant George L. Fox, a Methodist minister; Lieutenant Alexander D. Goode, a Jewish rabbi; Lieutenant Clark V. Poling, a Dutch Reformed minister; and Lieutenant John P. Washington, a Roman Catholic priest. They represented four different faiths, but in the weeks leading up to the sinking, they had formed a close bond, often seen together on deck, talking and sharing stories.

When the torpedo hit, the four chaplains made their way to the deck and immediately began organizing the panicked soldiers. In the darkness and confusion, men were scrambling without life jackets, unable to find their way to the lifeboats. Some had ignored the order to sleep with their life jackets on because the bulky vests were uncomfortable.

Survivors later described the chaplains moving through the crowd with calm authority, guiding soldiers toward the lifeboats, distributing life jackets from a storage locker, and helping men over the side of the listing ship. When the life jackets in the locker ran out, each of the four chaplains removed his own life jacket and gave it to a soldier who had none.

Sergeant Michael Warish, one of the survivors, later recalled the scene: the four chaplains standing together on the deck as the ship went down, arms linked, praying aloud. Some survivors reported hearing them reciting the Lord's Prayer, the Shema, and the Ave Maria — each praying in his own tradition, together.

## The Toll

The Dorchester sank at approximately 1:15 a.m., roughly 20 minutes after being struck. Of the 902 men aboard, 672 died — from the explosion, from drowning, or from hypothermia in the freezing North Atlantic waters. Many who made it into the water with life jackets still died from exposure before rescue ships could reach them. The water was so cold that a person could survive no more than a few minutes without additional protection.

The Coast Guard cutters Escanaba and Comanche rescued 230 survivors. The Comanche's crew, which included many Black Coast Guard sailors, pulled men from the water for hours. Petty Officer Charles Walter David Jr., an African American crew member of the Comanche, repeatedly entered the freezing water to rescue soldiers and later died from his injuries and exposure. He was posthumously awarded the Navy and Marine Corps Medal.

## Who Were the Four Chaplains?

Each of the four men had a life that brought him to that frozen deck.

**George Lansing Fox** was born in Lewistown, Pennsylvania, in 1900. He had enlisted in the Army at 17 to serve in World War I, where he was seriously wounded and awarded the Silver Star and Purple Heart. After the war, he became a Methodist minister and enlisted again after Pearl Harbor at the age of 42 — well above the typical age for military service.

**Alexander David Goode** was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1911, the son of a rabbi. He earned a doctorate from Johns Hopkins University and became a rabbi in York, Pennsylvania. He had tried to become a Navy chaplain but was rejected; the Army accepted him.

**Clark Vandersall Poling** was born in Columbus, Ohio, in 1910. His father, Daniel Poling, was a prominent minister and editor. Clark followed his father into ministry, serving a Dutch Reformed church in Schenectady, New York, before volunteering as a chaplain.

**John Patrick Washington** was born in Newark, New Jersey, in 1908. He grew up in an Irish Catholic family and was ordained as a priest in 1935. He was serving at a parish in New Jersey when he enlisted after Pearl Harbor.

None of them had to be on that ship. All of them had volunteered.

## The Legacy

The story of the Four Chaplains became one of the most celebrated acts of selflessness in American military history. In 1944, the U.S. Congress attempted to award each chaplain the Medal of Honor posthumously, but the award was not approved because their sacrifice, while extraordinary, did not occur in direct combat with the enemy as required by the medal's criteria at the time. Instead, in 1948, Congress created a special posthumous decoration — the Four Chaplains' Medal — which remains the only one of its kind ever awarded.

The story resonated deeply in wartime America, particularly because it crossed religious lines. A Protestant, a Catholic, and a Jew — in an era of considerable interfaith tension — had given their lives for one another and for men they barely knew. The Four Chaplains became a symbol of interfaith unity at a time when the country desperately needed one.

Their sacrifice echoes other stories of extraordinary courage under impossible conditions, like [Desmond Doss](/articles/desmond-doss-okinawa/), who refused to carry a weapon yet saved 75 lives at Okinawa, and the soldiers whose [firsthand accounts of D-Day](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/) reveal similar moments of selflessness amid chaos.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The Four Chaplains force us to confront a simple question: what would you give for someone else? Most of us will never face anything remotely as extreme as their choice. But every day presents smaller versions of the same decision — giving up your time, your comfort, or your advantage so that someone else can have what they need.

Their story also challenges us to look past the differences that divide people. Fox, Goode, Poling, and Washington came from different faiths, different backgrounds, and different traditions. In the moment that mattered most, none of that mattered. They saw men who needed help, and they helped.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, interfaith cooperation remains both a challenge and a necessity. Communities around the world continue to grapple with religious tension, and acts of violence motivated by religious hatred still make headlines. The Four Chaplains' story doesn't offer an easy solution, but it offers a powerful image: four men of four faiths, arms linked, praying together as the water rose around them. It is a reminder that our shared humanity is always greater than our differences — if we choose to see it that way.
