When Desmond Doss enlisted in the United States Army in 1942, he told his commanding officers something they had never heard before: he wanted to serve his country, but he would not carry a weapon. He would not even touch a gun. As a devout Seventh-day Adventist, Doss believed that killing was wrong, but he also believed that he had a duty to serve. He wanted to be a combat medic — to save lives instead of taking them.

The reaction from his fellow soldiers was harsh. They called him a coward. They bullied him, threw shoes at him while he slept, and tried to get him kicked out of the Army. His commanding officers tried to have him discharged on the grounds that he was mentally unfit for service. But Doss would not budge. He insisted that being a conscientious objector did not make him a coward. He simply had a different way of being brave.

Eventually, the Army allowed Doss to serve as a medic without carrying a weapon. He was assigned to the 77th Infantry Division and sent to the Pacific Theater. He served at Guam and Leyte in the Philippines, earning respect from the same men who had once mocked him. But his greatest moment was yet to come.

In May 1945, Doss's unit was ordered to capture a jagged cliff on the island of Okinawa that the soldiers called Hacksaw Ridge. The cliff rose 400 feet straight up, and the Japanese forces defending it were dug in with machine guns, mortars, and grenades. American soldiers had to climb cargo nets to reach the top, where they were immediately met with devastating fire.

On May 5, Doss's company climbed the ridge and was hit hard. Japanese counterattacks drove the Americans back, and the order came to retreat. Most of the soldiers scrambled back down the cliff. But Doss stayed behind. Alone on the ridge, surrounded by enemy soldiers, he began searching for wounded Americans.

One by one, Doss found injured soldiers, dragged them to the edge of the cliff, and lowered them down on a rope. He fashioned a special knot that allowed him to lower each man safely to the ground below, where other soldiers could carry them to safety. All the while, bullets flew around him and explosions shook the ground.

Throughout the night and into the next day, Doss kept going back. He crawled through enemy fire, found wounded men, and dragged them to safety. He later said that he prayed the same prayer over and over: "Lord, please let me get one more." Each time he saved a man, he went back for another.

By the time the battle was over, Desmond Doss had saved an estimated 75 soldiers. The official Army count was 75, though Doss himself modestly estimated 50. Either way, his actions were nothing short of miraculous. He had accomplished all of this without firing a single shot — without even carrying a weapon.

On October 12, 1945, President Harry Truman presented Desmond Doss with the Medal of Honor, the highest military decoration in the United States. It was the first time a conscientious objector had received the award. Truman reportedly told Doss, "I'm proud of you. You really deserve this. I consider this a greater honor than being President."

Doss returned home to a quiet life in Georgia. He suffered from tuberculosis and injuries sustained during the war, losing a lung and most of his hearing. But he never complained and never sought the spotlight. He lived simply and continued to serve his community until his death in 2006 at age 87.

The soldiers who had once bullied Doss came to admire him deeply. Many of the men he saved [at battles across the Pacific, including near Iwo Jima](/articles/iwo-jima-flag-photo/), credited him with their lives. His story shows the same bravery demonstrated by the [Allied soldiers on D-Day](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/).

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Desmond Doss's story is a powerful reminder that true courage means staying true to your beliefs, even when everyone around you disagrees. He endured ridicule, bullying, and pressure to conform, but he never wavered. His strength came not from physical power, but from an unshakable conviction about what was right.

This teaches us that standing up for what we believe in is never easy, but it is always worthwhile. Whether we face peer pressure, criticism, or outright hostility, Doss's example shows us that we can hold firm without being aggressive or cruel. He proved that you can be both gentle and incredibly brave at the same time.

Doss also reminds us that there are many ways to contribute. He could not fight, but he found another way to serve — and his contribution turned out to be just as important as anyone else's. When we feel like we do not fit the mold, Doss's story encourages us to find our own unique way to make a difference.

Finally, his story teaches humility. Despite saving 75 lives and earning the Medal of Honor, Doss never bragged or sought attention. He let his actions speak louder than any words ever could.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Desmond Doss's story resonates in a world that often seems to value aggression over compassion. His example reminds us that real strength does not require force. Some of the bravest people in our communities today are those who serve others without seeking credit — nurses, teachers, volunteers, and first responders.

Doss's willingness to stand by his principles, even under extreme pressure, is especially relevant in an era when social media and public opinion can make it tempting to follow the crowd. His story encourages us to think for ourselves and act according to our values, not according to what is popular.

His legacy also speaks to the importance of respecting different perspectives. The soldiers who once mocked Doss eventually became his greatest admirers. This reminds us that the people we disagree with today might surprise us tomorrow — if we give them the chance.
