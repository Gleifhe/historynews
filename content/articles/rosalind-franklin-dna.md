In 1953, two scientists at Cambridge University announced that they had discovered the structure of DNA — the molecule that carries the genetic instructions for all living things. James Watson and Francis Crick became famous around the world, and they eventually won the Nobel Prize. But the crucial evidence that made their discovery possible came from someone whose name most people have never heard: Rosalind Franklin, a brilliant scientist whose work was used without her knowledge and whose contributions were ignored for decades.

## The Race for DNA

By the early 1950s, scientists knew that DNA — deoxyribonucleic acid — was the molecule responsible for heredity. It carried the instructions that determined everything from eye color to blood type. But nobody knew what DNA actually looked like at the molecular level. Understanding its structure would be the key to understanding how it worked.

Several teams of scientists were racing to solve the puzzle. At Cambridge, Watson and Crick were building physical models of DNA, trying different arrangements of atoms until they found one that fit the available evidence. At King's College London, Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins were using a technique called X-ray crystallography to photograph DNA fibers.

## Rosalind Franklin

Franklin was born in London in 1920 into a prominent Jewish family. She was exceptionally intelligent and determined. She studied chemistry at Cambridge and then spent three years in Paris learning X-ray crystallography — a technique that uses X-rays to create images of molecular structures. She became one of the best crystallographers in the world.

In 1951, Franklin joined King's College London to study the structure of DNA. She was meticulous, patient, and skilled. Using painstaking techniques, she produced the sharpest X-ray images of DNA that anyone had ever made.

## Photograph 51

Franklin's most famous image is known as "Photograph 51." Taken in May 1952, it showed a clear X-shaped pattern that was strong evidence that DNA had a helical — or spiral — structure. The image also contained information about the dimensions and symmetry of the molecule.

Franklin was carefully analyzing her data and working toward solving the structure herself. She was methodical and cautious, preferring to be certain before making grand claims. She was getting close to the answer.

## The Betrayal

What happened next remains one of the most controversial episodes in the history of science. In January 1953, Maurice Wilkins — Franklin's colleague at King's College, with whom she had a difficult working relationship — showed Photograph 51 to James Watson without Franklin's permission or knowledge.

Watson later wrote that the moment he saw the photograph, "my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race." The image gave Watson and Crick the crucial evidence they needed. They could now see that DNA was a double helix — two intertwined spirals. Combined with other data, including a research report by Franklin that was shared through informal channels, Watson and Crick were able to build their famous model of DNA.

On April 25, 1953, Watson and Crick published their landmark paper in the journal Nature, describing the double helix structure of DNA. Franklin published her own paper in the same issue, presenting her X-ray evidence, but it was positioned as supporting work rather than as the breakthrough it actually was.

## Recognition Denied

Watson and Crick received widespread acclaim for their discovery. Along with Maurice Wilkins, they were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1962. Rosalind Franklin was not included.

There is a painful reason for this: Franklin had died of ovarian cancer in 1958, at the age of just 37. The Nobel Prize is not awarded posthumously, so she could not have received it. But many historians believe she would have been overlooked even if she had lived. In his 1968 memoir "The Double Helix," Watson described Franklin in dismissive and sexist terms, referring to her as "Rosy" — a nickname she disliked — and portraying her as difficult and uncooperative.

## Setting the Record Straight

In the decades since Franklin's death, historians and scientists have worked to set the record straight. It is now widely acknowledged that her experimental work was essential to the discovery of DNA's structure. Photograph 51 is recognized as one of the most important scientific images ever taken.

Franklin's story has become a powerful example of how women scientists have been overlooked and undervalued throughout history. In her short career, she made major contributions not only to the understanding of DNA but also to the study of viruses and coal. She published 37 scientific papers before her death.

Today, Rosalind Franklin is honored with buildings, scholarships, and awards named after her. In 2022, the European Space Agency named its Mars rover "Rosalind Franklin." Her story reminds us that scientific discoveries are rarely the work of one or two individuals — they are built on the contributions of many, and it matters that we remember all of them.
