Before 1921, a diagnosis of Type 1 diabetes was a death sentence. The disease, which prevents the body from producing insulin — the hormone needed to turn sugar into energy — left patients wasting away. Children with diabetes were placed on near-starvation diets to slow the disease, but they rarely survived more than a year or two. Hospital wards full of skeletal, dying children were a heartbreaking reality of early 20th century medicine.

Frederick Banting was a young Canadian surgeon who became obsessed with finding a cure. In the spring of 1921, he convinced Professor J.J.R. Macleod at the University of Toronto to give him a laboratory and an assistant — a medical student named Charles Best. Working through the hot summer months with limited resources, Banting and Best conducted experiment after experiment on dogs with diabetes, trying to isolate the substance produced by the pancreas that regulated blood sugar.

Their breakthrough came when they successfully extracted a substance they called "isletin" — later renamed insulin — from the pancreas of healthy dogs and injected it into diabetic dogs. The results were dramatic. Dogs that had been near death suddenly regained their energy and appetite. Their blood sugar levels dropped to normal. The substance worked.

On January 11, 1922, the team made history by injecting insulin into their first human patient: Leonard Thompson, a 14-year-old boy who weighed only 65 pounds and was close to death. The first injection caused a severe allergic reaction, but biochemist James Collip purified the extract, and a second injection on January 23 worked beautifully. Thompson's blood sugar dropped, his symptoms improved, and he went on to live another 13 years.

The discovery caused a sensation. Word spread quickly, and desperate families from around the world began arriving in Toronto seeking the miracle treatment. The results were almost unbelievable. Entire hospital wards of dying children were transformed as patients received insulin and came back to life before their families' eyes. Doctors and nurses openly wept at the sight.

Banting was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1923, sharing it with Macleod. Upset that Best had not been included, Banting shared his prize money with his assistant. In one of the most selfless acts in medical history, Banting sold the insulin patent to the University of Toronto for just one dollar. He believed that a life-saving medicine should not be used to make one person rich. This spirit of generosity echoed the same values that drove [the discovery of penicillin](/articles/discovery-of-penicillin/), where Alexander Fleming also chose not to profit from his breakthrough.

The development of insulin showed what was possible when determined scientists refused to accept the unacceptable, much like the global effort that led to [the eradication of smallpox](/articles/smallpox-eradication/) decades later.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Banting's story is a reminder that you do not need to be the smartest person in the room to change the world. He was not a brilliant researcher — he was a struggling surgeon with a bold idea and relentless determination. Many established scientists dismissed his approach, but he persisted anyway.

This teaches us that passion and hard work can overcome a lack of experience or credentials. If you believe in something strongly enough, and you are willing to put in the effort, you can achieve things that others think are impossible. The people who change the world are not always the most qualified — they are often the most determined.

Banting's decision to sell the patent for one dollar also provides a powerful lesson about values. In a world that often measures success by wealth, his choice reminds us that the greatest achievements are those that serve others. When we use our talents to help people rather than simply to profit, we create a legacy that lasts far beyond our own lifetime.

## How This Connects to 2026

More than a century after Banting and Best's discovery, insulin remains essential to the survival of hundreds of millions of people worldwide. In 2026, approximately 537 million adults are living with diabetes globally, and that number continues to grow. The World Health Organization has called diabetes one of the most important public health challenges of the 21st century.

The good news is that insulin delivery has improved dramatically. Modern insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors allow patients to manage their blood sugar with unprecedented precision. Artificial pancreas systems, which automatically adjust insulin delivery based on real-time glucose readings, are becoming more widely available and affordable.

Researchers are also making progress on potential cures. Stem cell therapies that could restore the body's ability to produce its own insulin are in clinical trials. Gene editing techniques are being explored as another potential path to a permanent solution. Meanwhile, efforts to make insulin more affordable are gaining momentum, as the high cost of insulin in countries like the United States has become a major social justice issue.

Banting would be proud to know that his one-dollar patent has grown into a global movement to make diabetes care accessible to everyone. His legacy lives on in every insulin injection, every glucose monitor, and every researcher working toward the day when diabetes is finally cured.
