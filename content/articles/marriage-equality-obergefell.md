On the morning of June 26, 2015, Jim Obergefell sat outside the Supreme Court of the United States, waiting for a decision that would change his life and the lives of millions of Americans. When the ruling came down — 5 votes to 4 — he broke down in tears. The highest court in the land had declared that same-sex couples have a constitutional right to marry. Love had won.

The case that bore Jim's name began with a simple, deeply human story. Jim Obergefell and John Arthur had been together for over 20 years. When John was diagnosed with ALS, a fatal disease that slowly takes away the ability to move and breathe, the couple decided to get married. Because their home state of Ohio did not recognize same-sex marriage, they flew to Maryland, where it was legal. John was so sick that the ceremony took place on the airport tarmac, inside a medical transport plane.

John Arthur died just months later. But Ohio refused to list Jim as his surviving spouse on the death certificate. For Jim, this was not just a legal issue — it was a denial of the love and commitment they had shared. He decided to fight, and his case eventually made it all the way to the Supreme Court.

Jim's case was joined with cases from Michigan, Kentucky, and Tennessee, all asking the same question: Does the Constitution guarantee same-sex couples the right to marry? On April 28, 2015, the justices heard arguments. On June 26, they delivered their answer.

Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote the majority opinion, and his words were powerful. "No union is more profound than marriage, for it embodies the highest ideals of love, fidelity, devotion, sacrifice, and family," he wrote. "Their hope is not to be condemned to live in loneliness, excluded from one of civilization's oldest institutions. They ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. The Constitution grants them that right."

The decision built on decades of struggle. The modern LGBTQ+ rights movement is often traced to the Stonewall riots of 1969, when patrons of a New York City bar fought back against police harassment. In the years that followed, activists worked tirelessly to change hearts, minds, and laws. They marched, organized, and told their stories, much like the civil rights activists who [marched on Washington](/articles/civil-rights-march-on-washington/) in 1963.

Progress came slowly at first. In 1996, Congress passed the Defense of Marriage Act, which defined marriage as between a man and a woman under federal law. Many states passed similar bans. But public opinion was shifting. More and more Americans came to support marriage equality as they got to know LGBTQ+ people in their own families, workplaces, and communities.

Massachusetts became the first state to legalize same-sex marriage in 2004. Other states followed. By the time the Supreme Court took up the Obergefell case, 36 states and the District of Columbia already allowed same-sex couples to marry.

The ruling drew on the same principles of equality and liberty that have shaped American history since the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/) proclaimed that all people are created equal. Justice Kennedy's opinion rooted the right to marry in the Fourteenth Amendment, which guarantees equal protection and due process under the law.

The four dissenting justices argued that the decision should have been left to the states and to voters. But for supporters of marriage equality, the ruling was a long-overdue recognition that love is love, and that the government should not discriminate based on who you love.

On the evening of June 26, the White House was lit in rainbow colors. Celebrations broke out across the country. Couples who had waited years — sometimes decades — lined up at courthouses to get married. It was a day of joy, relief, and hope.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of Obergefell v. Hodges teaches us the power of standing up for what is right, even when the odds seem stacked against you. Jim Obergefell was not a politician or a lawyer. He was an ordinary person who loved someone and refused to accept injustice. His courage changed an entire nation.

This story also reminds us that change often starts with personal connections. Many Americans changed their views on marriage equality because they knew and cared about someone who was LGBTQ+. Being open about who you are, and being willing to listen to others, can break down barriers and build understanding.

Finally, the Obergefell decision shows that progress takes time. The fight for marriage equality lasted decades. There were setbacks and heartbreaks along the way. But those who believed in equality never gave up. In your own life, when progress feels slow, remember that persistence matters. Every step forward counts.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, marriage equality is the law of the land, but the work of building a more inclusive society continues. LGBTQ+ people still face discrimination in employment, housing, and healthcare in many parts of the country and around the world. The principles established in Obergefell — that every person deserves equal dignity — remain a guiding light.

The decision also set an important precedent for how we think about civil rights in the digital age. As new forms of discrimination emerge — in algorithms, online platforms, and data collection — the legal framework built by cases like Obergefell provides tools for challenging unfairness.

Perhaps most importantly, the story of marriage equality reminds us that societies can grow and change. Ideas that once seemed radical can become widely accepted when people have the courage to speak up and the patience to keep working. In 2026, as we face new challenges around equity and inclusion, the Obergefell story is a reminder that progress is always possible.
