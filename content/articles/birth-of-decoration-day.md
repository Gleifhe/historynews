## A Graveyard Where Horses Once Ran

The war was barely over. Confederate General Robert E. Lee had surrendered at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865. Abraham Lincoln was assassinated five days later. And in Charleston, South Carolina — the city where the first shots of the Civil War had been fired at [Fort Sumter](/articles/fort-sumter-to-appomattox/) — thousands of formerly enslaved people were about to do something no one had done before.

They were going to honor the dead.

The Washington Race Course and Jockey Club in Charleston had been one of the most fashionable horse racing venues in the antebellum South. But during the final year of the war, the Confederacy converted it into an open-air prison for captured Union soldiers. Conditions were brutal. More than 260 Union prisoners of war died of disease and exposure and were buried in a mass grave behind the grandstand.

When Union forces took Charleston in February 1865, the city's Black community — most of them recently freed from slavery — saw the neglected graves and decided to act.

## Twenty-Eight Black Workmen and a White Fence

In the last days of April 1865, twenty-eight Black workmen exhumed the Union dead from the mass grave and reburied them properly in individual graves. They built a fence around the new burial ground and painted it white. Over the arched entrance, they put up a sign that read: "Martyrs of the Race Course."

These men did this work by hand, in the ruins of a city that had been the capital of the slave trade. They honored soldiers who had fought for their freedom — soldiers most of them had never met.

On May 1, 1865, approximately 10,000 people — mostly freed Black men, women, and children — gathered at the race course for a ceremony. The procession included nearly 3,000 Black schoolchildren carrying armfuls of roses and singing "John Brown's Body." Behind them marched Black women carrying baskets of flowers, wreaths, and crosses. Then came Black men marching in formation, followed by Union infantry regiments — including the 54th Massachusetts and the 34th and 104th United States Colored Troops.

The crowd decorated the graves with flowers. Ministers gave prayers. Union soldiers fired rifle salutes. Then the crowd listened to speeches from both Black and white speakers about the meaning of freedom and sacrifice.

Afterward, the soldiers drilled on the infield of the race course while the crowd picnicked among the graves. It was, in the words of a reporter from the New York Tribune who was present, "a celebration of freedom that no one who witnessed it would ever forget."

## Historical Context: Why Charleston?

Charleston was the epicenter of the American slave trade. Nearly half of all enslaved Africans brought to North America arrived through the port of Charleston. The city's wealth was built on the labor of enslaved people who worked the rice and cotton plantations of the surrounding lowcountry.

It was also where secession began. South Carolina was the first state to leave the Union in December 1860, and the Confederacy fired the first shots of the war at Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor in April 1861.

So it was profoundly meaningful that the first large-scale ceremony honoring the Union war dead took place here — organized by the very people whose enslavement had caused the war. The formerly enslaved community understood exactly what the Union soldiers had died for. As the [Emancipation Proclamation](/articles/emancipation-proclamation/) had made clear, this war had become a war for freedom.

## A Historian Uncovers the Story

For more than a century, this event was largely forgotten. The story of how Memorial Day began was claimed by multiple Northern towns — Waterloo, New York and Boalsburg, Pennsylvania among them. The Charleston ceremony, organized by Black Americans in the defeated South, didn't fit the narrative that white communities preferred to tell.

In the early 2000s, Yale historian David W. Blight was researching at Harvard's Houghton Library when he found a remarkable document — a contemporaneous account of the May 1, 1865 ceremony written by a Union officer stationed in Charleston. Blight also found corroborating reports in the New York Tribune and the Charleston Courier.

Blight published his findings in his 2001 book *Race and Reunion: The Civil War in American Memory* and in subsequent articles. His research established the Charleston ceremony as one of the earliest — and largest — known Decoration Day observances.

"This was the first Memorial Day," Blight wrote. "African Americans invented Memorial Day in Charleston, South Carolina. What you have is Black Americans recently freed from slavery giving the gift of memorialization to the nation."

Not all historians agree that this was definitively the "first" Memorial Day — several smaller ceremonies may have occurred in other Southern towns in 1864 and 1865. But the scale and organization of the Charleston event — 10,000 participants, a formal procession, decorated graves — make it the most significant early observance yet documented.

## The Aftermath: Memory and Forgetting

The Washington Race Course was subsequently renamed Hampton Park, and for years the Black community of Charleston returned there to honor the dead. But as Reconstruction ended and white supremacist governments regained power across the South, the story of the freedmen's ceremony was deliberately suppressed.

By the 1890s, the cemetery at the race course had been moved to the Beaufort National Cemetery in South Carolina, and the origin story of Memorial Day was being rewritten. Northern veterans' organizations — particularly the Grand Army of the Republic — promoted narratives that centered white communities and white veterans as the founders of the holiday. The role of formerly enslaved people was erased.

It took over a hundred years for historians like Blight to bring the Charleston story back. Today, the site is marked by a historical marker, and the story is increasingly taught in schools — though it remains far less well-known than it should be.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of the first Decoration Day is about gratitude expressed through action. These were people who had been enslaved — who had suffered unimaginable cruelty — and one of their first acts of freedom was to honor strangers who had died fighting for their liberation. They didn't wait for someone else to do it. They organized, they built, they marched, and they remembered.

That's a powerful model for anyone. When someone helps you — a teacher, a mentor, a friend — don't just feel grateful in your head. Show it. Do something. The freedmen of Charleston could have walked away from those neglected graves. They chose not to.

There's also a lesson about whose stories get told and whose get erased. For a hundred years, the Charleston ceremony was buried because it didn't fit the version of history that powerful people wanted to promote. But the truth has a way of resurfacing. If you know a story that matters — your family's story, your community's story — tell it. Write it down. Don't assume someone else will preserve it.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Memorial Day is often associated with barbecues, sales, and the unofficial start of summer. The Charleston story is a reminder of what the day was actually created for — and who created it.

The debate over who "owns" Memorial Day continues. Communities across America claim to be the birthplace of the holiday. But Blight's research forces a harder question: Why was it so easy to forget that Black Americans played a central role in creating one of the nation's most important traditions? The answer lies in the same patterns of erasure that affect how history is taught, whose monuments get built, and whose sacrifices get honored.

The freedmen of Charleston showed us something essential: the people closest to suffering often understand sacrifice most clearly. In 2026, as Americans lay flowers on graves and attend ceremonies, the original Decoration Day asks us to think about who we remember — and who we've been taught to forget.
