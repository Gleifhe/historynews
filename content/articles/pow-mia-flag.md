## A Symbol Born From Anguish

The POW/MIA flag is everywhere in America — flying over post offices, courthouses, firehouses, and veterans' memorials from coast to coast. Its design is stark and unforgettable: a white silhouette of a bowed head in profile, a guard tower, a strand of barbed wire, and the words "YOU ARE NOT FORGOTTEN," all set against a solid black background. It is the only flag in the United States, other than the Stars and Stripes, that is authorized by federal law to fly over the White House.

But the flag did not come from the government. It did not come from the military. It came from families — specifically, from the wives and mothers of American prisoners of war and service members missing in action during the Vietnam War. The story of how they created it is a story about what happens when ordinary citizens refuse to accept silence as an answer.

## Historical Context: The Missing Men of Vietnam

By the late 1960s, hundreds of American service members were being held as prisoners of war in North Vietnam, and hundreds more were listed as missing in action. The conditions in North Vietnamese prison camps — most notoriously the Hoa Lo Prison in Hanoi, which American POWs called the [Hanoi Hilton](/articles/hanoi-hilton-pows/) — were brutal. Prisoners were beaten, starved, kept in isolation, and subjected to torture designed to extract propaganda statements.

For years, the U.S. government pursued a "keep quiet" policy, advising the families of POWs and MIAs to say nothing publicly about their loved ones' captivity. The theory was that drawing attention to the prisoners would make their situation worse. The families complied — at first. But as the war dragged on and information about the treatment of prisoners began to leak out, a growing number of wives and mothers concluded that silence was not protecting their husbands and sons. Silence was abandoning them.

In 1967, Sybil Stockdale — the wife of Navy Commander James Stockdale, who was being held in the Hanoi Hilton — began quietly organizing other POW and MIA families. In 1970, that effort became the National League of POW/MIA Families, a formal organization dedicated to keeping public pressure on both the American and North Vietnamese governments to account for every missing service member.

## The Event: Designing the Flag

The idea for a flag came from Mary Hoff, a member of the National League whose husband, Lieutenant Commander Michael Hoff, was listed as missing in action. Hoff believed that the POW/MIA cause needed a single, powerful visual symbol — something that could be displayed everywhere and that communicated the message instantly.

She approached Norman Rivkees, the vice president of Annin & Co., then the largest flag manufacturer in the United States. Rivkees agreed to help. He brought in Newt Heisley, a World War II veteran and advertising professional, to design the flag.

Heisley's design process was deeply personal. He used his own son — Jeffrey Heisley, who was serving in the military at the time and had returned home ill and gaunt from a training exercise — as the model for the silhouette. The bowed head, the drawn features, the look of someone enduring suffering with quiet dignity — it was drawn from life.

The flag was first flown publicly on March 9, 1972, at a National League of POW/MIA Families event in Washington, D.C. Its impact was immediate. The stark black-and-white design stood out from every other flag. The words "YOU ARE NOT FORGOTTEN" functioned as both a promise and a challenge — a promise to the missing, a challenge to the public to remember them.

The National League adopted the flag as its official symbol, and within months it began appearing at veterans' events, on government buildings, and in communities across the country. The families had done what the government would not: they had created a permanent, visible reminder that Americans were still unaccounted for.

## The Fight for Official Recognition

Making the flag a grassroots phenomenon was one thing. Getting it officially recognized by the federal government was another — and it took decades.

In 1990, Congress passed a resolution requiring the POW/MIA flag to be flown on Armed Forces Day, Memorial Day, Flag Day, Independence Day, National POW/MIA Recognition Day (the third Friday of September), and Veterans Day at designated federal properties, including the White House, the Capitol, and all major federal buildings.

But the families pushed for more. In 2019, Congress passed the National POW/MIA Flag Act, signed into law as Public Law 116-67, which required the flag to be displayed whenever the American flag is displayed on prominent federal properties — effectively making it a permanent fixture at the White House, the Capitol, the World War II Memorial, the Korean War Veterans Memorial, the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, and every national cemetery.

No other flag in American history has received this distinction. The POW/MIA flag now flies 365 days a year over the White House, a testament to the persistence of the families who created it.

## The Ongoing Mission

The flag represents not just a symbol but an active mission. The Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency (DPAA), established by the Department of Defense, is tasked with recovering and identifying the remains of Americans still missing from past conflicts. As of 2025, more than 81,500 Americans remain unaccounted for from World War II, the Korean War, the Vietnam War, the Cold War, and the Gulf Wars.

The [search for Vietnam-era MIAs](/articles/vietnam-mia-search/) continues to this day. DPAA teams conduct excavation missions in Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, and other countries, using advanced forensic techniques including DNA analysis to identify remains that have been unrecoverable for decades. Each identification — each name matched to a set of remains — fulfills the promise stitched into the flag.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The POW/MIA flag exists because a small group of people refused to accept the status quo. The government told the families to stay quiet. The families decided that silence was unacceptable. They organized, they lobbied, they created a symbol, and they changed the national conversation. Their story is a reminder that the most powerful movements often begin not with politicians or generals but with ordinary people who decide that something matters too much to ignore.

There is also a lesson about persistence. The flag was first flown in 1972. It did not receive full federal recognition until 2019 — 47 years later. The families never stopped pushing. The lesson is simple and difficult: if the cause is right, keep going. The timeline does not matter as much as the commitment.

Finally, the flag's message — "You Are Not Forgotten" — is itself a lesson. Remembering people who are absent is an act of loyalty. It costs nothing and means everything. In your own life, reaching out to someone who might feel forgotten — a friend who has been struggling, a family member who lives far away, a veteran in your community — is one of the simplest and most meaningful things you can do.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the POW/MIA flag continues to fly over the White House and at federal buildings across the nation. The DPAA reports that it identifies an average of approximately 100 previously unaccounted-for service members each year, using increasingly sophisticated forensic tools. The National League of POW/MIA Families, now more than 55 years old, remains active in advocating for full accounting. The flag that began as a grassroots symbol has become one of the most enduring fixtures of American civic life — proof that a promise, once made visible, is harder to break.
