In December 1938, a young British stockbroker named Nicholas Winton canceled his ski vacation and traveled to Prague, Czechoslovakia. What he saw there changed his life — and saved the lives of 669 children. Nazi Germany had just taken over parts of Czechoslovakia, and Jewish families were desperate to get their children to safety. Winton decided he would help, even though he had no experience in rescue work and no official support.

Working from his hotel room in Prague, Winton began making lists of children whose parents wanted them sent to safety in Britain. He took photographs, gathered documents, and started the enormous task of finding British families willing to take in refugee children. He also had to raise money for travel costs and a fifty-pound guarantee (a large sum at the time) that the British government required for each child.

Back in London, Winton worked on the rescue operation from his office desk during lunch breaks and after work. He created a small organization, advertised in newspapers for foster families, and fought through mountains of paperwork. The British government had already set up a program called Kindertransport — "children's transport" in German — that allowed Jewish children to enter Britain without visas. But the official program was slow, and Winton knew that time was running out.

Between March and August 1939, Winton organized eight trains that carried children from Prague through Germany and the Netherlands to Britain. The journeys were terrifying for the young passengers, some as young as two years old. Parents said goodbye at the Prague train station, not knowing if they would ever see their children again. Many never did — most of the parents who stayed behind were killed in the Holocaust.

A ninth train was scheduled to leave Prague on September 1, 1939 — the day Germany invaded Poland and World War II began. That train, carrying 250 children, never departed. Nearly all of those children died in concentration camps. It was a tragedy that haunted Winton for the rest of his life.

After the war, Winton never spoke publicly about what he had done. He went back to his normal life, working and raising a family. The rescued children grew up in Britain, many not knowing the full story of how they got there. For fifty years, Winton's heroism remained completely unknown.

Then, in 1988, Winton's wife Grete found a scrapbook in their attic. Inside were lists of children's names, photographs, and letters from parents. She showed it to a Holocaust researcher, and the story finally came to light. The BBC television show *That's Life!* invited Winton to be in the audience — and then revealed that the people sitting around him were the very children he had saved, now adults with families of their own. The moment when they stood up and embraced him became one of the most emotional scenes ever broadcast on British television.

Winton's story echoes the experiences described in [Anne Frank's diary](/articles/anne-frank-diary/), which showed the world what Jewish children endured during the Nazi era. His actions also stand as a powerful counterpoint to the horrors of [the Holocaust](/articles/the-holocaust/) itself, proving that even in humanity's darkest hour, individuals could make an extraordinary difference.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Nicholas Winton's story teaches us that you don't need a title, a position, or special training to save lives. He was an ordinary person — a stockbroker who liked skiing. But he saw a need, and instead of waiting for someone else to act, he stepped up. He didn't ask for permission or wait for the perfect plan. He just started doing what needed to be done.

This is a lesson we can all use in our daily lives. When you see a problem, don't wait for someone more qualified or more powerful to solve it. Start where you are, with what you have. Winton worked from his office desk during lunch breaks. He used his own money. He made phone calls and wrote letters. None of it was glamorous, but all of it mattered.

His fifty years of silence also teach us something important about humility. Winton didn't do what he did for praise or recognition. He did it because it was the right thing to do. True courage doesn't need an audience.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, millions of children around the world are refugees, displaced by war, poverty, and climate change. Organizations like UNICEF, Save the Children, and the International Rescue Committee work every day to protect vulnerable young people — continuing the kind of work Winton began in 1938.

Winton's story also reminds us of the power of ordinary citizens to influence government policy. His lobbying of British officials helped create pathways for refugee children. Today, citizens continue to advocate for welcoming refugee policies in their communities and countries.

The discovery of Winton's scrapbook in 1988 shows how important it is to preserve and share stories of courage. In the digital age of 2026, we have more tools than ever to document acts of kindness and heroism. Social media and online archives help ensure that stories like Winton's reach new generations who can be inspired to act.

Nicholas Winton was knighted in 2003 and died in 2015 at the age of 106. Today, the descendants of the children he saved number over 6,000. His life proves that one person's decision to help can echo through generations.
