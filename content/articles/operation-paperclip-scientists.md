In the final months of World War II, as Allied armies advanced into Germany from east and west, a secret race began. It was not a race for territory. It was a race for brains. American, British, and Soviet intelligence teams scoured German laboratories, universities, and weapons facilities, searching for the scientists who had built the Third Reich's most advanced weapons — and offering them a deal: come work for us, or fall into the hands of the other side.

## The Program

Operation Paperclip — named for the paperclips attached to the personnel files of selected scientists — was the U.S. program to recruit German scientists and engineers for American military research. It began in 1945 under the Joint Intelligence Objectives Agency (JIOA) and continued through the 1950s. Over the course of the program, more than 1,600 Germans were brought to the United States with their families.

The recruits included specialists in rocketry, aeronautics, medicine, chemistry, and physics. They were assigned to military installations, research labs, and defense contractors across the country. Their expertise accelerated American programs in ballistic missiles, jet propulsion, aerospace medicine, and chemical weapons.

## The Moral Problem

President Truman had ordered that no one with Nazi Party membership or active support for Nazism should be recruited. The JIOA routinely circumvented this directive. Officers sanitized personnel files — removing references to Nazi membership, SS service, or war crimes — to make recruits eligible for admission.

The most famous Paperclip scientist was Wernher von Braun, the brilliant rocket engineer who had developed the V-2 ballistic missile for Nazi Germany. The V-2 was built by slave laborers at the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp, where an estimated 20,000 prisoners died from starvation, disease, and brutal working conditions. Von Braun, who held the rank of SS Sturmbannführer (major), later claimed he was aware of the conditions but powerless to change them. His critics argued he was more complicit than he admitted.

In the United States, von Braun became a celebrity — the public face of the American rocket program. He led the development of the Redstone missile, the Jupiter rocket, and ultimately the Saturn V that carried the [Apollo 11](/articles/apollo-11-mission/) astronauts to the Moon. His wartime past was known but largely ignored.

Other Paperclip scientists had darker records. Hubertus Strughold, called the "father of space medicine," was linked to human experimentation at Dachau. Kurt Blome, who directed Nazi biological weapons research, was recruited despite being tried (and acquitted) at Nuremberg. Walter Schreiber, a former surgeon general of the German Army, was connected to medical experiments on concentration camp prisoners.

## The Strategic Logic

The justification was always the same: if we do not take them, the Soviets will. And the Soviets were, in fact, running their own recruitment program. Both sides understood that the scientists who had built the V-2 could build the intercontinental ballistic missiles that would define the Cold War.

The strategic logic was sound. Paperclip scientists made significant contributions to American military technology and space exploration. The knowledge they brought accelerated programs that would otherwise have taken years or decades to develop. The United States won the Space Race and maintained technological superiority over the Soviet Union in part because of the head start provided by German expertise.

## The Legacy

Operation Paperclip remained classified for decades. Its full scope was not publicly acknowledged until the 1990s, when investigative journalists and historians gained access to declassified documents. The program raises questions that have never been fully resolved: Is it acceptable to trade moral accountability for strategic advantage? Where is the line between pragmatism and complicity?

## What This Means for You in 2026

Operation Paperclip is a case study in the cost of moral compromise. The scientists delivered genuine value — rockets, satellites, medical advances. But the price was a decision to overlook crimes committed against the most vulnerable people on Earth. In your own decisions, be honest about the tradeoffs you are making. Pragmatism is not inherently wrong, but refusing to acknowledge the moral cost of a pragmatic choice is. You can make a hard decision and still call it what it is.
