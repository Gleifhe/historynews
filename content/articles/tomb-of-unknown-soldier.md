## One Body, One Nation

On the morning of November 11, 1921 — the third anniversary of the armistice that ended World War I — a horse-drawn caisson carried a flag-draped casket through the streets of Washington, D.C. Inside the casket lay the remains of an American soldier. Nobody knew his name.

That was the point.

President Warren G. Harding walked behind the caisson to [Arlington National Cemetery](/articles/arlington-national-cemetery/), where an enormous crowd had gathered on the hillside overlooking the Potomac. Foreign dignitaries, Gold Star mothers, wounded veterans, and ordinary citizens stood in silence as the casket was carried to a white marble tomb on the cemetery's plaza.

The Unknown Soldier was awarded the Medal of Honor and the Distinguished Service Cross. Allied nations bestowed their own highest military decorations. Then the casket was lowered into the tomb, and a 50,000-pound marble sarcophagus was placed over it.

The inscription on the tomb's west face reads: **"Here rests in honored glory an American soldier known but to God."**

## How He Was Chosen

The process of selecting the Unknown Soldier was designed to ensure that no one — not even the military — could identify him.

In October 1921, four unidentified American remains were exhumed from four separate American cemeteries in France: Aisne-Marne, Meuse-Argonne, Somme, and St. Mihiel. Each set of remains had been confirmed as belonging to an American serviceman, but none could be identified by name, unit, or any personal effects.

The four caskets were brought to the city hall in Châlons-sur-Marne, France. On October 24, 1921, Sergeant Edward F. Younger — a decorated combat veteran who had been wounded in battle and gassed — was given the task of choosing one.

Younger entered the room where the four identical caskets were laid out. He carried a spray of white roses. He walked past the caskets, paused, and placed the roses on the third casket from the left. Years later, Younger said he had felt drawn to that casket but could never explain why.

The selected remains were placed in a specially made casket and transported aboard the USS *Olympia* — Admiral Dewey's flagship from the Spanish-American War — back to the United States. The three unchosen remains were buried with full military honors at the Meuse-Argonne American Cemetery in France.

## The Ceremony

The funeral on November 11, 1921 was one of the largest public ceremonies in American history to that date. President Harding delivered the eulogy, speaking to a crowd of approximately 100,000 people gathered at Arlington and along the procession route.

"We do not know the eminence of his birth, but we do know the glory of his death," Harding said. "He died for his country, and greater devotion hath no man than this."

Chief Plenty Coups of the Crow Nation placed his war bonnet and coup stick on the casket — a gesture of extraordinary honor in Native American tradition. He said: "I feel it an honor to the red man that he, too, is represented here."

The ceremony connected the sacrifice of World War I to the longer tradition of American military service. Many of those buried nearby at Arlington had fought in the Civil War, and the ideals they died for — union, freedom, democracy — were the same ideals invoked to justify the war in Europe.

## The Perpetual Guard

For the first eleven years after the burial, the tomb was open to the public, and visitors occasionally behaved disrespectfully — sitting on the tomb, picnicking nearby, and leaving trash. In 1925, the first civilian guards were posted intermittently. In 1926, a military guard was stationed during cemetery hours.

On July 2, 1937, the Army began the 24-hour guard that continues to this day. The sentinels are members of the 3rd U.S. Infantry Regiment, known as "The Old Guard" — the oldest active-duty infantry unit in the Army, established in 1784.

The requirements for becoming a sentinel are among the most demanding in the military. Candidates must be between 5 feet 10 inches and 6 feet 4 inches tall, with a proportional weight and build. They undergo an exhaustive training period during which they memorize the locations of approximately 300 notable graves at Arlington. They must pass uniform inspections so rigorous that a single loose thread can disqualify them.

The sentinel walks a precise pattern: 21 steps south, a pause of 21 seconds facing east, a turn, 21 steps north, a pause of 21 seconds facing north, a turn. The number 21 represents the highest symbolic military honor — the 21-gun salute.

The guard has never been abandoned. Sentinels have maintained their post through hurricanes, blizzards, the September 11 attacks, and the COVID-19 pandemic. During Hurricane Derecho in 2012, with 70-mph winds battering Arlington, sentinels continued their patrol without interruption.

## Additional Unknown Soldiers

The original 1921 tomb holds only the World War I Unknown. In 1958, unknown service members from World War II and the Korean War were interred in crypts beside the original tomb. President Eisenhower presided over the ceremony.

In 1984, an unknown service member from the Vietnam War was interred in a third crypt. However, in 1998, advances in DNA technology led to the identification of those remains as First Lieutenant Michael Joseph Blassie of the U.S. Air Force, who was shot down near An Lộc, South Vietnam, in 1972. Blassie's remains were disinterred and returned to his family for burial in St. Louis.

The Vietnam crypt now remains empty, covered with a marble slab that reads: "Honoring and Keeping Faith with America's Missing Servicemembers." Because of advances in forensic identification, it is unlikely that another truly unidentifiable set of remains from recent conflicts will ever be interred there.

This reality speaks to a broader shift. In the wars fought by the generation that stormed the beaches on [D-Day](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/), thousands of soldiers were buried without names. Modern science has made it possible to identify remains that were once considered permanently unknown — a development that is both a triumph of technology and a quiet ending to the era the tomb represents.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier carries a powerful idea: you don't have to know someone's name to honor their sacrifice. The Unknown Soldier represents every service member who gave everything and received nothing in return — no recognition, no headstone with a name, no family notified.

In your own life, think about the people who make sacrifices that go unrecognized. The parent who works a second job so you can have opportunities. The teacher who stays late. The friend who shows up when no one else does. You may not always know the full extent of what people give up for you. The tomb reminds us to honor sacrifice even when — especially when — we can't see it all.

The sentinels also teach something about devotion to duty. These soldiers walk their post in silence, in all weather, whether anyone is watching or not. They don't do it for praise or social media attention. They do it because they said they would. That kind of quiet, consistent commitment is rare and powerful. It's the opposite of doing things only when they're convenient or when someone is watching.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier remains one of the most visited sites in the Washington, D.C. area, drawing millions of visitors annually. The changing of the guard ceremony continues to move spectators to silence and, frequently, tears.

But the tomb's meaning is evolving. With DNA technology making it possible to identify nearly every fallen service member, the concept of the "unknown" soldier is becoming historical rather than ongoing. The Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency (DPAA) continues to identify remains from World War II, Korea, and Vietnam — bringing closure to families decades after their loved ones were lost.

This raises a philosophical question: In an age when science can name the nameless, what does the tomb represent? The answer may be shifting from "we don't know who this is" to "this represents everyone we've lost" — a symbol not of ignorance but of collective grief.

The sentinel's vigil continues, as it has for nearly ninety years. Rain or shine. Day or night. Known but to God.
