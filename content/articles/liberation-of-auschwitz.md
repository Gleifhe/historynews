## What the Soldiers Found

On the morning of January 27, 1945, soldiers of the Soviet 322nd Rifle Division approached a vast complex of buildings surrounded by barbed wire fences near the town of Oświęcim in southern Poland. The Germans called it Auschwitz.

Nothing in their experience — not the brutal fighting on the Eastern Front, not the devastation of Stalingrad — had prepared them for what they found inside.

Roughly 7,000 prisoners were still alive in the camp. Most were too sick or weak to walk. They were skeletal, dressed in thin striped uniforms, huddled in unheated barracks in the middle of a Polish winter. Many weighed less than 80 pounds. Soviet medics who rushed in to help later said the survivors looked more like ghosts than human beings.

The soldiers also found warehouses full of evidence the Nazis had not had time to destroy: 7,000 kilograms of human hair, hundreds of thousands of men's suits and women's dresses, and tens of thousands of pairs of shoes — including tiny shoes that had belonged to children.

## The Death Factory

Auschwitz was not a single camp but a network of more than 40 sub-camps. The main camp, Auschwitz I, served as an administrative center. Auschwitz II, known as Birkenau, was the killing center. Auschwitz III and its sub-camps provided forced labor for German industry.

Between 1940 and 1945, the Nazis murdered approximately 1.1 million people at Auschwitz. About 90 percent of the victims were Jewish. The rest included Polish political prisoners, Roma and Sinti people, Soviet prisoners of war, and others the Nazis deemed undesirable.

The killing was industrialized. Trains brought prisoners from across occupied Europe. Upon arrival, SS doctors conducted "selections" — deciding in seconds who would be sent to forced labor and who would be sent immediately to the gas chambers. Those selected for death were told they were going to take showers. They were herded into sealed rooms, and Zyklon B gas was pumped in through vents in the ceiling. The horror of this system would later be laid bare at the [Nuremberg Trials](/articles/nuremberg-trials/), where Nazi leaders faced justice for their crimes.

## The Death Marches

By January 1945, Soviet forces were closing in from the east. The Nazis knew the war was lost, but they were determined to destroy the evidence of their crimes and keep their prisoners from being liberated.

On January 17, ten days before the Soviets arrived, the SS forced roughly 60,000 Auschwitz prisoners on death marches westward. Prisoners were made to walk for days in freezing temperatures with little food or water. Those who fell behind or collapsed were shot on the spot. An estimated 15,000 prisoners died during these marches.

The 7,000 who remained in the camp when the Soviets arrived were those too sick to march — the ones the Nazis had essentially left to die.

## Bearing Witness

Soviet cameramen filmed what they found at Auschwitz. The footage was among the most disturbing ever recorded. It showed emaciated survivors behind barbed wire, mass graves, and the ruins of the gas chambers and crematoria that the Germans had tried to blow up before retreating.

This footage, along with similar evidence from other camps liberated in the following months, shocked the world. When Allied soldiers [fought their way across Europe after D-Day](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/), they liberated camps in the West — Dachau, Buchenwald, Bergen-Belsen — and found similar horrors. General Dwight D. Eisenhower ordered that the camps be photographed and filmed extensively, saying the evidence should be preserved so that no one could ever claim it didn't happen.

The liberation of Auschwitz is now recognized as one of the defining moments of [the Holocaust](/articles/the-holocaust/), the systematic murder of six million Jews and millions of others by the Nazi regime.

## After Liberation

Many survivors were so weakened that they continued to die in the days and weeks after liberation, even with medical care. Those who recovered faced an agonizing reality: most of their families had been murdered. Their homes had been taken. Their communities had been destroyed.

Some survivors stayed in displaced persons camps for years, waiting for countries to accept them. Others emigrated to Palestine, the United States, or other nations to rebuild their lives. Many did not speak about their experiences for decades, carrying the trauma in silence.

In 2005, the United Nations designated January 27 — the anniversary of the liberation of Auschwitz — as International Holocaust Remembrance Day.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The liberation of Auschwitz forces us to confront one of the hardest truths about humanity: that ordinary people can participate in extraordinary evil. The guards at Auschwitz were not monsters from another planet. They were human beings who made choices — to follow orders, to look away, to dehumanize others. Recognizing this capacity in ourselves is uncomfortable, but it is essential. It keeps us alert to the small steps by which a society slides toward cruelty.

The survivors also teach us about the depth of human resilience. People who lost everything — family, health, home, identity — found ways to rebuild their lives, to love again, to create meaning from unimaginable suffering. Their example is a reminder that even the worst experiences do not have to define you permanently.

Finally, Auschwitz teaches us the absolute necessity of speaking up. Many people across Europe knew what was happening and said nothing. Silence in the face of injustice is not neutrality — it is complicity. When you see someone being mistreated, bullied, or dehumanized, your silence gives permission for the behavior to continue.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the last living survivors of the Holocaust are in their nineties. Within a few years, there will be no one left who can say, "I was there. I saw it. It happened." This makes Holocaust education and remembrance more important than ever.

At the same time, surveys consistently show that young people know less about the Holocaust than previous generations. A 2020 study found that nearly two-thirds of young American adults did not know that six million Jews were murdered. Misinformation and denial continue to spread online, often disguised as "just asking questions."

The rise of authoritarian movements around the world makes the lessons of Auschwitz especially urgent. When leaders scapegoat minorities, undermine democratic institutions, and use propaganda to divide people, they are following a playbook that history has shown us before. The liberation of Auschwitz is not just a historical event to be remembered — it is a warning that demands constant vigilance.
