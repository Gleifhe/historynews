## The War Enters Its Seventh Year

By the summer of 1781, the American Revolution had dragged on for six years. Both sides were exhausted. The Continental Army, which had been forged in the terrible winter at [Valley Forge](/articles/valley-forge/), was experienced but undersupplied. The British held New York City and key positions in the South. Neither side could deliver a knockout blow.

George Washington was growing desperate. His army was shrinking as enlistments expired. Congress was nearly bankrupt. The French alliance, secured in 1778, had provided essential support — money, soldiers, and a powerful navy — but had not yet produced a decisive victory.

Then, in August 1781, Washington received intelligence that changed everything. British General Charles Cornwallis had marched his army of about 8,000 troops to the small tobacco port of Yorktown, Virginia, on the Chesapeake Bay. He was fortifying the position and waiting for reinforcements and supplies by sea.

Washington saw the opportunity of the war.

## The Plan Comes Together

The Yorktown campaign was a masterpiece of coordination between American and French forces. Washington and French General Jean-Baptiste de Rochambeau had been planning an attack on the British in New York. But when word came that French Admiral François de Grasse was sailing a fleet of 28 warships from the Caribbean to the Chesapeake, they changed plans immediately.

Washington and Rochambeau marched their combined force of about 17,000 troops — roughly 9,500 American and 7,500 French — south from New York to Virginia in a rapid, 450-mile march. It was the longest march of the war, and they managed it in just a few weeks.

The deception was critical. Washington left a small force behind to make the British in New York believe a major attack was coming. He had campfires lit, fake fortifications built, and supply boats visible on the Hudson River. By the time the British commander in New York, General Henry Clinton, realized Washington had slipped away, it was too late.

## The French Navy Seals the Trap

On September 5, 1781, Admiral de Grasse's fleet fought the Battle of the Chesapeake against a British fleet under Admiral Thomas Graves. The battle lasted about two hours, and although neither side lost a ship, the French inflicted enough damage to force the British fleet to retreat to New York for repairs.

This naval engagement was arguably the most important of the entire war. With the French fleet controlling the Chesapeake Bay, Cornwallis could not escape by sea, and the British could not send reinforcements or supplies. The trap was shut.

Cornwallis was surrounded — the French fleet to the east, and the combined American-French army closing in from the west.

## The Siege

The formal siege began on September 28, 1781, when American and French forces encircled Yorktown. Washington's army was well trained in siege warfare, thanks in part to French engineers and the discipline instilled by von Steuben at Valley Forge.

The allies dug a series of parallel trenches, moving their artillery closer to the British lines with each advance. On October 9, the allied artillery opened fire. Washington himself reportedly fired the first cannon shot. The bombardment was devastating — over 100 guns poured fire into the British positions day and night.

Two key British defensive positions, Redoubts 9 and 10, blocked the completion of the second parallel trench. On the night of October 14, two assault forces — one American, one French — attacked the redoubts simultaneously. The American force, led by Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Hamilton, captured Redoubt 10 in about ten minutes of fierce hand-to-hand fighting. The French captured Redoubt 9 shortly after.

With the second parallel complete, the allied guns were close enough to rake the entire British position. Cornwallis' situation was hopeless.

## The Surrender

On October 17, 1781 — four years to the day after the American victory at Saratoga — a British drummer boy appeared on the ramparts at Yorktown, beating a signal for a parlay. A British officer waved a white handkerchief.

Cornwallis asked for a 24-hour ceasefire to negotiate terms. Washington gave him two hours.

On October 19, 1781, more than 7,000 British soldiers marched out of Yorktown and laid down their arms. According to tradition, the British band played a tune called "The World Turned Upside Down" — though historians debate whether this actually happened.

Cornwallis himself did not attend the surrender ceremony, claiming illness. He sent his second-in-command, Brigadier General Charles O'Hara, who attempted to surrender his sword to Rochambeau — perhaps to save face by surrendering to a fellow European rather than to the colonial rebels. Rochambeau directed O'Hara to Washington. Washington, in turn, directed O'Hara to his own second-in-command, General Benjamin Lincoln.

## After Yorktown

When news of Yorktown reached London, British Prime Minister Lord North reportedly said: "Oh God, it is all over." He was right. Although scattered fighting continued for another year, Britain had lost the will to continue the war. In September 1783, the Treaty of Paris officially recognized the independence of the United States.

The revolution that began with [a shot at Lexington Green](/articles/lexington-concord/) ended with a surrender at Yorktown. The ideals outlined in the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/) — that all people are created equal and have the right to govern themselves — had been defended on the battlefield and now had a nation to call their own.

The American victory also inspired revolutionary movements across the globe. In France, soldiers who fought at Yorktown returned home with ideas about liberty and equality that helped fuel the French Revolution. In the Caribbean, the ideals of the American Revolution echoed in the [Haitian Revolution](/articles/haitian-revolution/), where enslaved people fought for their own freedom.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Yorktown teaches us about the power of seizing the right moment. Washington spent six years fighting a war he was mostly losing. He suffered defeats, endured betrayals, and watched his army nearly disintegrate. But when the right opportunity finally appeared — Cornwallis exposed at Yorktown, the French fleet available — Washington moved decisively. Patience and persistence are essential, but so is the ability to recognize and act on the moment when everything comes together.

The campaign also demonstrates the importance of teamwork and alliances. The Americans could not have won at Yorktown without the French — their soldiers, their ships, their money, and their engineers. Asking for help is not a sign of weakness. It is often the key to success. The greatest achievements are rarely solo efforts.

Finally, the surrender ceremony carries a lesson in humility and respect. Washington could have humiliated Cornwallis. Instead, the surrender was conducted with military formality and dignity. Treating others with respect, even in victory, speaks to character. How you win matters as much as whether you win.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, as the United States approaches the 250th anniversary of its independence, Yorktown serves as a reminder of how fragile the nation's founding was. Victory was never guaranteed. It required sacrifice, international cooperation, brilliant timing, and more than a little luck.

The French-American alliance at Yorktown also speaks to the importance of international partnerships. In a world of growing nationalism and isolationism, the Yorktown story reminds us that the United States literally could not have been born without foreign allies willing to fight and die alongside American soldiers.

The campaign's lessons about strategic deception and rapid movement remain studied at military academies around the world. Washington's march from New York to Virginia — keeping the British guessing until it was too late — is considered one of the great strategic maneuvers in military history. In an age of information warfare and cyber deception, the principles Washington employed remain remarkably relevant.
