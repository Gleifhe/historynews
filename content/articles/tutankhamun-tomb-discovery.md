## Six Years of Digging

By 1922, British archaeologist Howard Carter had been searching the Valley of the Kings in Egypt for six years. He was looking for the tomb of Tutankhamun — a relatively minor pharaoh who had ruled Egypt around 1332 BC and died when he was only about 18 years old.

Most experts thought the Valley of the Kings had been thoroughly explored. Every tomb worth finding had been found — and most had been robbed by tomb raiders centuries ago.

Carter's sponsor, Lord Carnarvon, a wealthy British aristocrat, was running out of patience and money. He told Carter that the 1922 season would be the last one he would fund. If Carter didn't find something, the search was over.

## The Discovery

On November 4, 1922, a water boy working on Carter's team stumbled on a stone step carved into the rock. Carter's workers carefully cleared away the sand and revealed a staircase leading down to a sealed door.

Carter could see ancient plaster seals on the door — seals bearing the name of Tutankhamun.

He was trembling. He sent a telegram to Lord Carnarvon in England: **"At last have made wonderful discovery in Valley; a magnificent tomb with seals intact; re-covered same for your arrival; congratulations."**

Carter had to wait three weeks for Carnarvon to travel from England to Egypt. It must have been the longest three weeks of his life.

## "Can You See Anything?"

On November 26, 1922, Carter, Carnarvon, and a small group gathered at the sealed door. Carter carefully chipped a small hole in the upper left corner of the door. He held up a candle and peered inside.

The candle flickered in the rush of escaping air — air that had been sealed inside for over 3,000 years.

Carter later described what he saw:

> "At first I could see nothing, the hot air escaping from the chamber causing the candle flame to flicker, but presently, as my eyes grew accustomed to the light, details of the room within emerged slowly from the mist, strange animals, statues, and gold — everywhere the glint of gold."

Lord Carnarvon, standing behind him, asked: **"Can you see anything?"**

Carter replied with two words that became some of the most famous in archaeology: **"Yes, wonderful things."**

## What Was Inside

The tomb contained four chambers packed with over 5,000 objects — the most complete royal burial treasure ever found in Egypt:

- **The golden death mask**: A stunning mask of solid gold weighing 24 pounds, placed directly over the mummy's face. It is one of the most recognized artifacts in the world
- **Three nested coffins**: The inner coffin was made of solid gold and weighed 243 pounds
- **A golden throne**: Decorated with scenes of the young king and his wife
- **Chariots, weapons, and furniture**: Including a camping bed that folded up — the world's oldest known folding furniture
- **Food and wine**: Left for the king's journey to the afterlife
- **Tutankhamun's mummy**: Preserved for over 3,000 years

## Egyptomania Sweeps the World

The discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb was an instant worldwide sensation. Newspapers sent reporters to Egypt. Newsreels showed footage of the excavation. Carter became one of the most famous people in the world.

A craze called "Egyptomania" swept through Western culture:

- **Fashion**: Egyptian-inspired jewelry, clothing, and hairstyles became trendy
- **Architecture**: Buildings were designed with Egyptian motifs
- **Movies**: Egyptian themes appeared in Hollywood films
- **Music**: Songs with Egyptian themes became popular

The tomb's discovery also sparked intense interest in ancient Egyptian history and culture that continues to this day — an interest first ignited by [the decoding of the Rosetta Stone](/articles/rosetta-stone/) a century earlier.

## The "Curse" of the Pharaoh

When Lord Carnarvon died from an infected mosquito bite on April 5, 1923 — just a few months after the tomb was opened — newspapers went wild with stories about a "curse of the pharaohs." They claimed that anyone who disturbed Tutankhamun's rest would die.

Several other people connected to the excavation died in the following years, and each death was attributed to the "curse." It made great headlines.

The truth is much less dramatic. Most people involved in the excavation lived long, healthy lives. Howard Carter himself — the man who actually opened the tomb — lived until 1939 and died at the perfectly normal age of 64.

## Why It Still Matters

Tutankhamun's tomb is important for several reasons:

- **It's the most complete pharaoh's burial ever found**, giving us an unparalleled look at ancient Egyptian royal life
- **It proved that major discoveries were still possible** in areas that had been explored for centuries
- **It made archaeology exciting to the general public**, inspiring generations of scientists and explorers

Smithsonian Magazine's coverage, combined with the Griffith Institute's archive of Carter's original photographs and notes, allows anyone to experience the discovery that captured the world's imagination over 100 years ago.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Howard Carter's story is a masterclass in persistence. He searched the Valley of the Kings for six years while experts told him he was wasting his time. He was on his very last season — his funding was about to be cut — when he finally found the tomb. If he had given up one year earlier, someone else might have made the discovery, or it might still be buried today. The lesson is simple: don't quit just because results aren't coming fast enough.

Carter also succeeded because he was willing to do the unglamorous work. Before the thrilling moment of peering into the tomb, there were years of digging in the heat, cataloging rocks, and studying maps. Most big achievements in life look the same way — long stretches of boring, difficult work followed by a breakthrough that seems sudden to everyone else but wasn't.

The story of Egyptomania is also a useful lesson about hype. The whole world went crazy for Egyptian fashion, art, and culture after the discovery. But much of that excitement was shallow — people were more interested in curses and gold than in actually understanding ancient Egyptian civilization. Learning to go deeper than the surface-level hype, in any subject, is what separates real understanding from passing fads.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, new technologies are transforming how we discover and study the past. AI-powered tools can analyze satellite images to find buried structures, translate ancient texts in minutes, and even reconstruct damaged artifacts digitally. What took Howard Carter years of painstaking work can now be accelerated dramatically with technology. But the human qualities that made Carter successful — curiosity, patience, and dedication — are still irreplaceable.

The debate over who owns ancient artifacts is more relevant than ever. Tutankhamun's treasures are in Egypt, but many other countries' artifacts — including treasures like [the Hope Diamond](/articles/hope-diamond/) — sit in museums in London, Paris, and New York. In 2026, nations around the world are demanding the return of their cultural heritage. This connects to broader conversations about colonialism, justice, and who gets to tell whose story.

The Egyptomania of the 1920s also mirrors how trends spread in 2026 — just much faster. Today, a single viral TikTok can create a global craze overnight. AI can generate endless content about any topic, flooding the internet with surface-level takes. The challenge is the same one Carter would recognize: getting past the hype and doing the real work of understanding what matters.
