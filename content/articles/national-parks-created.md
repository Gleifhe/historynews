On March 1, 1872, President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law that had never been tried before. He set aside over two million acres of wild land in Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho as Yellowstone National Park. This was the first time any country in the world had protected a large piece of nature for the enjoyment of all people, not just the wealthy or powerful. It was a bold and simple idea: some places are too special to be owned by anyone. They belong to everyone.

Before Yellowstone, there was no such thing as a national park. Wild places were seen mostly as resources to be used — forests to cut, land to farm, rivers to dam. But explorers who visited the Yellowstone region came back with stories of boiling springs, towering waterfalls, and geysers that shot water hundreds of feet into the sky. They argued that this land should never be sold or settled. Congress agreed, and the national park idea was born.

The idea might have stayed small if not for one man: President Theodore Roosevelt. Roosevelt loved the outdoors more than almost anything. He hiked, hunted, and camped across the American West. When he became president in 1901, he used his power to protect wild places on a massive scale. During his time in office, Roosevelt created five new national parks, 18 national monuments, and 150 national forests. He protected roughly 230 million acres of public land — an area larger than the states of Texas and California combined.

Roosevelt once said, "The nation behaves well if it treats the natural resources as assets which it must turn over to the next generation increased, and not impaired, in value." He believed protecting nature was not just nice — it was a duty.

Over the following decades, the national park system kept growing. The Grand Canyon, the Great Smoky Mountains, Zion, Acadia, and dozens more joined the list. In 1916, Congress created the National Park Service to manage all of these places. Today, the system includes 63 national parks and over 400 other protected sites, from battlefields to seashores to historic buildings.

The numbers are staggering. More than 312 million people visit national parks each year. Yellowstone alone gets about four million visitors annually. These parks protect some of the last truly wild places in the country — home to grizzly bears, wolves, bison, and thousands of other species. The [successful reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone](/articles/wolves-yellowstone/) showed how protecting wild spaces can help entire ecosystems heal and thrive.

National parks have also inspired the world. After America's example, countries on every continent created their own park systems. Today, there are thousands of national parks around the globe, from the Galápagos Islands to the Serengeti to the Swiss Alps. The idea that started at Yellowstone became a worldwide movement.

The parks have not been perfect. For much of their history, Indigenous peoples were pushed off their ancestral lands to create these protected areas. In recent years, the National Park Service has worked to recognize this painful history and partner with tribal nations. There is still much work to be done, but the conversation has changed for the better.

Writer and historian Wallace Stegner called national parks "the best idea we ever had." He meant that choosing to protect nature — instead of always using it for profit — showed something good about the American character. It proved that a country could value beauty, wildness, and the future just as much as money and growth.

The parks also helped save species from disappearing. Animals like the [bald eagle](/articles/bald-eagle-recovery/) were given safe habitats inside park boundaries, which played a key role in their recovery from the edge of extinction.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of national parks teaches us that protecting what matters takes courage and long-term thinking. When President Grant signed the Yellowstone Act, he could not have known that millions of people would benefit more than a century later. But he chose to act anyway.

In our own lives, we face similar choices. Do we use up everything now, or do we save some for the future? Do we take care of the things we love, even when no one is watching? The national parks remind us that the best decisions are often the ones that benefit people we will never meet. Thinking beyond ourselves — about future generations, about the health of the planet — is one of the most powerful things we can do.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, national parks face new challenges. Record-breaking heat, wildfires, and overcrowding threaten many parks. Some glaciers in Glacier National Park have nearly disappeared. But the park system is adapting. New reservation systems help manage crowds. Restoration projects are repairing damaged trails and habitats. And public support for national parks remains strong across political lines.

The lesson from 1872 still applies: when we choose to protect nature, good things follow. Whether it is volunteering for a park cleanup, supporting conservation funding, or simply visiting a park and leaving it better than you found it, every person can be part of this story. The parks belong to all of us — and so does the responsibility to keep them wild.
