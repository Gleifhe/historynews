Wake Island is a V-shaped coral atoll in the central Pacific, roughly 2,300 miles west of Honolulu and 1,500 miles east of Guam. It has no indigenous population. It has no natural fresh water. It measures barely 2.5 square miles. In the grand strategy of World War II, it was a speck — but for sixteen extraordinary days in December 1941, it became a symbol of defiance that a devastated nation desperately needed.

## The Outpost

The United States had claimed Wake Island in 1899 and used it primarily as a cable relay station and seaplane base. In the late 1930s, as war with Japan became increasingly likely, the Navy began constructing a military airstrip and fortifications. By December 1941, the garrison consisted of 449 Marines of the 1st Defense Battalion under Major James Devereux, 68 Navy personnel, and about 1,200 civilian construction workers employed by Morrison-Knudsen Company.

They had twelve F4F Wildcat fighters (the only aircraft on the island), six 5-inch coastal defense guns, and a handful of 3-inch anti-aircraft batteries. There was no radar. Reinforcements were expected but had not arrived.

## The Attack

Hours after the attack on Pearl Harbor, on December 8, 1941 (December 7 east of the International Date Line), Japanese bombers struck Wake, destroying eight of the twelve Wildcats on the ground. The initial bombing killed 23 Americans and wounded many more.

On December 11, a Japanese invasion force — two light cruisers, six destroyers, two patrol boats, and two troop transports — approached Wake Island expecting easy pickings. They got a shock. The Marines held their fire until the ships were within 4,500 yards, then opened up with their 5-inch guns. The destroyer Hayate was sunk with all hands — the first Japanese surface warship lost in the war. A second destroyer was damaged. The remaining four Wildcats attacked the invasion fleet, sinking another ship. The Japanese withdrew.

It was the first time in the war that an amphibious assault had been repulsed. The news electrified America, still reeling from Pearl Harbor. Newspapers hailed the defenders of Wake as heroes.

## The Fall

The Japanese returned on December 23 with a much larger force, including two aircraft carriers diverted from the Pearl Harbor strike force. This time, the outcome was inevitable. After fierce fighting, with ammunition nearly exhausted and reinforcements that had been dispatched from Hawaii recalled (a decision that remains controversial), Commander Winfield Cunningham ordered the garrison to surrender.

The capture of Wake was a propaganda victory for Japan but a moral victory for the United States. The defenders had held out for sixteen days against overwhelming odds, inflicting significant casualties and proving that American forces could fight.

## The Prisoners

The fate of Wake Island's defenders was grim. Most of the military and civilian prisoners were shipped to prisoner-of-war camps in China and Japan, where they endured years of forced labor, starvation, and brutality.

Ninety-eight civilian workers were kept on Wake by the Japanese to perform forced labor. On October 7, 1943, Rear Admiral Shigematsu Sakaibara ordered all 98 executed. They were blindfolded, bound, and machine-gunned. One prisoner, whose identity is unknown, escaped briefly and carved the inscription "98 US PW 5-10-43" on a coral rock before being recaptured and beheaded. Sakaibara was convicted of war crimes and executed in 1947.

## The Forgotten Territory

After the war, Wake Island returned to U.S. control. It served as a military airfield and refueling stop during the Korean and Vietnam Wars. Today it is administered by the U.S. Air Force and has no permanent civilian population. It remains an unincorporated territory of the United States — one of the many remote Pacific outposts that together form the invisible infrastructure of American global power.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The defenders of Wake Island fought not because they could win but because resistance itself had value. Their sixteen-day stand gave a traumatized nation proof that courage was still possible even in defeat. Sometimes the most important battles are the ones you cannot win. Fighting for what matters — even when the outcome is certain — sends a message that outlasts the fight itself.
