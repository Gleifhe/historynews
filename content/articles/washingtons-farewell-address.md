On September 19, 1796, the *American Daily Advertiser* in Philadelphia published a lengthy letter from President George Washington to "Friends and Fellow Citizens." It was not a speech — Washington never delivered it orally. It was a written address, carefully composed over months with the help of Alexander Hamilton and James Madison, announcing that Washington would not seek a third term as president.

The announcement itself was historic. No one had doubted that Washington could win reelection. He was the most popular man in America, the hero of the Revolution, the man who had held the fragile republic together through its first eight years. By stepping down voluntarily, he established the precedent of peaceful transfer of power that would define American democracy for centuries.

## The Warnings

But Washington did not merely announce his retirement. He used the address to deliver a set of warnings to the nation he had helped create — warnings that read, over two centuries later, with unsettling prescience.

**On political parties**: Washington was deeply troubled by the growing divide between Federalists and Democratic-Republicans. He warned that "the spirit of party" was "the worst enemy" of democratic government. Parties, he argued, would elevate faction over country, allow "cunning, ambitious, and unprincipled men" to "subvert the power of the people," and create "a frightful despotism" as one party used the other's excesses to justify seizing power.

**On national unity**: Washington urged Americans to think of themselves as one people, not as Northerners or Southerners, Easterners or Westerners. He warned that regional jealousies and sectional interests could tear the country apart. "The name of American, which belongs to you in your national capacity, must always exalt the just pride of Patriotism," he wrote.

**On foreign entanglements**: Washington cautioned against permanent alliances with foreign nations. He did not oppose trade or temporary alliances when necessary, but he warned that "passionate attachment" to one country or "habitual hatred" of another would distort American policy and drag the nation into conflicts that served no American interest.

**On public debt**: Washington warned against accumulating national debt, arguing that one generation should not burden the next with its spending.

## Why He Stepped Down

Washington's decision to retire was not inevitable. The Constitution did not limit presidential terms — that would not happen until the 22nd Amendment in 1951. Many Americans expected Washington to serve for life. King George III reportedly said that if Washington voluntarily relinquished power, he would be "the greatest man in the world."

Washington was tired. Eight years of the presidency had taken their toll on a man who had already spent eight years commanding the Continental Army. He was 64 years old, in declining health, and eager to return to his plantation at Mount Vernon. But his decision was also principled. He believed that a republic should not depend on any one person, no matter how capable or popular. The system had to be bigger than any individual.

## The Legacy

The Farewell Address was read aloud in the United States Senate every year on Washington's birthday — a tradition that began in 1862 during the Civil War, when the nation needed to be reminded of its founding ideals. The tradition continues today.

Washington's two-term precedent held until 1940, when Franklin Roosevelt ran for and won a third term. The 22nd Amendment, ratified in 1951, formally limited presidents to two terms, codifying what Washington had established by example.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Washington's greatest act of leadership was letting go. He could have held power indefinitely — no one would have stopped him. Instead, he chose to demonstrate that the republic was more important than any one leader. That is the hardest lesson of leadership: knowing when to step aside so that what you built can outlast you. The measure of your work is not whether it needs you forever, but whether it thrives without you.
