Sometime in late 1971, a quiet engineer named Ray Tomlinson sat down at a computer terminal in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and did something no one had ever done before. He sent a message from one computer to another computer sitting right next to it — and in doing so, he invented email.

The message itself was completely forgettable. Tomlinson later said he could not remember what it said. It was probably something like "QWERTYUIOP" — just a quick test to see if the system worked. He had no idea that this simple experiment would transform how billions of people communicate.

Tomlinson was working for Bolt, Beranek and Newman, a company that helped build ARPANET — the early military and research network that would eventually become the internet. At the time, people could send messages to others on the same computer, but there was no way to send a message from one machine to another. Tomlinson wrote a small program that made it possible to send text between two different computers on the network.

He needed a way to separate the user's name from the computer's name in the address. He looked at his keyboard and chose the @ symbol. It was already on the keyboard but was rarely used in computing. It made perfect sense: the user was "at" a particular computer. So the first email address format was born: user@computer. That same format is still used by every email address in the world more than 50 years later.

When Tomlinson showed his invention to a colleague, he reportedly said, "Don't tell anyone! This isn't what we're supposed to be working on." He knew the invention was clever, but he had no idea it would change the world. At the time, only a few dozen computers were connected to ARPANET, and the idea of ordinary people using computers to send personal messages seemed far-fetched.

But email spread quickly among the small community of ARPANET users. Researchers loved it because it was faster than mail and less intrusive than a phone call. By the mid-1970s, email had become the most popular use of ARPANET — something its creators had never planned. The network had been designed for sharing computing resources, but people were mostly using it to talk to each other.

Over the following decades, email grew along with the internet. In the 1980s, it spread to universities and large companies. In the 1990s, services like AOL, Hotmail, and Yahoo Mail brought email to everyday people. By the early 2000s, email had become one of the most essential tools in business and personal life.

Today, more than 350 billion emails are sent every day worldwide. Email has transformed business, politics, education, and personal relationships. It made possible the kind of instant global communication that previous generations could only dream about. And it paved the way for text messaging, social media, and all the other digital communication tools we use today.

The [invention of the World Wide Web](/articles/invention-of-world-wide-web/) two decades later would build on the same network foundations that made email possible. And when the [events of September 11, 2001](/articles/internet-on-911/) shocked the world, email and the internet became crucial tools for communication, information sharing, and human connection during a time of crisis.

Ray Tomlinson remained modest about his achievement throughout his life. He rarely gave interviews and never sought fame or fortune from his invention. He continued working as an engineer until his death in 2016 at age 74. When asked about inventing email, he would shrug and say that he just saw a problem and found a simple solution.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Ray Tomlinson's story teaches us that sometimes the biggest breakthroughs come from solving small, everyday problems. He did not set out to revolutionize communication. He just wanted to find a way to send a message from one computer to another. But by solving that small problem, he opened a door that changed everything.

This reminds us that we do not always need grand plans to make a difference. Sometimes the most impactful things we do start as simple experiments, side projects, or solutions to problems that just bother us. The key is to be curious and willing to try things, even when the outcome is uncertain.

Tomlinson's humility is also inspiring. He could have spent the rest of his life boasting about inventing email, but instead he kept working and kept contributing. His example reminds us that our best work often comes when we focus on the work itself rather than on the recognition we might receive.

Finally, his story shows us the power of building things that connect people. Email's greatest impact was not technical — it was human. It brought people closer together, made it easier to share ideas, and broke down barriers of distance and time. When we create things that help people communicate and connect, we are contributing to something much bigger than ourselves.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, email remains one of the most widely used communication tools in the world, even as newer technologies like messaging apps, video calls, and social media have joined it. The @ symbol that Tomlinson chose more than 50 years ago is now one of the most recognized characters on the planet, used not just in email but in social media handles and other digital contexts.

Tomlinson's invention also set the stage for the digital communication revolution that defines our era. Every text message, every social media post, every video call traces its roots back to that forgotten test message sent between two computers in a Cambridge lab.

In an age when we sometimes feel overwhelmed by digital communication — too many emails, too many messages, too many notifications — it is worth remembering the original promise of email. It was supposed to make it easier for people to connect and share ideas. When we use our communication tools thoughtfully and with purpose, we honor the simple, powerful idea that Ray Tomlinson brought to life in 1971.
