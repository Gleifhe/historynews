In the summer of 1940, as Nazi Germany swept through Western Europe, the United States began fortifying its Caribbean defenses. Puerto Rico — acquired from Spain in 1898 during the [Spanish-American War](/articles/spanish-american-war-1898/) — became the linchpin of that effort. The island sat at the entrance to the Caribbean, guarding the approaches to the Panama Canal. Within months, military construction transformed Puerto Rico into one of the most heavily fortified American territories in the world.

## The Fortress

The buildup was massive. Roosevelt Roads Naval Station expanded to become one of the largest naval facilities in the world. Ramey Air Force Base in Aguadilla hosted long-range patrol aircraft. Fort Buchanan near San Juan became a major Army installation. Camp Tortuguero served as a training center. Vieques and Culebra, small islands off Puerto Rico's eastern coast, became live-fire bombing and naval gunnery ranges.

The military presence transformed the island's economy. Thousands of Puerto Ricans found employment building and servicing military installations. The sugar-based economy shifted toward manufacturing and services oriented around military demand.

## The Borinqueneers

Puerto Ricans had been U.S. citizens since the Jones Act of 1917 — passed, critics noted, just in time to make them eligible for the World War I draft. During World War II, over 65,000 Puerto Ricans served in the armed forces.

The most famous Puerto Rican unit was the 65th Infantry Regiment, known as the Borinqueneers (from Borinquen, the indigenous Taino name for Puerto Rico). The all-Puerto Rican regiment served in North Africa, Italy, France, and Germany. Despite being segregated from other units and facing discrimination, the Borinqueneers earned a distinguished combat record.

Puerto Rican women also served, including as Army nurses. Puerto Rican civilians contributed to war bond drives, civil defense programs, and agricultural production for the war effort.

## The Contradiction

The paradox of Puerto Rico's wartime service was impossible to ignore. Puerto Ricans fought and died for a nation that defined its war aims as defending democracy and self-determination against tyranny. The Atlantic Charter, signed by Roosevelt and Churchill in 1941, declared the right of all peoples to "choose the form of government under which they will live." Yet Puerto Ricans could not choose their own governor — he was appointed by the president — and had no vote in the government that sent them to war.

The contradiction fueled demands for reform. In 1947, Congress passed the Elective Governor Act, allowing Puerto Ricans to elect their own governor for the first time. Luis Muñoz Marín won the first election in 1948. In 1952, Puerto Rico adopted its own constitution and became a "commonwealth" — a unique status that provided self-government in local affairs while maintaining U.S. sovereignty.

## Vieques

The military legacy left scars. Vieques, a small island used as a bombing range from 1941 to 2003, became a symbol of the costs of military presence. For six decades, the U.S. Navy conducted bombing practice, ship-to-shore shelling, and weapons testing on Vieques. Residents reported elevated rates of cancer, respiratory illness, and other health problems, which they attributed to contamination from munitions and depleted uranium.

After decades of protest — including civil disobedience that drew international attention — the Navy ceased operations on Vieques in 2003. Cleanup of unexploded ordnance and contamination continues as of 2026.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Puerto Rico's wartime experience exposes the gap between contribution and representation. The people of the island gave their blood, labor, and land to a cause they believed in — and received less than full citizenship in return. In any organization or relationship, if you expect people to contribute, you owe them a voice in decisions that affect them. Loyalty without reciprocity is not partnership; it is extraction.
