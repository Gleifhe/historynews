On August 3, 1949, President Harry Truman signed a bill that many Americans assumed had been law for generations: the official designation of June 14 as National Flag Day. The date commemorated the Continental Congress's adoption of the Stars and Stripes on June 14, 1777. But getting that date recognized had taken one determined schoolteacher over 30 years of tireless campaigning.

## The Schoolteacher's Crusade

Bernard J. Cigrand was a 19-year-old schoolteacher in Waubeka, Wisconsin, when he placed a small flag on his desk on June 14, 1885, and asked his students to write essays about what the flag meant to them. That day became his personal Flag Day, and he spent the rest of his life trying to make it the nation's.

Cigrand wrote articles, gave speeches, and lobbied politicians relentlessly. He is said to have delivered over 2,188 speeches on patriotism and the flag over the course of his career. He became a dentist and later a professor, but the flag remained his cause.

Cigrand was not alone. Other advocates pushed for official recognition throughout the late 1800s and early 1900s. In 1916, President Woodrow Wilson issued a proclamation establishing June 14 as Flag Day, but it was not backed by an act of Congress and had no legal force.

## The Long Road to Law

For decades, Flag Day existed in a gray area — widely observed but not officially established by Congress. Various states passed their own Flag Day laws. Pennsylvania became the first state to make Flag Day a legal holiday in 1937. But a national law remained elusive.

The push for federal legislation gained momentum after World War II. The war had strengthened Americans' attachment to national symbols, and the flag had taken on renewed emotional significance. Millions of families had displayed service stars in their windows — blue for a loved one serving, gold for one who had died. The flag had draped countless coffins.

When Congress finally passed the National Flag Day Act and President Truman signed it on August 3, 1949, it designated June 14 as National Flag Day but did not make it a federal holiday. Government offices remained open. Mail was still delivered. Workers did not get the day off. It was a day of observance, not of rest — a distinction that stands to this day.

## Flag Day Today

Flag Day remains one of the quieter observances on the American calendar. It does not feature the barbecues of the Fourth of July or the solemnity of Memorial Day. But in communities across the country, Flag Day ceremonies mark the anniversary of the nation's most recognizable symbol.

The city of Fairfield, Washington, claims to be the oldest continuous Flag Day celebration in the country, dating to 1909 or 1910. Troy, New York, holds an annual Flag Day parade. And in Waubeka, Wisconsin — where Bernard Cigrand placed that small flag on his desk in 1885 — the schoolhouse is now a landmark on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Why Not a Federal Holiday?

Congress deliberately chose to make Flag Day a national observance rather than a full federal holiday. The distinction matters. Federal holidays like Independence Day and Memorial Day close government offices, halt mail delivery, and give federal employees a paid day off. Flag Day does none of these things. Several attempts have been made over the decades to elevate it to full holiday status, but each has stalled. Opponents argue that adding another federal holiday would cost the government billions in lost productivity and paid leave. Supporters counter that the flag deserves the same recognition as other national symbols honored on full holidays. Pennsylvania remains the only state that treats Flag Day as a state holiday, closing state offices and giving employees the day off. For the rest of the country, June 14 passes as a working day — a date marked by proclamations and ceremonies, but not by the weight of law that a true holiday carries.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Bernard Cigrand did not have wealth, political connections, or a platform. He had a desk, a small flag, and an unwavering belief that something mattered. It took him over 30 years and thousands of speeches, but he changed a nation's calendar. If you believe something deserves recognition — a person, an idea, a cause — do not wait for someone else to champion it. Start where you are, with what you have.
