In a climate-controlled case at the Bennington Museum in Vermont sits one of the most remarkable artifacts of the American Revolution: a faded, fragile flag with thirteen red and white stripes, thirteen seven-pointed stars arranged in an arch, and the large numeral "76" in its blue canton. The Bennington Flag is believed to be one of the oldest Stars and Stripes in existence — a tangible link to the chaotic first years of the republic.

## The Battle of Bennington

On August 16, 1777, a force of approximately 2,000 American militia under General John Stark clashed with a detachment of British and Hessian troops near Bennington, Vermont (the battle actually took place in what is now Walloomsac, New York, just across the border). The British, under Lieutenant Colonel Friedrich Baum, had been sent by General John Burgoyne to seize supplies stockpiled by the Americans.

Stark's militia overwhelmed the British force in a decisive battle that lasted less than two hours. The Americans killed or captured nearly the entire enemy detachment. The victory was a critical blow to Burgoyne's campaign, which would end two months later with the British surrender at Saratoga — the turning point of the war.

According to tradition, the Bennington Flag was carried during this battle by Nathaniel Fillmore, the grandfather of future President Millard Fillmore.

## A Unique Design

The Bennington Flag is distinctive in several ways. Its thirteen stars have seven points rather than the more common five or six. The stars are arranged in an arch pattern — an inverted U shape — with an additional star at the top. The "76" in the canton is unique among surviving Revolutionary-era flags, a direct reference to the year of independence.

The flag's stripes also begin with white rather than red, which differs from the modern flag. These variations reflect the reality of early flag-making: the [Flag Resolution of 1777](/articles/flag-resolution-1777/) specified stripes and stars but left nearly every design detail to individual interpretation.

## Questions and Authenticity

Historians debate the flag's precise age and origin. Some scholars believe it dates to the 1777 battle; others argue it may have been made later, possibly for a commemoration rather than for combat. The flag's relatively good condition (for a 200-plus-year-old textile) has fueled some skepticism. Textile analysis and paint studies have been inconclusive.

Regardless of whether the flag was at Bennington or was made shortly after, it is among the oldest surviving American flags and provides invaluable evidence of how early Americans interpreted the Flag Resolution.

## Preservation and Legacy

The Bennington Flag passed through the Fillmore family for generations before being donated to the Bennington Museum in 1926. By then, the flag was severely deteriorated — its fabric brittle, its colors faded by more than a century of exposure. In the 1960s, textile conservators at the museum undertook a painstaking restoration, stabilizing the flag's fragile linen and cotton fibers with a silk net backing. The flag has not been unfolded since. Today it is displayed flat under carefully controlled lighting to prevent further light damage. The museum maintains temperature and humidity levels within narrow tolerances, treating the artifact as both a historical document and a scientific specimen. In 2005, the flag was designated a National Treasure by the National Trust for Historic Preservation, placing it alongside the Declaration of Independence and the Star-Spangled Banner as one of America's most significant surviving artifacts.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Bennington Flag is a reminder that great things often start rough. The early Americans did not have a graphic design team or a branding guide. They had a one-sentence resolution and a cause worth fighting for, so they made what they could with what they had. The result was imperfect, improvised, and enduring. Do not wait for perfection to begin. Start with what you have, and let time refine it.
