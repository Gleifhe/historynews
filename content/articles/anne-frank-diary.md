## A Birthday Gift That Changed History

On June 12, 1942, a thirteen-year-old girl in Amsterdam received a red-and-white checkered diary for her birthday. She named it "Kitty" and began writing in it immediately. That girl was Anne Frank, and her diary would become one of the most widely read books in history.

Anne was born in Frankfurt, Germany, in 1929 to a Jewish family. When the Nazis came to power in 1933, the Franks moved to Amsterdam, hoping the Netherlands would be safe. For several years, life was good. Anne went to school, made friends, and rode her bicycle through the city's streets.

But on May 10, 1940, Germany invaded the Netherlands. Within days, the country surrendered. The Nazis began imposing anti-Jewish laws — Jews could not go to cinemas, ride bicycles, or attend public schools. They were forced to wear yellow stars on their clothing. The freedoms Anne had known were stripped away one by one.

## Into Hiding

On July 6, 1942, the Frank family went into hiding. Anne's father, Otto Frank, had prepared a secret annex behind his office building at Prinsengracht 263 in Amsterdam. The entrance was concealed behind a movable bookcase.

Eight people lived in the annex: Anne, her parents Otto and Edith, her older sister Margot, and four others — Hermann and Auguste van Pels, their son Peter, and Fritz Pfeffer. They relied on a small group of Otto's employees to bring them food, news, and supplies.

The rules were strict. During business hours, they could not make any noise. No flushing toilets. No walking in shoes. No speaking above a whisper. The windows were permanently covered. For 761 days, these eight people lived in a space roughly the size of a small apartment, never stepping outside.

## What Anne Wrote

Anne wrote about everything. She described the daily routines, the arguments between the families, and her complicated relationship with her mother. She wrote about her fears — the sound of Allied bombers overhead, the fear of discovery, the worry about friends who had been deported.

But she also wrote about hope. On July 15, 1944, just weeks before the annex was discovered, she wrote one of her most famous entries: "In spite of everything, I still believe that people are really good at heart."

Anne also wrote about wanting to be a writer and a journalist after the war. She heard a radio broadcast from the Dutch government in exile, asking people to save their diaries and letters as records of life during the occupation. Anne began revising her diary, imagining it as a book she would publish someday.

She was not just recording events. She was creating literature.

## Discovery and Tragedy

On August 4, 1944, the Gestapo raided the secret annex. Someone had betrayed the families — to this day, the identity of the informer remains debated. All eight people in hiding were arrested and sent to concentration camps.

Anne and Margot were sent to Bergen-Belsen, where both died of typhus in February or March 1945 — just weeks before British soldiers liberated the camp. Of the eight people who hid in the annex, only Otto Frank survived. He was liberated from Auschwitz by Soviet troops — a scene echoed in the [liberation of that camp](/articles/the-holocaust/) and later commemorated at the [Nuremberg Trials](/articles/nuremberg-trials/) that sought justice for millions.

When Otto returned to Amsterdam, Miep Gies, one of the helpers who had protected the family, gave him Anne's diary. She had found it scattered on the floor of the annex after the arrest and had kept it safe, unread, hoping to return it to Anne herself.

## The Diary's Journey

Otto Frank read his daughter's words and was deeply moved. He decided to publish the diary, fulfilling Anne's dream of becoming a published writer. It was first printed in Dutch in 1947 under the title *Het Achterhuis* (The Secret Annex). English and other translations followed.

Today, the diary has been translated into more than 70 languages and has sold over 30 million copies worldwide. The secret annex at Prinsengracht 263 is now a museum — the Anne Frank House — visited by over a million people each year.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Anne Frank's diary teaches us that your voice matters, even when the world tries to silence you. Anne was a teenager with no power, no freedom, and no guarantee of survival. Yet she chose to write. She chose to think deeply, to question, to dream. That decision made her one of the most influential voices of the 20th century.

There is also a lesson about resilience. Anne faced unimaginable circumstances, yet she continued to find moments of beauty — a patch of sky through the attic window, the sound of church bells, the first buds of spring on a chestnut tree she could see from the annex. Finding small joys in difficult times is not naive. It is an act of resistance against despair.

Finally, Anne's story reminds us of the importance of empathy. She wrote about the people around her with honesty — sometimes sharply, sometimes tenderly. She tried to understand others, even when she disagreed with them. That habit of looking at the world through other people's eyes is one of the most important skills any person can develop.

## How This Connects to 2026

Anne Frank's diary remains urgently relevant. In 2026, antisemitism is rising in many countries. Hate crimes against Jewish communities have increased across Europe and North America. Holocaust denial and distortion continue to spread on social media, where algorithms can amplify conspiracy theories faster than educators can counter them.

At the same time, millions of people around the world are living as refugees — displaced by war, persecution, and political violence, just as the Frank family was. The questions Anne faced — Where is it safe? Who can we trust? Will anyone help us? — are being asked by families today in Syria, Ukraine, Sudan, and beyond.

Anne's diary is also a reminder of the power of personal testimony in an age of misinformation. When facts are disputed and history is rewritten, first-person accounts like Anne's are irreplaceable. They ground us in reality. They make the abstract concrete. And they make it much harder to look away.
