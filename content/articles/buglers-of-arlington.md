## Twenty-Four Notes

It takes roughly sixty seconds to play Taps. Twenty-four notes, unaccompanied, on a single bugle. No harmony, no percussion, no accompaniment of any kind. Just one soldier, one instrument, and a melody so spare and so familiar that it has become the sound of American grief.

At Arlington National Cemetery, that sound is heard approximately thirty times every weekday. Each funeral service conducted at the cemetery — and Arlington averages between 27 and 30 per day — concludes with a live bugler playing Taps. The bugler stands at a respectful distance from the gravesite, raises the instrument, and sends those twenty-four notes across the headstones. Then the bugler lowers the instrument, and the ceremony is over.

The soldiers who perform this duty are members of the 3rd U.S. Infantry Regiment, known as "The Old Guard" — the oldest active-duty infantry unit in the United States Army, tracing its lineage to 1784. Based at Fort Myer, Virginia, adjacent to Arlington National Cemetery, The Old Guard is the Army's official ceremonial unit, responsible for funeral honors at Arlington, the guarding of the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, and presidential escort duties.

## Historical Context: The Old Guard and Arlington

The relationship between The Old Guard and Arlington National Cemetery is inseparable from the history of the cemetery itself. Arlington was established as a military cemetery during the Civil War, and soldiers have been providing funeral honors there since the first burials in 1864. But the formalization of the relationship — the assignment of a dedicated infantry regiment to perform ceremonial duties at the cemetery full-time — came in April 1948, when the 3rd Infantry was officially designated as the Army's ceremonial unit and stationed at Fort Myer.

Since then, The Old Guard has conducted hundreds of thousands of funeral services. The regiment's soldiers perform the full military funeral honors specified by regulation: the horse-drawn caisson that carries the casket, the escort platoon that marches alongside, the firing party that fires three rifle volleys, the flag-folding detail that presents the burial flag to the next of kin, and the bugler who plays Taps.

The [origins of Taps itself](/articles/taps-bugle-call/) — composed during the Civil War and adopted as the military's official lights-out and funeral call — are intertwined with the history of Arlington. The same bugle call that once told soldiers in camp to extinguish their lights and go to sleep has become the nation's farewell to its fallen.

## The Event: A Day in the Life of an Arlington Bugler

The buglers who play at Arlington funerals are members of The Old Guard's ceremonial companies, and many are assigned to the unit specifically for their musical ability. Each bugler may play at multiple funerals in a single day, moving from one service to the next on a schedule that is meticulously coordinated by the cemetery's scheduling office.

A typical day begins before dawn. The bugler checks the schedule, inspects the uniform — The Old Guard's dress blue uniform is held to exacting standards, with every button, crease, and insignia positioned precisely — and reports for the first service. At the gravesite, the bugler takes a position approximately 50 to 100 feet from the mourners, usually behind a tree or headstone, partially concealed so that the sound of the bugle seems to come from the cemetery itself rather than from a specific person.

When the moment comes — after the chaplain's benediction, after the rifle volleys, after the flag is folded into a tight triangle and presented to the family — the bugler raises the instrument and plays. The twenty-four notes of Taps float across the rows of headstones. There is no sheet music. Every Arlington bugler has the melody committed to memory, and each one plays it with slight personal variations in tempo and phrasing that make every performance unique.

Between services, the bugler may have fifteen minutes or two hours. Some days are heavier than others. Around Memorial Day and Veterans Day, the schedule intensifies. During periods of active combat operations, the number of funerals increases. The buglers play regardless of weather — in summer heat that makes the brass instrument too hot to hold comfortably, in winter cold that numbs the lips and makes the notes harder to form, in rain that soaks the uniform and fogs the instrument.

There is no day off from this duty. Funerals are conducted at Arlington on Saturdays and, when needed, on Sundays and holidays. The dead do not wait for convenient scheduling.

## Aftermath: The Human Cost of Constant Mourning

The emotional toll of playing Taps thirty times a day, week after week, is something that the soldiers of The Old Guard rarely discuss publicly but acknowledge privately. Each service is someone's worst day. The bugler sees the folded flag pressed into the hands of a widow, a mother, a child. The bugler hears the grief. And then the bugler moves to the next service and does it again.

The Old Guard takes this responsibility with extraordinary seriousness. Soldiers assigned to the regiment understand that they are not performing for an audience — they are providing a sacred service to families in the worst moment of their lives. The regiment's unofficial motto, "Soldiers never die until they are forgotten," captures the philosophy: every funeral, no matter how routine it might appear from the outside, is treated as if it were the only one.

The standards are absolute. An Old Guard soldier whose uniform is not perfect does not perform. A bugler who cannot play Taps flawlessly does not play. The regiment conducts regular training and inspections to ensure that every ceremony meets the same standard, whether the deceased is a four-star general or a private first class. At [Arlington National Cemetery](/articles/arlington-national-cemetery/), rank distinctions that mattered in life are honored but do not determine the quality of the farewell.

Many Old Guard veterans describe their time at Arlington as the most meaningful service of their careers. They may have deployed to combat zones and received decorations for valor, but it is the quiet duty at the cemetery — the daily act of honoring the dead — that stays with them longest.

## Personal Growth: The Discipline of Showing Up

The buglers of Arlington teach a lesson that has nothing to do with music and everything to do with character: the most important things in life are the ones you do every day, whether anyone is watching or not.

Playing Taps once at a high-profile ceremony is a memorable experience. Playing it thirty times a day, five or six days a week, month after month, requires something entirely different. It requires the discipline to treat the thirtieth performance with the same care and reverence as the first. It requires the humility to understand that the ceremony is not about the performer — it is about the person being laid to rest and the family left behind.

This principle — that excellence is not an event but a habit — applies to every area of life. The student who studies diligently every day, not just before exams, learns more deeply. The employee who brings full effort to routine tasks, not just to high-visibility projects, earns genuine respect. The friend who shows up consistently, not just during crises, builds lasting relationships.

The Old Guard soldiers also model something important about grief: that it deserves to be met with dignity and presence. In a culture that often rushes past loss — that expects people to "move on" and "stay positive" — the buglers of Arlington insist on pausing. For sixty seconds, everything stops. The notes hang in the air. The loss is acknowledged. And then, only then, does the world resume.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, The Old Guard continues its mission at Arlington with the same intensity it has maintained for more than seven decades. The regiment's soldiers still fold flags with geometric precision, still fire rifle volleys with synchronized exactness, and still play Taps with the clarity and solemnity that the moment demands.

The demands on the regiment have evolved. Arlington National Cemetery is approaching capacity — a challenge that has prompted ongoing discussions about eligibility criteria and potential expansion. As the cemetery adapts to serve future generations, The Old Guard will adapt with it, maintaining the ceremonial standards regardless of how the institution changes around them.

The regiment has also expanded its role in public engagement, participating in educational programs and community events that help Americans understand the significance of military funeral honors. In an era when fewer Americans have a personal connection to the military, The Old Guard serves as a visible reminder that the nation's obligation to its fallen does not expire.

Every day at Arlington, a bugler raises an instrument and plays twenty-four notes. It is the same melody that has been played at military funerals since the Civil War, and it will be played tomorrow, and the day after that, for as long as the nation chooses to honor its dead. The buglers of The Old Guard do not play for applause or recognition. They play because someone who served this country has come to their final rest, and that moment — every single time — deserves to be marked with beauty, precision, and respect.
