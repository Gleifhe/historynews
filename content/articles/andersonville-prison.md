## A Name That Became a Curse

There were many terrible places during the Civil War. There were battlefields where thousands died in a single afternoon and hospitals where surgeons worked without anesthesia. But no name carried more horror than Andersonville. Officially called Camp Sumter, this Confederate prisoner-of-war camp in southwestern Georgia became the deadliest ground of the entire war — a place where approximately 13,000 Union soldiers died not from bullets but from starvation, disease, and deliberate neglect.

The men who survived Andersonville carried its scars for the rest of their lives. And the trial that followed became one of the first modern war crimes prosecutions — a grim precedent for the international tribunals that would come decades later.

## Why Andersonville Was Built

By early 1864, the Confederacy's prisoner-of-war system was collapsing. Earlier in the war, the Union and Confederacy had exchanged prisoners through a cartel system — captured soldiers were paroled or swapped for enemy prisoners of equal rank. But in 1863, the exchange system broke down, partly because the Confederacy refused to treat captured Black soldiers as prisoners of war, instead threatening to re-enslave or execute them.

Without exchanges, prison populations on both sides swelled. The Confederacy, already struggling to feed its own soldiers and civilians, decided to build a new, larger prison camp. They chose a site near the village of Andersonville in Sumter County, Georgia — far from the front lines and connected to the railroad.

Construction began in January 1864. The design was brutally simple: a rectangular stockade of pine logs, enclosing about 16.5 acres of open ground with a small stream running through it. There were no barracks, no tents, no shelter of any kind. A line of stakes called the "deadline" was set 19 feet inside the stockade wall. Any prisoner who crossed it would be shot.

## Life Inside the Stockade

The first prisoners arrived in February 1864. The camp, designed to hold a maximum of 10,000 men, received thousands more every month as the war ground toward the campaigns that would end at [Appomattox](/articles/fort-sumter-to-appomattox/). By August 1864, approximately 33,000 prisoners were crammed into the stockade — more than three times its intended capacity. The 16.5 acres had been expanded to 26.5 acres, but the overcrowding remained catastrophic.

Conditions were almost beyond description. Prisoners had no shelter from the broiling Georgia sun or the drenching rains. They dug holes in the red clay with their bare hands, draping scraps of cloth or blankets over sticks to create crude shelters called "shebangs." The stream that was supposed to provide fresh water quickly became an open sewer, contaminated by the cookhouse upstream and the latrines of tens of thousands of men. Prisoners drank from it anyway because there was nothing else.

Food rations were meager even by Confederate standards — and the Confederacy was starving. Prisoners received small amounts of raw cornmeal, sometimes with bugs ground into it, and occasionally a scrap of rancid meat. Many men received fewer than 300 calories per day. Scurvy, dysentery, diarrhea, and gangrene swept through the camp. The death rate reached approximately 100 men per day during the worst months of the summer of 1864.

Survivors described scenes of unimaginable suffering. Men became living skeletons, their ribs and joints protruding through skin stretched like paper. Some went mad. Others simply lay down and died. A prisoner named John Ransom, who kept a diary that survived the war, wrote: "There is so much filth about the camp that it is terrible trying to live here. New prisoners are shocked at the sights."

## The Commandant

The camp was commanded by Captain Henry Wirz, a Swiss-born Confederate officer. Wirz was widely despised by the prisoners, who blamed him personally for their suffering. Whether Wirz was a cruel sadist or simply an overwhelmed officer in an impossible situation remains debated by historians, though neither interpretation absolves him of responsibility for the conditions under his command.

Wirz restricted rations, ordered guards to shoot prisoners who crossed the deadline, and made little effort to improve sanitation or shelter. Some defenders have argued that the Confederacy simply lacked the resources to feed its prisoners when it couldn't feed its own army. Others point out that Wirz had opportunities to improve conditions — allowing prisoners to forage for wood, expanding the water supply, requesting medical supplies — and consistently failed to act.

## Liberation and Trial

In April 1865, the war ended. Union forces liberated Andersonville and were stunned by what they found. Photographs taken by Union army photographers showed emaciated men who looked more like concentration camp survivors than soldiers. These images, widely published in Northern newspapers, generated enormous public outrage.

Henry Wirz was arrested and brought to Washington, D.C., for trial by a military tribunal. The trial lasted from August to October 1865 and was one of the most closely watched legal proceedings in American history. Wirz was charged with conspiring with Confederate leaders to "impair and injure the health and destroy the lives" of Union prisoners, and with 13 specific counts of murder.

On November 10, 1865, Wirz was hanged at the Old Capitol Prison in Washington — the only person executed for war crimes committed during the Civil War. His trial established a precedent that would echo forward through history: that a military commander could be held personally responsible for the treatment of prisoners in his custody. That principle would be invoked again at the [Nuremberg Trials](/articles/nuremberg-trials/) after World War II, when Nazi leaders were prosecuted for crimes against humanity.

## Counting the Dead

The final toll at Andersonville was staggering. Of approximately 45,000 Union prisoners held at the camp between February 1864 and April 1865, roughly 13,000 died — a mortality rate of nearly 29 percent. They were buried in trenches, row after row, in what became Andersonville National Cemetery. A former prisoner named Dorence Atwater had secretly kept a copy of the death register, recording each man's name, rank, regiment, and date of death. After the war, Atwater worked with Clara Barton to identify and mark the graves, ensuring that the dead would not be forgotten.

Today, Andersonville National Historic Site preserves the grounds of the prison and the cemetery. The rows of white headstones stretch across a quiet Georgia hillside — 13,000 markers for 13,000 men who never came home.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Andersonville forces us to confront an uncomfortable truth: cruelty doesn't always come from villains twirling mustaches. It often comes from systems — from bureaucracies that fail, from leaders who look away, from ordinary people who convince themselves that someone else's suffering isn't their problem.

The lesson for personal growth is about moral courage. It's easy to be decent when conditions are good. The real test comes when resources are scarce, when you're under pressure, and when no one is watching. The guards at Andersonville weren't monsters before the war. They were farmers, clerks, and tradesmen. What they did — and what they failed to do — is a warning about what any of us might become if we stop seeing other people as fully human.

Andersonville also teaches the importance of bearing witness. Dorence Atwater risked his life to keep the death register because he believed the dead deserved to be remembered by name. John Ransom kept his diary because he believed the world needed to know what happened. Recording the truth, especially when it's painful, is an act of moral courage in itself.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the treatment of prisoners and detainees remains a live issue around the world. International humanitarian organizations continue to document abuses in detention facilities, refugee camps, and conflict zones. The Geneva Conventions — which established the modern rules for treatment of prisoners of war — exist in large part because of places like Andersonville.

The lesson of Andersonville is not just historical. It's a warning. When societies dehumanize their enemies, when leaders prioritize ideology over basic human decency, when systems break down and no one takes responsibility — the result is suffering on an almost incomprehensible scale. Remembering Andersonville means committing to the idea that even in war, even against enemies, there are lines that must never be crossed.
