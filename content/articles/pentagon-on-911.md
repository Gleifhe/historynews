## 9:37 AM

At 9:37 AM on the morning of September 11, 2001, American Airlines Flight 77 — a Boeing 757 carrying 59 passengers and crew and five hijackers — struck the western face of the Pentagon at approximately 530 miles per hour. The aircraft had been hijacked shortly after taking off from Washington Dulles International Airport, bound for Los Angeles. The hijackers, armed with box cutters and knives, had commandeered the cockpit, turned the plane around over Ohio, and aimed it directly at the headquarters of the United States Department of Defense.

The impact was catastrophic. The plane penetrated three of the Pentagon's five concentric rings, creating a path of destruction approximately 310 feet deep into the building. A massive fireball erupted, fueled by thousands of gallons of jet fuel. The structural damage caused a section of the building's outer wall to collapse approximately 20 minutes after impact. One hundred and twenty-five people inside the Pentagon were killed. All 59 passengers and crew members on the plane perished. Combined with the five hijackers, the total death toll at the Pentagon was 189 people, though the attack's victims are typically counted as the 184 innocent lives lost.

## Historical Context

September 11, 2001, is the deadliest day of terrorism in American history. That morning, 19 hijackers affiliated with the terrorist organization al-Qaeda carried out a coordinated attack using four commercial aircraft. Two planes struck the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center in New York City, causing both to collapse and killing approximately 2,750 people. A fourth plane, United Airlines Flight 93, crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after passengers attempted to retake the cockpit, killing all 40 passengers and crew on board.

The attack on the Pentagon was the third strike of the morning. It came 51 minutes after the first plane hit the North Tower in New York and 34 minutes after the second plane hit the South Tower. By the time Flight 77 reached Washington, America already knew it was under attack. Fighter jets had been scrambled, but miscommunication and confusion meant they were not in position to intercept the plane. The hijackers exploited a window of vulnerability that closed within hours but that, on that morning, was wide open.

The Pentagon — the largest office building in the world, with approximately 23,000 military and civilian employees — had never been attacked. Built during World War II and completed in 1943, it was the symbolic and operational center of American military power. Striking it was a deliberate choice by al-Qaeda: an attack not just on people, but on the idea of American invulnerability.

## The Attack

Flight 77 approached the Pentagon from the southwest, descending rapidly and leveling off just above the ground in the final seconds. The plane clipped several light poles on Washington Boulevard before slamming into the first floor of the building's western wall. The area struck was Wedge 1, which had recently undergone a renovation that included blast-resistant windows, reinforced columns, and a Kevlar-lined interior. These upgrades, which had been designed to protect against a truck bomb, saved an unknown number of lives by slowing the spread of the damage and preventing an even larger collapse.

Inside the building, the destruction was immediate and total in the impact zone. Offices were obliterated. Fires raged. Survivors described being thrown across rooms, blinded by smoke, and crawling through debris to find exits. Many of those killed were Army and Navy personnel working in the offices that took the direct hit, including several who had just arrived for the workday.

The response was immediate. Military personnel and civilians in the unaffected parts of the building rushed toward the damage, pulling survivors from the wreckage. The Pentagon's own fire department was on the scene within minutes, joined quickly by Arlington County firefighters and rescue teams from across the region. Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, whose office was on the opposite side of the building, went to the crash site and helped carry stretchers before being urged to return to the command center.

The outer wall of the Pentagon collapsed at approximately 10:10 AM, more than 30 minutes after impact. Remarkably, rescue workers had already cleared many survivors from the area. The collapse killed no additional people on the ground, though it complicated rescue and recovery efforts for days afterward.

## The Victims

The 125 Pentagon victims ranged in age from 23 to 71. They included generals and privates, career civil servants and contract workers, men and women from every branch of the military. Among the dead were Lieutenant General Timothy Maude, the highest-ranking military officer killed in the attack and the highest-ranking soldier killed by a foreign attack on American soil since the Civil War. Navy electronics technician Ronald Hemenway, Army specialist Craig Amundson, and civilian accountant Rhonda Rasmussen were among those who perished — ordinary people doing ordinary work on what had started as an ordinary Tuesday morning.

On the plane, the passengers and crew included a three-year-old girl, Dana Falkenberg, and her eight-year-old sister, Zoe, traveling with their parents. Asia Cotton, age 11, was on the flight with her teacher and classmates. Their deaths — along with those of all the other innocent people on Flight 77 — remain among the most painful dimensions of the September 11 attacks.

## Aftermath

The Pentagon was repaired and the damaged section rebuilt within a year — a deliberate demonstration of resilience. The reconstruction project, known as the Phoenix Project, was completed on September 11, 2002, exactly one year after the attack. The rebuilt section included a small chapel at the point of impact.

In 2008, the Pentagon Memorial was dedicated on the grounds outside the building. It consists of 184 illuminated benches, one for each victim, arranged by age from the youngest (three-year-old Dana Falkenberg) to the oldest. Each bench is positioned over a small reflecting pool and is oriented to indicate whether the victim was in the building or on the plane. The memorial is open to the public 24 hours a day, every day of the year.

The Pentagon attack, along with the strikes on the World Trade Center, triggered the most significant shift in American national security policy since [Pearl Harbor](/articles/pearl-harbor-attack/). The United States launched the War on Terror, invaded Afghanistan within weeks, and created the Department of Homeland Security. The Authorization for Use of Military Force, passed by Congress on September 14, 2001, remains the legal basis for American counterterrorism operations in multiple countries more than two decades later.

Yet among the three attack sites, the Pentagon is often the least discussed. The sheer scale of the World Trade Center's collapse and the heroism of Flight 93's passengers have, understandably, dominated the public narrative. But the attack on the Pentagon killed 184 people and struck at the command center of the world's most powerful military. It deserves to be remembered with equal gravity.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of the Pentagon on 9/11 is, in part, a story about what people do in the moments after disaster. The soldiers and civilians inside the building did not wait for instructions. They ran toward the fire, pulled colleagues from burning offices, and carried the wounded to safety. Many of those who survived did so because a coworker refused to leave them behind. The renovation upgrades that limited the damage were the result of years of planning by people who prepared for a threat they hoped would never come.

Both responses — the instinct to help and the discipline to prepare — are qualities worth developing. You do not need to be in a crisis to practice them. Helping someone who is struggling, learning first aid, thinking about emergency plans — these small actions build the kind of character that matters when everything falls apart.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Pentagon Memorial continues to welcome visitors from around the world. The September 11 attacks remain the defining event of 21st-century American history, shaping foreign policy, civil liberties, and national identity in ways that are still unfolding. The [Beirut barracks bombing of 1983](/articles/beirut-barracks-bombing/) had warned that America's enemies would strike at symbols of military power. September 11 proved that no symbol was too large and no target was beyond reach. Twenty-five years later, the 184 benches outside the Pentagon — each one lit from below, each one bearing a name — stand as both a memorial and a challenge: to remember what was lost, and to build a world where it cannot happen again.
