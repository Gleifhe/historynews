In January 1942, as Japanese forces swept across the Pacific, the United States rushed to fortify American Samoa. The territory — seven small islands totaling just 76 square miles, located about 2,600 miles south of Hawaii — sat on the critical supply line between the United States and Australia. If Japan seized Samoa, it could cut the lifeline to Australia and New Zealand, isolating America's most important Pacific allies.

## America's Oldest Colony

The United States acquired Eastern Samoa through the Treaty of Berlin in 1899 and the Deed of Cession in 1900, when local chiefs voluntarily ceded their islands to the United States in exchange for protection from European colonial powers. The U.S. Navy administered the islands from 1900 to 1951.

American Samoa holds a unique and often misunderstood status in the American political system. Its residents are U.S. nationals — not U.S. citizens. They can live and work freely in the United States, they carry U.S. passports (stamped "national" rather than "citizen"), and they can serve in the military. But they are not citizens by birth, cannot vote in federal elections, and must go through a naturalization process to become citizens — even though they are born on American soil.

This status is not shared by any other inhabited U.S. territory. Residents of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the U.S. Virgin Islands are all citizens at birth.

## The Marine Corps Arrives

In January 1942, the 2nd Marine Brigade — approximately 7,000 troops — arrived in American Samoa to build defenses against a possible Japanese invasion. The Marines constructed fortifications, airfields, and coastal gun emplacements. They also recruited Samoan men into the Marine Corps Reserve, forming the Samoan Marine units that would serve with distinction.

The expected Japanese attack never came. The Battles of the Coral Sea and Midway in mid-1942 shifted the strategic balance, and the threat to Samoa receded. But the military buildup had lasting effects. The sudden influx of thousands of Marines — into a community of roughly 12,000 people — transformed the islands' economy, infrastructure, and social dynamics.

## The Fono and the Flag

American Samoa's relationship with the United States is shaped by a factor rarely discussed in mainland politics: the Samoans themselves have been ambivalent about changing their status. The traditional governing council, the Fono, has historically resisted full U.S. citizenship partly because of concerns that the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment could be used to challenge *fa'a Samoa* — the traditional Samoan way of life — including communal land ownership systems that restrict land sales to people of Samoan descent.

This is one of the rare cases where a U.S. territory has been cautious about gaining more rights, fearing that constitutional protections designed for mainland America could undermine indigenous customs.

## Military Service

Despite their ambiguous political status, American Samoans serve in the U.S. military at one of the highest per capita rates in the nation — a rate estimated at two to three times the national average. The tradition of military service, established during World War II, runs deep. The cost has been high: American Samoa has suffered disproportionate casualties in Iraq and Afghanistan relative to its tiny population.

## What This Means for You in 2026

American Samoa's story challenges simple narratives about rights and citizenship. The Samoans' cautious approach to full constitutional integration reflects a sophisticated understanding that rights come with tradeoffs — and that protecting a cultural identity sometimes means accepting fewer legal protections. Before you assume what someone else should want, understand what they value. The framework that works for one community may not work for another. Listening matters more than prescribing.
