## The Speech That Went Viral

On May 17, 2014, Admiral William H. McRaven stepped to the podium at the University of Texas at Austin's commencement ceremony. He had commanded the operation that killed Osama bin Laden. He had spent 37 years as a Navy SEAL. He had led thousands of special operations missions across the globe. And his first piece of advice to 8,000 graduates was this:

"If you want to change the world, start off by making your bed."

The audience laughed. McRaven didn't. He was completely serious. He explained that during SEAL training — the grueling six-month program called BUD/S — instructors inspect trainees' beds every morning. The bed must be made to exacting standards: corners square, covers pulled tight, pillow centered. It seems absurd when you're about to spend the day being battered by freezing surf and carrying logs over your head. But the instructors don't care. The bed comes first.

"If you make your bed every morning, you will have accomplished the first task of the day," McRaven told the graduates. "It will give you a small sense of pride, and it will encourage you to do another task, and another, and another. And by the end of the day, that one task completed will have turned into many tasks completed."

The video of the speech spread across the internet. Within months, it had been viewed millions of times. By 2026, the full speech has surpassed 10 million views on YouTube. McRaven's 2017 book expanding on the speech, *Make Your Bed: Little Things That Can Change Your Life…And Maybe the World*, became a number-one *New York Times* bestseller.

## Historical Context

The military's obsession with small, precise tasks didn't begin with McRaven. It's baked into the DNA of every branch of service and stretches back centuries. The logic is rooted in a basic training philosophy: discipline in small things creates discipline in all things.

During the American Revolution, Baron Friedrich von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge in 1778 to train George Washington's ragged Continental Army. One of his first priorities was teaching soldiers to maintain their equipment properly — cleaning muskets, folding uniforms, organizing camps. These weren't combat skills. They were discipline skills. Von Steuben understood that an army that couldn't keep its camp orderly couldn't keep its formation under fire.

The same principle ran through every military era that followed. In World War II, paratroopers in the 101st Airborne Division were drilled relentlessly on packing their parachutes with precision. The task was repetitive, tedious, and enormously consequential — a poorly packed chute could kill you. But the real lesson wasn't about parachutes. It was about attention to detail under pressure.

Navy SEAL training takes this to its extreme. BUD/S candidates who fail bed inspection face punishment — often in the form of being soaked in freezing water and rolled in sand (known as a "sugar cookie"). The point isn't cruelty. The point is teaching trainees that standards matter even when you're exhausted, cold, and broken. Especially then. As the instructors at the Naval Special Warfare Center put it: how you do anything is how you do everything.

McRaven's speech gave this ancient military insight a civilian vocabulary. He translated a principle that warriors have known for millennia into language that a college freshman could understand and apply immediately.

## The Core Lesson: Small Wins Build Momentum

The science behind McRaven's advice is well-documented. Psychologist Roy Baumeister's research on willpower, published in *Willpower: Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength* (2011), demonstrates that self-discipline functions like a muscle — it strengthens with use and weakens with neglect. Small acts of discipline early in the day build what Baumeister calls "self-regulatory strength," making it easier to tackle harder tasks later.

Harvard Business School professor Teresa Amabile's research on the "progress principle" found that the single most motivating factor in a person's workday is making progress on meaningful work — even small progress. Completing a task, no matter how minor, triggers a sense of forward motion that fuels further effort. Making your bed is a progress trigger.

McRaven framed it in SEAL terms: "If you can't do the little things right, you will never do the big things right." The bed is a test case. If you can impose order on one small corner of your world before you leave the house, you're practicing agency. You're telling yourself, before the day has had a chance to go sideways, that you are a person who finishes what they start.

This connects directly to the military principle that [calm is contagious](/articles/calm-is-contagious/). The discipline to make your bed is the same discipline that keeps your voice steady in a crisis. Both are habits, and habits are built one repetition at a time.

## Real-World Examples

McRaven's ten lessons from the speech — each drawn from SEAL training — have been adopted far beyond the military. The lessons include finding people to paddle with you, measuring people by the size of their heart rather than their flippers, getting over being a "sugar cookie," not being afraid of the circus (extra physical training imposed on underperformers), and singing when you're up to your neck in mud.

But the bed-making lesson resonated most widely because it requires nothing — no equipment, no money, no special talent. Just the decision to start your day with a completed task.

Tim Ferriss, author and podcast host, has asked hundreds of high performers about their morning routines. He found that over 80 percent have some form of structured morning ritual — whether it's meditation, exercise, journaling, or, yes, making their bed. The common thread isn't the specific activity. It's the act of beginning the day with intention rather than reaction.

Lieutenant Michael Murphy, the Navy SEAL who earned the Medal of Honor in Afghanistan, was known by his teammates for his meticulous habits — from gear preparation to physical training schedules. As documented in [his story](/articles/navy-seal-michael-murphy/), Murphy's attention to small disciplines created the operational readiness that sustained him in combat. The bed, the gear check, the workout log — they were all links in the same chain.

## Personal Growth: The Compound Effect in 2026

In 2026, the case for small daily wins is stronger than ever. The average person checks their phone within ten minutes of waking. Social media algorithms are engineered to hijack attention before the day has even started. Making your bed is a countermeasure — a deliberate act that puts you in control before the notifications take over.

McRaven's advice is not about beds. It's about agency. Every completed task, no matter how small, is evidence that you can affect your environment. Stack enough of those tasks together and you build something psychologists call "self-efficacy" — the belief that your actions matter. People with high self-efficacy set harder goals, persist longer, and recover faster from setbacks. People with low self-efficacy give up early and blame circumstances.

The military teaches this through relentless repetition. Civilians can learn it the same way. Pick one small task. Do it first thing every morning. Do it to a standard. Do it whether you feel like it or not. After a week, it becomes easier. After a month, it becomes automatic. After a year, it has reshaped how you approach everything else.

McRaven ended his speech with a final thought: "If you want to change the world, start off by making your bed." He wasn't being cute. He was summarizing 37 years of leading warriors into combat. The lesson is simple, and it works: start small, start now, and don't stop.
