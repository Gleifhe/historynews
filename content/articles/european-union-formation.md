## Enemies Become Partners

For centuries, Europe was the most war-torn continent on Earth. France and Germany alone fought three devastating wars between 1870 and 1945. World War I killed 20 million people. World War II killed 70 to 85 million. By 1945, the continent was shattered, and millions of Europeans were asking the same question: how do we stop this cycle of destruction?

The answer came from an unlikely place — not the battlefield, but the coal mine. In 1950, French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman proposed that France and Germany pool their coal and steel production under a shared authority. Coal and steel were the raw materials of war. If you controlled them together, the thinking went, you made war between the two countries not just unlikely, but physically impossible.

The idea was revolutionary. Just five years after the end of World War II, the two countries that had been the bitterest enemies in history agreed to share control of their most important industries.

## Building Blocks of Unity

In 1951, six countries — France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg — signed the Treaty of Paris, creating the European Coal and Steel Community. It was the first step toward something much bigger.

The success of the Coal and Steel Community inspired the same six nations to go further. In 1957, they signed the Treaty of Rome, creating the European Economic Community (EEC). The EEC established a common market — a zone where goods, workers, and money could move freely across borders without tariffs or restrictions. Farmers in France could sell their products in Germany. Workers in Italy could take jobs in Belgium. Trade between the six countries boomed.

This was the genius of the European project. It didn't start with grand political declarations or military alliances. It started with practical economic cooperation — trade deals, shared standards, and common rules. Each success built trust, and each layer of trust made deeper cooperation possible.

Over the following decades, the community grew. Britain, Ireland, and Denmark joined in 1973. Greece joined in 1981. Spain and Portugal joined in 1986. The [Marshall Plan](/articles/marshall-plan-rebuilds-europe/) had planted the seeds by requiring European nations to cooperate in their recovery. Now those seeds were growing into something far larger than anyone had imagined.

## The Maastricht Treaty and the Birth of the EU

The biggest leap came after the [fall of the Berlin Wall](/articles/berlin-wall-falls/) in 1989. With the Cold War ending and Germany reunifying, European leaders decided to take their partnership to a new level.

On February 7, 1992, leaders from 12 European nations gathered in the small Dutch city of Maastricht and signed a treaty that transformed the European Economic Community into the European Union. The Maastricht Treaty went into effect on November 1, 1993.

The EU was much more than a trade agreement. It created European citizenship, allowing people to live and work in any member state. It established common foreign and security policies. And it laid the groundwork for a shared currency — the Euro — which launched in 1999 and is now used by 21 member states.

Today, the European Union has 27 member nations and a combined population of about 450 million people. It represents the largest single market in the world. Countries that spent centuries fighting wars now debate fishing quotas and product safety standards in the European Parliament.

## The Price of Peace

The EU hasn't been without problems. Critics argue that it's too bureaucratic, too distant from ordinary citizens, and that it takes power away from national governments. Britain's decision to leave the EU in 2020 — known as Brexit — showed that not everyone was convinced the union was worth the trade-offs.

There are also ongoing tensions between wealthier northern countries and struggling southern economies. Debates about immigration, national sovereignty, and the role of EU institutions continue to divide member states.

But the EU's greatest achievement is the one that's easiest to overlook: peace. France and Germany — countries that fought three wars in 75 years — have not fired a single shot at each other since the Coal and Steel Community was founded. The European Union was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2012 for its role in transforming Europe "from a continent of war to a continent of peace."

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The European Union's story teaches us that cooperation starts small. Robert Schuman didn't propose a political union of 27 countries in 1950. He proposed sharing coal and steel between two countries. That small, practical step built the trust needed for bigger steps later, drawing on the same principle of self-governance found in the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/) — that people can choose how they are governed.

In your own life, building trust works the same way. You don't become best friends with someone overnight. You start with small acts of reliability — showing up when you say you will, keeping your promises, sharing something you care about. Over time, those small acts build a relationship strong enough to survive disagreements and setbacks.

The EU also demonstrates that former enemies can become partners. If France and Germany can go from fighting the bloodiest wars in history to sharing a currency and an open border, then most conflicts in your own life — with classmates, teammates, or family members — can be resolved, too. It takes time, patience, and a willingness to focus on what you share instead of what divides you.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the European Union faces some of its biggest tests yet. The war in Ukraine has forced the EU to rethink its security and energy policies. Rising nationalism in several member states challenges the idea of "ever closer union." Climate change requires coordinated action that not all members agree on.

At the same time, the EU continues to demonstrate the power of cooperation. Its collective response to the COVID-19 pandemic, its joint sanctions against aggressor nations, and its investments in green energy show what's possible when countries work together.

The EU's story is a reminder that peace isn't something that just happens. It's something that has to be built, maintained, and defended — one treaty, one trade deal, one compromise at a time. That work is never finished, and in 2026, it matters more than ever.
