On June 14, 1777, while the American Revolution raged across the eastern seaboard, the Continental Congress in Philadelphia quietly passed a resolution that would echo through centuries. "Resolved," the record stated, "that the flag of the thirteen United States be thirteen stripes, alternate red and white; that the union be thirteen stars, white in a blue field, representing a new constellation."

## A Nation Needs a Symbol

Before June 1777, American forces fought under a confusing patchwork of flags. Militia units carried their colony's banner. Some regiments flew flags with pine trees, rattlesnakes, or local symbols. The Grand Union Flag — thirteen red and white stripes with the British Union Jack in the corner — had served as an unofficial national flag since early 1776, but it sent a mixed message. The colonies were fighting for independence from Britain, yet their flag still bore the British cross.

The Declaration of Independence in July 1776 made the need for a distinct national flag urgent. The new nation needed a symbol that was entirely its own — something that said, unmistakably, "We are not British. We are American."

## The Resolution

The Flag Resolution was remarkably brief — just one sentence. It did not specify how many points the stars should have, how the stars should be arranged, or even which shade of red or blue to use. This vagueness was intentional or, more likely, the result of Congress having far more pressing matters to attend to. The war was not going well in the summer of 1777, and a flag design was a minor concern compared to keeping an army in the field.

The result was that early American flags varied wildly. Some had stars in rows, others in a circle, still others scattered across the blue field. Stars had five points, six points, or even eight. The stripes sometimes started with red, sometimes with white. It would take decades before the design became standardized.

## Who Designed It?

The question of who designed the first American flag has sparked debate for nearly 250 years. No congressional record names a designer. Francis Hopkinson, a New Jersey delegate to the Continental Congress and a signer of the Declaration of Independence, later claimed credit and sent Congress a bill for his work. Congress refused to pay, noting that "he was not the only one consulted" on the design.

The most famous origin story — that Betsy Ross sewed the first flag at George Washington's request — did not emerge publicly until 1870, when Ross's grandson William Canby told the story to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania. No contemporary evidence supports the claim, though Ross was indeed an upholsterer who made flags for the Pennsylvania navy. The story may contain a kernel of truth wrapped in family legend, but historians cannot confirm it.

## The Flag Evolves

As new states joined the Union, the flag had to change. In 1794, Congress added two stars and two stripes for Vermont and Kentucky, creating the fifteen-star, fifteen-stripe flag that would fly over Fort McHenry and inspire ["The Star-Spangled Banner."](/articles/star-spangled-banner/) But as more states joined, it became clear that adding stripes indefinitely would make the flag unwieldy.

In 1818, Congress passed a new law: the flag would return to thirteen stripes — honoring the original colonies — and a new star would be added for each new state on the Fourth of July following its admission. This elegant solution has governed the flag's design ever since. The flag has been modified 27 times, from the original thirteen stars to today's fifty.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Flag Resolution reminds us that symbols matter. In one sentence, the Continental Congress gave a struggling nation something to rally around — a shared identity that transcended colony, religion, and background. The flag did not win battles by itself, but it told soldiers and citizens alike that they were part of something larger than themselves. When you are building something new — a project, a team, a vision — do not underestimate the power of a unifying symbol. Sometimes the simplest statement of purpose is the most enduring.
