There is a story happening right now that barely makes the news, but it might be the most important story of our time. In 1990, about 12.6 million children under the age of five died every single year. That means roughly 34,000 children died every day — mostly from causes that were entirely preventable. By 2020, that number had fallen to under 5 million per year. The world had saved the lives of more than 7 million children annually — and most people don't even know it happened.

This is not a story about one hero or one invention. It's a story about what happens when the whole world works together on a problem and refuses to give up. It's a story about vaccines, clean water, nutrition, education, and some of the simplest medical treatments ever invented.

One of the biggest killers of young children has always been diarrheal disease. In the developing world, contaminated water and poor sanitation cause millions of children to get severe diarrhea, which leads to deadly dehydration. For decades, the only treatment was expensive intravenous fluids administered in hospitals — something most families in poor countries couldn't access.

Then came oral rehydration therapy (ORT) — a mixture of clean water, salt, and sugar that costs just a few cents. When given to a child with diarrhea, this simple solution replaces the lost fluids and prevents dehydration. UNICEF and the World Health Organization launched massive campaigns to teach parents how to mix and administer ORT at home. This single invention is estimated to have saved over 50 million lives since the 1970s.

Vaccines have been another game-changer. In 1990, many children in developing countries had little or no access to basic immunizations. Global campaigns changed that dramatically. The measles vaccine alone prevented an estimated 25.5 million deaths between 2000 and 2020. Vaccines against polio, diphtheria, tetanus, and whooping cough reached hundreds of millions of children who would otherwise have gone unprotected.

The fight against child mortality built on the success of [eradicating smallpox](/articles/smallpox-eradication/), which proved that global vaccination campaigns could eliminate deadly diseases entirely. These achievements also contributed to the broader story of [halving global poverty](/articles/global-poverty-halved/), since healthier children grow into healthier, more productive adults.

Better nutrition played a crucial role too. Vitamin A supplements, given to children twice a year, reduce death from infectious diseases by about 24%. Fortifying salt with iodine prevents brain damage and developmental problems. Breastfeeding promotion campaigns helped millions of mothers give their babies the best possible start in life.

Improved access to clean water and sanitation made a huge difference as well. Between 1990 and 2015, over 2.6 billion people gained access to clean drinking water for the first time. When families have clean water, children get sick far less often.

Malaria prevention has also saved millions of young lives. The distribution of insecticide-treated bed nets — simple mesh nets that keep mosquitoes away while children sleep — has reduced malaria deaths among children by more than 50% in sub-Saharan Africa.

Education, especially for girls, has been one of the most powerful factors. Studies show that when mothers are educated, their children are far more likely to survive. Educated mothers are better at recognizing illnesses, more likely to seek medical care, and more likely to practice good hygiene and nutrition.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of halving child mortality teaches us that progress is possible, even on problems that seem too big to solve. In 1990, the idea of cutting child deaths by more than half would have seemed impossibly ambitious. But it happened — through consistent effort, smart strategies, and millions of people working together.

This is an important lesson for anyone facing a daunting challenge. Break it into smaller pieces. Find what works. Scale it up. Don't get discouraged by the size of the problem — focus on the next step.

The story also teaches us about the power of simple solutions. Oral rehydration therapy is just water, salt, and sugar. Bed nets are just mesh and insecticide. Vitamin A supplements cost pennies. Sometimes the most effective answers are the simplest ones. When you're trying to solve a problem in your own life, don't overlook the obvious solutions just because they seem too easy.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the fight to save children's lives continues. While enormous progress has been made, nearly 5 million children still die each year before their fifth birthday. Most of these deaths occur in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and most are from preventable causes.

New tools are helping push the numbers down further. A groundbreaking malaria vaccine has been approved and is being rolled out across Africa. Improved diagnostic tools allow community health workers to identify and treat pneumonia, diarrhea, and malaria at the village level, without needing to transport sick children to distant hospitals.

Climate change presents new challenges. Rising temperatures are expanding the range of mosquito-borne diseases, drought threatens food security, and extreme weather events disrupt healthcare systems. Addressing these challenges while continuing to reduce child mortality will require the same kind of global cooperation that has worked so well over the past three decades.

The decline in child mortality is proof that the world can solve its biggest problems when it decides to. Every child saved represents a life of possibility — a future teacher, doctor, farmer, or leader. There is no better investment humanity can make.
