## The Men From New York

They called them "New York's Own." The 77th Infantry Division was formed in 1917 at Camp Upton on Long Island, drawn almost entirely from draftees who lived in and around New York City. The ranks were filled with immigrants and the sons of immigrants — Italians, Jews, Poles, Irish, Chinese, and dozens of other nationalities from the tenements and streets of Manhattan, Brooklyn, and the Bronx. Many spoke limited English. Some could barely communicate with their own officers.

Their commander for the operation that would make them famous was Major Charles White Whittlesey, a tall, thin, bespectacled Harvard-educated lawyer who looked more like a professor than a combat officer. Whittlesey was quiet, meticulous, and deeply concerned for his men. He was not the kind of leader who gave rousing speeches. He was the kind who checked on the wounded personally and shared his last rations.

In late September 1918, the 77th Division was thrown into the Meuse-Argonne Offensive — the massive American push designed to crack the Hindenburg Line and end the war. Their sector was the Argonne Forest, a dense, tangled wilderness of trees, ravines, and ridges that the Germans had spent four years fortifying.

## Into the Pocket

On October 2, 1918, Whittlesey was ordered to advance. His force — elements of the 308th and 307th Infantry Regiments, plus parts of the 306th Machine Gun Battalion — numbered approximately 554 men. They pushed forward through the forest, following orders to advance regardless of what was happening on their flanks.

The advance worked — at first. Whittlesey's men pushed through the German lines and established a position on the slope of a ravine called Charlevaux Mill. But the French units on their left and American units on their right failed to keep pace. By the end of the day, the Germans had closed the gap behind them. Whittlesey and his 554 men were completely surrounded.

They were not, strictly speaking, "lost." Whittlesey knew exactly where he was and sent runner after runner back with his coordinates. The runners were killed or captured. He sent carrier pigeons with messages. Most were shot down. The battalion's position was known to American headquarters, but every attempt to break through to them failed.

## Six Days Without Relief

What followed was an ordeal that tested the limits of human endurance. The surrounded Americans had limited ammunition, no food, and their only water source — a muddy stream at the bottom of the ravine — was covered by German machine guns. Any man who crawled down to fill a canteen risked being shot.

The Germans attacked repeatedly, probing the American perimeter with infantry and grenades. Whittlesey's men beat back each assault but at a mounting cost. Wounded men lay in shallow foxholes with no medical supplies beyond what had been in their packs on October 2. There were no bandages, no morphine, and no way to evacuate the injured.

On October 4, the situation became even more desperate. American artillery, trying to support the trapped battalion, began shelling their own men. The shells crashed into the ravine with devastating effect. Whittlesey sent his last carrier pigeon — a bird named Cher Ami — with a frantic message: "We are along the road parallel to 276.4. Our own artillery is dropping a barrage directly on us. For heaven's sake, stop it."

Cher Ami was shot through the breast, blinded in one eye, and had a leg nearly severed by shrapnel, but the pigeon made it back to division headquarters. The artillery fire stopped. Cher Ami later received the Croix de Guerre and was preserved by the Smithsonian Institution, where the bird remains on display today.

## "I Will Not Surrender"

On October 7, the Germans sent a captured American private, Lowell Hollingshead, back through the lines carrying a written surrender demand. The note, composed by a German officer, was respectful in tone. It acknowledged the Americans' bravery and offered generous terms: surrender, and the wounded would receive immediate medical attention.

Whittlesey read the note and said nothing. He did not send a reply. According to accounts from men who were present, he simply put the note in his pocket and walked away. The American press later reported that Whittlesey had responded with the phrase "Go to hell!" — but Whittlesey himself insisted he never said it. His actual response was silence, which may have been even more defiant.

The men held on. They ate leaves, bark, and whatever they could scrounge. Some drank rainwater from puddles. They redistributed ammunition from the dead. Whittlesey moved constantly among his men, calm and steady, giving quiet encouragement.

## Relief

On October 8, American forces finally broke through the German encirclement. When the relief column reached the ravine, they found Whittlesey standing among the survivors, gaunt and hollow-eyed. Of the 554 men who had entered the pocket, only 194 walked out under their own power. Approximately 107 had been killed, and the rest were wounded, missing, or captured.

The stand occurred during the same week that [Sergeant Alvin York made his legendary one-man assault](/articles/sergeant-alvin-york/) just a few miles away in the same forest. Together, the Lost Battalion and York's action became the defining American stories of the Meuse-Argonne campaign.

Whittlesey received the Medal of Honor, as did Captain George McMurtry and Captain Nelson Holderman. Several other men received the Distinguished Service Cross. The 77th Division became one of the most celebrated units in American military history.

## The Weight of Command

But the story did not end in the Argonne. Charles Whittlesey came home a hero and was chosen to select the Unknown Soldier who was interred at Arlington National Cemetery on November 11, 1921. He was haunted by the men he had lost. He received letters from the families of dead soldiers asking what had happened to their sons and brothers. He answered every one.

On November 26, 1921 — just fifteen days after the Unknown Soldier ceremony — Whittlesey boarded the SS Toloa bound for Havana. Somewhere in the night, he disappeared from the ship. His body was never recovered. He was forty years old. No note was found, but those who knew him believed the weight of what he had experienced in the Argonne had never left him.

Whittlesey's fate is a stark reminder that survival is not the same as recovery. The physical wounds of war may heal, but the psychological wounds — what we now call post-traumatic stress — can persist for a lifetime. The men who held that ravine in the Argonne carried their experience with them forever, as would soldiers in every subsequent war, from the beaches of [Dunkirk](/articles/dunkirk-evacuation/) to the mountains of Afghanistan.

## Personal Growth: Endurance and Its Limits

The Lost Battalion teaches a powerful lesson about endurance — and an equally important one about its limits. The men in that ravine survived because they refused to quit. They had every reason to surrender: no food, no water, no ammunition, no hope of rescue. They held on anyway. That kind of resilience is real, and it matters.

But the story also teaches that endurance has a cost. Whittlesey endured the pocket, endured the fame, endured the letters from grieving families — and eventually could endure no more. His story is a reminder that toughness alone is not enough. We also need support, connection, and the willingness to ask for help. Being strong does not mean carrying everything alone.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, conversations about mental health — especially among veterans and first responders — are more open than they have ever been, but stigma persists. Whittlesey's story puts a human face on a problem that is still with us: the people who seem strongest on the outside are sometimes the ones struggling the most. If the Lost Battalion teaches us anything, it is that courage and vulnerability are not opposites. The bravest thing a person can do is sometimes not to keep fighting, but to reach out and say, "I need help." That lesson, more than any battlefield victory, is the one that could save the most lives.
