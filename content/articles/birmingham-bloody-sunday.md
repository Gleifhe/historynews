## The Most Segregated City in America

In 1963, Birmingham, Alabama, was considered the most segregated city in the United States. The city's public safety commissioner, Eugene "Bull" Connor, was a fierce defender of white supremacy who had used police power for years to crush any challenge to segregation.

Martin Luther King Jr. and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) chose Birmingham deliberately. They knew that Connor's violent response to peaceful protest would be broadcast on national television, forcing Americans to confront the brutal reality of segregation. King called it "Project C" — the C stood for confrontation.

The campaign began on April 3, 1963, with sit-ins at lunch counters and marches to city hall. Hundreds of protesters were arrested, including King himself on April 12. While in solitary confinement, King wrote his famous "Letter from Birmingham Jail," answering white clergy who had called his protests "unwise and untimely." King responded: "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere."

## The Children's Crusade

The campaign's turning point came on May 2, 1963, when organizer James Bevel launched the Children's Crusade. Over a thousand Black students, some as young as six years old, marched out of Sixteenth Street Baptist Church toward downtown Birmingham.

Bull Connor ordered police to arrest them. More than 600 children were jailed on the first day. The next day, May 3, Connor escalated. He ordered firefighters to turn high-pressure fire hoses on the young marchers. The water was powerful enough to strip bark from trees. It knocked children off their feet and sent them tumbling across the pavement. Connor also unleashed police dogs, which attacked teenagers while photographers captured every moment.

The images were published on front pages across the country and around the world. Americans who had been indifferent to segregation were suddenly confronted with photographs of children being attacked by dogs and blasted with hoses for the crime of asking to be treated as human beings.

President John F. Kennedy reportedly said the images made him "sick." Within weeks, Kennedy went on national television and called civil rights "a moral issue" — the first time a sitting president had framed it that way. He introduced the Civil Rights Act, which would eventually be signed into law by President Lyndon Johnson on July 2, 1964. The Birmingham campaign had built on years of struggle, including the [Montgomery Bus Boycott](/articles/montgomery-bus-boycott/) and the legacy of the [Emancipation Proclamation](/articles/emancipation-proclamation/).

## Bloody Sunday at Selma

Despite the passage of the Civil Rights Act, Black Americans in the South still faced enormous barriers to voting. Literacy tests, poll taxes, and outright intimidation kept millions from exercising their constitutional right. In Dallas County, Alabama, where Selma is located, Black people made up more than half the population but only 2 percent of registered voters.

On March 7, 1965, about 600 marchers set out from Selma to walk 54 miles to the state capital of Montgomery to demand voting rights. Leading them were John Lewis of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee and Hosea Williams of the SCLC.

They made it six blocks.

At the Edmund Pettus Bridge, Alabama state troopers and county deputies were waiting. Without warning, the officers charged. They beat the marchers with billy clubs and fired tear gas into the crowd. John Lewis, just 25 years old, was struck in the head and suffered a fractured skull. Amelia Boynton Robinson was beaten unconscious. Cameras recorded it all.

That evening, ABC interrupted its broadcast of the movie *Judgment at Nuremberg* — a film about Nazi war crimes — to show footage of Americans beating other Americans on a bridge in Alabama. The juxtaposition was devastating.

## The March Completes

The violence at Selma shocked the nation. People of all races and faiths traveled to Alabama to join the marchers. King called on clergy, students, and ordinary citizens to come to Selma. They did — by the thousands.

On March 21, 1965, protected by federalized National Guard troops, more than 3,000 marchers set out again from Selma. By the time they reached Montgomery four days later, the crowd had grown to 25,000. They marched to the steps of the Alabama State Capitol, where King addressed the crowd.

Five months later, President Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act of 1965, one of the most important pieces of legislation in American history. It outlawed the discriminatory practices that had kept Black Americans from voting for generations.

## The Power of Witness

What made Birmingham and Selma so effective was the role of television. For the first time, Americans could see racial violence in their living rooms. They could not look away. The images created a moral crisis that demanded a response, much as the [March on Washington](/articles/civil-rights-march-on-washington/) the same year showed the movement's peaceful aspirations to the world.

Bull Connor and the troopers at Selma thought violence would crush the movement. Instead, it amplified it. The brutality they displayed became the movement's most powerful recruitment tool.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Birmingham and Selma teach us that courage often means accepting the consequences of doing the right thing. The marchers knew they would be met with violence. The children who walked out of Sixteenth Street Baptist Church knew they might be arrested. They did it anyway, because they believed their cause was just.

These events also demonstrate that young people can be powerful agents of change. The Children's Crusade was carried out by teenagers and children. John Lewis was 25 on Bloody Sunday. Young people did not wait for permission or for adults to act first. They stepped forward and changed the course of history.

Finally, Selma teaches us that setbacks are not the end. The marchers were turned back at the bridge. They regrouped, reorganized, and marched again. When you face failure or rejection, the question is not whether you got knocked down — it's whether you get back up.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, voting rights remain fiercely contested in the United States. Since 2013, when the Supreme Court weakened key provisions of the Voting Rights Act in *Shelby County v. Holder*, states have passed hundreds of new laws that critics say make it harder for minority voters to cast ballots. Debates over voter ID requirements, polling place closures, and gerrymandering echo the same fundamental question the Selma marchers raised: Who gets to participate in democracy?

The power of visual media to shape public opinion is also more relevant than ever. In 1963, television images changed minds. In 2026, smartphone videos and social media posts play a similar role — documenting police encounters, protests, and injustice in real time. The lesson of Birmingham and Selma is that visibility matters. When people can see injustice with their own eyes, it becomes much harder to ignore.
