On June 22, 1942, with the United States six months into World War II, Congress passed Joint Resolution 303 — the first comprehensive federal flag code. The resolution established rules for nearly every aspect of how Americans should treat their flag: when to fly it, how to display it, how to salute it, and how to dispose of it when it was worn beyond repair.

## Before the Code

Before 1942, flag etiquette was governed by custom, not law. Various organizations — particularly the American Legion, the Daughters of the American Revolution, and the Grand Army of the Republic — had published their own guidelines, but they sometimes contradicted each other. During World War I, the National Flag Conference began meeting to standardize flag practices, and their recommendations formed the basis of the 1942 code.

The wartime context made the code feel urgent. Millions of Americans were displaying flags to show support for the troops, and many were doing it incorrectly — hanging flags upside down, using them as decorations, draping them over vehicles, or letting them touch the ground. Congress wanted to establish a uniform standard.

## What the Code Says

The Flag Code covers dozens of specific situations. Some of the key provisions include:

The flag should be displayed from sunrise to sunset on buildings and stationary flagstaffs. It may be displayed at night if properly illuminated. The flag should not be displayed during bad weather unless it is an all-weather flag. When displayed with other flags, the American flag should be at the center and highest point. The flag should never touch the ground, floor, water, or merchandise. It should never be used as clothing, bedding, or drapery. When a flag is in such condition that it is no longer a fitting emblem, it should be destroyed in a dignified way, preferably by burning.

The code also replaced the Bellamy salute — the extended right arm — with the hand-over-heart gesture for civilians during the [Pledge of Allegiance](/articles/pledge-of-allegiance/). The resemblance of the old salute to the Nazi gesture had become too troubling.

## Advisory, Not Criminal

One of the most important and misunderstood aspects of the Flag Code is that it is entirely advisory. Congress deliberately chose not to attach penalties for violations. The code uses the word "should," not "must." No one can be arrested, fined, or prosecuted for violating the Flag Code.

This was not an oversight. Congress recognized that attaching penalties would raise serious First Amendment concerns. The Supreme Court later confirmed this principle in *Texas v. Johnson* (1989), ruling that flag burning is protected speech under the First Amendment.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Flag Code is an exercise in an interesting idea: establishing standards through respect rather than punishment. Congress set clear expectations but trusted citizens to follow them voluntarily. That approach works in many areas of life. When you lead a team, set a community standard, or raise children, the most durable rules are the ones people follow because they understand why — not because they fear consequences.
