## The Seven Words That Run the Military

There's a saying in the military that gets passed from drill sergeants to privates, from officers to cadets, from one generation of warriors to the next. It's crude. It's effective. And it's been shaping military culture for decades: "Prior Proper Planning Prevents Piss-Poor Performance."

The 7 P's — sometimes rendered with a different number of words depending on the branch and the company present — are more than a slogan. They represent a fundamental belief that preparation is not something you do before the real work starts. Preparation *is* the real work. The mission itself is just the execution of what was planned.

General James Mattis, who commanded Marines in Iraq and Afghanistan before serving as Secretary of Defense, put it this way in his memoir *Call Sign Chaos*: "Be polite, be professional, but have a plan to kill everybody you meet." The quote sounds extreme out of context. In context, it captures the military's core planning philosophy: hope for the best, but prepare for every scenario — including the worst ones you'd rather not think about.

## Historical Context

The military's obsession with planning has deep roots. Sun Tzu wrote in *The Art of War* around 500 BCE: "Every battle is won before it is ever fought." The idea that preparation determines outcome more than courage, strength, or luck has been validated on every battlefield since.

No operation in history illustrates this better than D-Day. The Allied invasion of Normandy on June 6, 1944, was the most complex military operation ever attempted. Over 156,000 troops crossed the English Channel. More than 5,000 ships and 11,000 aircraft were involved. The planning took over a year and consumed thousands of staff officers working around the clock.

Dwight Eisenhower, the Supreme Allied Commander, understood something crucial about planning. As documented in [Eisenhower's approach to leadership under uncertainty](/articles/eisenhowers-two-letters/), he wrote two letters before D-Day — one announcing success, one accepting full blame for failure. That act of preparation wasn't pessimism. It was the planning mind at work: consider every outcome, prepare for each one, and then act with confidence because you've already accounted for the worst case.

The planning for D-Day included detailed tide charts, weather analysis, terrain studies, intelligence assessments, supply calculations, and — critically — contingency plans for what to do when things went wrong. And things did go wrong. At Omaha Beach, almost nothing went according to plan. The landing craft arrived in the wrong locations. The aerial bombardment missed its targets. The initial assault waves suffered devastating casualties. But the troops adapted because the planning process had prepared them to think, not just to follow a script.

## The Core Lesson: Planning Reduces Anxiety by Reducing Uncertainty

Here's what the military discovered long before psychologists had the vocabulary for it: anxiety is largely a response to uncertainty. When you don't know what's going to happen, your brain generates worst-case scenarios on a loop. You can't stop it — it's a survival mechanism. But you can redirect it by systematically working through the scenarios yourself.

Military planning is, at its core, a structured method for processing uncertainty. The Marine Corps Planning Process (MCPP) walks officers through a series of steps: mission analysis, course of action development, war-gaming, and orders production. Each step forces planners to ask: What could go wrong? What would we do if it did? What resources do we need? What's the fallback?

By the time the plan is complete, the planner has already lived through the mission mentally — including the failures. This dramatically reduces anxiety during execution because the brain recognizes the situation. It's no longer unknown. As Mattis wrote: "The problem with being too busy to read is that you learn by experience — or by your men's experience. That's a lousy way to learn."

The military's [After Action Review process](/articles/after-action-review/) closes the loop. After every mission, units conduct a structured review of what happened, what was planned, and why the two diverged. The lessons feed back into the next planning cycle. Over time, the quality of planning improves because it's informed by actual experience rather than theory alone.

## Real-World Examples

The 7 P's philosophy extends far beyond combat operations. The Secret Service plans presidential movements with the same obsessive detail. Agents walk every route in advance, identify every potential threat, plan contingencies for medical emergencies, hostile actors, natural disasters, and vehicle breakdowns. By the time the president arrives, the team has already rehearsed dozens of scenarios.

NASA's mission planning follows the same pattern. The Apollo 13 mission in 1970 survived a catastrophic oxygen tank explosion because ground controllers had trained for similar failures. The specific failure hadn't been anticipated, but the process of contingency planning had given the team the mental frameworks to improvise under pressure. Gene Kranz, the flight director, later said: "Failure is not an option" — a phrase that captured the planning culture's insistence on accounting for every possible outcome.

In civilian life, the 7 P's translates directly. Veteran entrepreneurs consistently report that their military planning habits give them a competitive advantage. They write more detailed business plans, run more contingency scenarios, and prepare more thoroughly for meetings and presentations. A 2020 study by the Institute for Veterans and Military Families at Syracuse University found that veteran-owned businesses had a higher five-year survival rate than non-veteran-owned businesses — a finding researchers attributed partly to the planning discipline instilled by military service.

## Personal Growth: Planning as an Anti-Anxiety Tool in 2026

In 2026, anxiety disorders affect over 40 million American adults, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. Much of that anxiety stems from a feeling of being overwhelmed — too many tasks, too many uncertainties, too little control. The military's planning framework offers a practical countermeasure.

Start with what the military calls the "commander's intent" — a clear, simple statement of what success looks like. Before tackling any project, meeting, or conversation, ask yourself: What's the outcome I want? Write it down in one sentence. Everything else flows from that sentence.

Next, identify the obstacles. What could go wrong? What's the most likely failure mode? What's the worst case? Writing these down strips them of their power. An unnamed fear is paralyzing. A named risk is a planning input.

Then build contingencies. For each obstacle, ask: If this happens, what will I do? You don't need a perfect answer. You need *an* answer. Having any plan for a bad outcome is infinitely better than having no plan, because it tells your brain that the scenario is survivable.

Finally, rehearse. The military rehearses every operation — sometimes physically, sometimes through "sand table" exercises where leaders walk through the plan using models and maps. In civilian life, rehearsal might mean practicing a presentation out loud, role-playing a difficult conversation, or mentally walking through your morning routine the night before.

Mattis's quote — "have a plan to kill everybody you meet" — isn't about violence. It's about the radical reduction of uncertainty. When you've thought through every scenario, you walk into the room calmer, sharper, and more confident. You've already done the hard work. Now you just execute.
