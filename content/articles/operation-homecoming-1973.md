## The First Plane

On the morning of February 12, 1973, a United States Air Force C-141 Starlifter sat on the tarmac at Gia Lam Airport in Hanoi, North Vietnam. Inside the airport terminal, 116 American prisoners of war — men who had been held captive for years, some for more than seven years — waited to board.

They had been beaten, starved, isolated, and tortured. They had tapped coded messages through prison walls. They had resisted interrogation, endured solitary confinement measured in months and years, and watched fellow prisoners die. Some had been shot down over North Vietnam as young men and were now approaching middle age. They had not seen their families, their country, or a free sky in years.

When the first group walked across the tarmac to the waiting aircraft, many of them walked stiffly — their bodies broken by years of deprivation. But they walked upright. One by one, they saluted the American flag and climbed the stairs. The doors closed. The plane lifted off.

They were going home.

## Historical Context: The Prisoners of Hanoi

The first American prisoners of war in Vietnam were captured in the early 1960s, but the number grew rapidly after the United States began sustained bombing operations against North Vietnam in 1965. Pilots shot down over enemy territory who survived the ejection and the landing faced immediate capture by North Vietnamese forces or local civilians.

Most captured Americans were taken to prisons in and around Hanoi. The most infamous was Hoa Lo Prison — the [Hanoi Hilton](/articles/hanoi-hilton-pows/), as the prisoners bitterly nicknamed it. Conditions were deliberately harsh. The North Vietnamese government viewed the prisoners not primarily as military captives but as political tools — leverage for propaganda and for negotiations.

Prisoners were subjected to systematic torture, including rope bindings that dislocated shoulders, beatings that broke bones, and prolonged isolation in windowless cells. The goal was to extract confessions, anti-American statements, and propaganda recordings. Many prisoners resisted for as long as they could before breaking. The senior American officers in the camps, including Navy Captain James Stockdale and Air Force Colonel Robinson Risner, established a chain of command and a code of conduct that helped the prisoners maintain discipline and mutual support under almost unbearable conditions.

Communication between cells was conducted through a tap code — a system in which each letter of the alphabet was represented by a series of taps on the wall. Through this painstaking method, prisoners shared news, coordinated resistance, and maintained morale. The tap code became a lifeline — proof that they were not alone.

By 1972, the United States was holding virtually no North Vietnamese prisoners, while North Vietnam held nearly 600 Americans. The fate of these men became a central issue in the Paris Peace negotiations between U.S. National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger and North Vietnamese negotiator Le Duc Tho.

## The Event: Operation Homecoming

The Paris Peace Accords were signed on January 27, 1973. Among the key provisions was the return of all American prisoners of war within 60 days. The operation to bring them home was designated Operation Homecoming.

The releases were conducted in stages. The prisoners were divided into groups, generally organized by the order in which they had been captured — the longest-held prisoners came home first. This followed the military tradition of "first in, first out."

The first group of 116 men was released on February 12. They were bused from their prison camps to Gia Lam Airport, where they boarded C-141 Starlifters for the flight to Clark Air Base in the Philippines. At Clark, they received initial medical evaluations, new uniforms, and their first real meals in years. Many called their families for the first time from the base's telephone center.

The scenes at Clark Air Base were electric with emotion. Gaunt men in ill-fitting uniforms stepped off the planes to cheers, salutes, and the American flag. Some knelt and kissed the ground. Some could barely contain their composure. Captain Jeremiah Denton, who had been held for nearly eight years and had famously blinked the word "T-O-R-T-U-R-E" in Morse code during a forced television interview in 1966, stepped to the microphone and said: "We are honored to have had the opportunity to serve our country under difficult circumstances. We are profoundly grateful to our Commander-in-Chief and to our nation for this day. God bless America."

Over the following weeks, additional groups were released. The last group of prisoners departed Hanoi on March 29, 1973. In total, 591 American POWs were repatriated — 325 from the Air Force, 138 from the Navy, 77 from the Army, 26 from the Marine Corps, and 25 civilians. They ranged from men who had been held for a few months to those who had endured more than eight years of captivity.

## Coming Home to a Changed Country

The POWs returned to a nation that was deeply divided over the war. Unlike the soldiers who came home from World War II to ticker-tape parades and universal gratitude, Vietnam veterans had often returned to indifference or hostility. But the POWs occupied a unique place in public sentiment. They were seen — even by many who opposed the war — as men who had endured unconscionable suffering with honor. Their homecoming became one of the few moments of near-universal national pride during the Vietnam era.

President Richard Nixon hosted the largest dinner in White House history on May 24, 1973, honoring the returned POWs. Over 1,300 guests attended. It was one of the few occasions during the Watergate crisis when the president received unambiguous public goodwill.

But the celebrations masked difficult realities. Many of the returned prisoners faced years of physical rehabilitation. Bones that had been broken and never properly set required surgery. Malnutrition had caused lasting damage to joints, teeth, and internal organs. The psychological effects — what is now recognized as PTSD — haunted many for the rest of their lives.

Marriages had dissolved during the long absences. Children had grown up without their fathers. Some POWs came home to families that were essentially strangers. Rebuilding those relationships was, for many, as difficult as surviving captivity.

And for the families of the missing — the men who were never accounted for — Operation Homecoming brought not reunion but confirmation of their worst fears. The [POW/MIA flag](/articles/pow-mia-flag/), created just the year before, took on even greater significance as a symbol of those who did not come home.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The POWs of Vietnam survived by holding on to three things: each other, a code of conduct, and hope. In the worst conditions imaginable, they maintained discipline, supported each other through the walls, and refused to give up the belief that they would eventually go home. Not every prisoner made it. Not every day felt survivable. But the ones who endured described the same essential ingredients: purpose, connection, and stubborn refusal to quit.

Those ingredients work outside of prison walls too. When life gets hard — and it will — the people who endure are the ones who maintain their sense of purpose, stay connected to people who care about them, and keep going even when the outcome is uncertain.

The POWs also teach a lesson about integrity under pressure. Every prisoner was pressured to make statements against the United States. Many were tortured until they broke. But they established a standard: resist as long as you can, and if you break, recover and resist again. Perfection was not the expectation. Getting back up was. That is a standard anyone can apply to their own failures.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the surviving POWs of Vietnam are in their 80s and 90s. Their numbers diminish each year, but their stories remain among the most powerful in American military history. The debriefs they gave after repatriation — now partially declassified and held by the National Archives — continue to inform military training on survival, resistance, and the psychology of captivity. The SERE (Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape) program, which trains American military personnel to withstand capture, draws directly from the experiences of the Vietnam POWs. Their endurance set a standard that every subsequent generation of American service members has been trained to uphold.
