On July 4, 1777, the United States of America was exactly one year old — and it was not at all clear the country would survive to see a second birthday. The Revolutionary War was going badly. The Continental Army had suffered devastating defeats at Long Island, Fort Washington, and Brandywine. British forces occupied New York City. The outcome of the war was deeply uncertain.

But on this day, Americans decided to celebrate anyway.

## Philadelphia Leads the Way

Philadelphia, which had served as the nation's capital and the birthplace of the Declaration of Independence, organized the most elaborate celebration. The city was festooned with decorations. Ships in the harbor flew streamers and fired cannon salutes. Congress adjourned for the day and held a formal dinner at the City Tavern, complete with toasts, music, and Hessian band members captured at the Battle of Trenton playing patriotic songs.

That evening, the city was illuminated. Residents placed candles in their windows — a common 18th-century celebration practice. Bonfires blazed. Fireworks exploded over the waterfront. Church bells rang throughout the city. The *Virginia Gazette* reported that the celebrations were accompanied by "ringing of bells, and at night there were bonfires and other illuminations."

John Adams, who had championed the Declaration of Independence in Congress the year before, had predicted exactly this kind of celebration. In a letter to his wife Abigail on July 3, 1776, Adams wrote that the anniversary "ought to be solemnized with Pomp and Parade, with Shews, Games, Sports, Guns, Bells, Bonfires and Illuminations from one End of this Continent to the other from this Time forward forever more."

Adams was wrong about only one thing: he thought the celebration would be on July 2, the day Congress voted for independence, rather than July 4, the day the Declaration was formally adopted.

## Celebrations Beyond Philadelphia

The festivities were not limited to the capital. In Boston, which had been liberated from British occupation the previous year, Colonel Thomas Crafts organized a celebration that included a 13-cannon salute at dawn, a prayer, a public reading of the Declaration, a 13-gun salute from forts and ships, speeches, and a night of illumination and fireworks.

Other towns and cities across the new states held their own observances, adapted to local customs and available resources. In some places, celebrations included the public burning of effigies of King George III — a symbolic act of final separation from the monarchy.

## A Tradition Takes Root

The pattern established in 1777 — fireworks, cannon salutes, public readings of the Declaration, feasts, toasts, and music — became the template for Fourth of July celebrations ever after. The holiday was not officially declared a federal holiday until 1870, but it was universally observed long before that.

Over time, the celebration evolved. Fireworks became larger and more elaborate. Barbecues replaced formal dinners. Parades became a central feature. But the core elements that John Adams imagined in 1776 and that Philadelphia enacted in 1777 — "Pomp and Parade," "Bonfires and Illuminations" — remain essentially unchanged nearly 250 years later.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Americans who celebrated the first Fourth of July did so in the middle of a war they were losing. They had every reason to be afraid and none to be confident. But they celebrated anyway — because the act of celebration itself was an act of defiance, a declaration that the cause was worth marking even when the outcome was uncertain. When you are in the middle of something difficult, take time to acknowledge how far you have come. You do not need to wait for victory to celebrate progress.
