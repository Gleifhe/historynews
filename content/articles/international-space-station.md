## From Rivals to Partners

For decades during the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union competed fiercely in the Space Race. Each side poured billions of dollars into building rockets, satellites, and spacecraft, trying to prove that its political system was superior. The race produced incredible achievements — from the Soviet Union's Sputnik satellite in 1957 to the American [Moon landing in 1969](/articles/moon-landing-headlines/). But it was also driven by fear and mistrust between two nuclear superpowers.

When the Cold War ended in the early 1990s, something remarkable happened. Instead of continuing to compete, the two former rivals decided to work together. Russia's space agency, Roscosmos, joined forces with NASA and space agencies from Europe, Japan, and Canada to build something that none of them could build alone: a permanent laboratory orbiting the Earth.

The idea wasn't entirely new. NASA had been planning a space station called Freedom since the 1980s, and Russia had years of experience with its Mir space station. But combining these efforts into one international project was a bold step. Countries that had once pointed nuclear missiles at each other during the [Cuban Missile Crisis](/articles/cuban-missile-crisis-13-days/) were now trusting each other with the lives of their astronauts.

## Building a City in the Sky

Construction of the International Space Station began in 1998, when a Russian rocket launched the Zarya module — the station's first piece — into orbit. Over the next 13 years, more than 40 missions carried additional modules, solar panels, laboratories, and equipment to the station. Astronauts performed hundreds of spacewalks to connect the pieces together, working in the vacuum of space with nothing between them and the endless darkness below.

The station is huge. It stretches about 357 feet from end to end — roughly the size of a football field. Its solar panels generate enough electricity to power 40 homes. Inside, there are six sleeping quarters, two bathrooms, a gym, and a 360-degree viewing window called the Cupola that provides stunning views of Earth.

On November 2, 2000, the first long-term crew arrived: American astronaut Bill Shepherd and Russian cosmonauts Yuri Gidzenko and Sergei Krikalev. From that day on, humans have lived continuously in space. The station has never been empty. As of 2026, more than 290 crew members from 26 countries have called it home.

## Life at 17,200 Miles Per Hour

The ISS orbits Earth at an altitude of about 254 miles, traveling at roughly 17,200 miles per hour. At that speed, it circles the planet about every 93 minutes, meaning the crew sees 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day.

Daily life on the station is a mix of hard work and strange challenges. Astronauts spend most of their time conducting scientific experiments — studying everything from how cancer cells grow in zero gravity to how plants develop without the pull of Earth's gravity. These experiments have led to advances in medicine, materials science, and our understanding of the human body.

But living in space is also physically tough. Without gravity, astronauts' muscles and bones weaken. They exercise about two hours every day on specially designed treadmills and exercise machines to stay healthy. Eating is an adventure — food comes in pouches, and crumbs float away if you're not careful. Even sleeping is different: astronauts zip themselves into sleeping bags attached to the wall so they don't drift around the cabin.

The hardest part might be the isolation. Crew members spend months away from their families, living in a space smaller than a typical house with the same few people. They rely on video calls, emails, and care packages launched from Earth to stay connected.

## A Laboratory for Peace

The ISS is more than a science lab. It's proof that nations can work together on something bigger than their differences. The station's five partner agencies — NASA, Roscosmos, ESA (European Space Agency), JAXA (Japan), and CSA (Canada) — represent countries with very different governments, languages, and cultures. Yet they've managed to share resources, coordinate complex operations, and keep humans alive in the most hostile environment imaginable.

This cooperation hasn't always been easy. Political tensions on Earth have sometimes threatened the partnership. When relations between Russia and the West soured over conflicts in Ukraine, some worried the ISS partnership would collapse. But the space agencies kept working together. Astronauts and cosmonauts continued to share meals, conduct experiments, and rely on each other for survival 254 miles above the political disputes below.

The [Wright Brothers' first flight](/articles/wright-brothers-first-flight/) in 1903 lasted just 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. A little over a century later, humans built a permanent home in space where people from 15 nations live and work together. That's an astonishing leap — not just in technology, but in our ability to cooperate.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The ISS teaches us that working with people who are different from you can produce something greater than what any of you could achieve alone. The astronauts on the station come from different countries and speak different languages. They have different customs, different foods, and different ways of seeing the world. But they share a common mission, and that shared purpose is what holds them together.

In your own life, you'll work with people who think differently, look differently, or come from different backgrounds. The ISS shows that these differences aren't obstacles — they're strengths. Different perspectives lead to better solutions. The station's design itself reflects this: Russian modules and American modules fit together because engineers from both countries figured out how to make their systems compatible.

There's also a lesson about persistence. Building the ISS took more than a decade and cost over $100 billion. There were budget cuts, technical failures, and political obstacles. But the partners kept going because they believed the goal was worth the effort. When you're working on something hard — a school project, learning a new skill, building a relationship — the ISS reminds you that great things take time and patience.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the ISS is entering its final chapter. The station, originally designed to last 15 years, has been in orbit for over 25. NASA plans to deorbit it around 2030 and transition to commercially operated space stations. Private companies like Axiom Space are already building modules that will eventually become independent stations.

At the same time, the ISS partnership faces new challenges. Russia has signaled its intention to build its own space station. China, which was never part of the ISS program, has launched its own Tiangong station. The era of a single shared international station may be coming to an end.

But the legacy of the ISS will endure. It proved that international cooperation in space is possible — and valuable. The scientific discoveries made on the station have benefited people on Earth in countless ways. And the image of former rivals working together in orbit remains one of the most hopeful symbols of what humanity can achieve when we choose cooperation over conflict.

The next frontier — whether it's a return to the Moon, a mission to Mars, or something we haven't imagined yet — will require the same spirit of teamwork that built the ISS. The question is whether the world's nations will rise to that challenge again.
