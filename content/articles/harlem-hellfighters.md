## The Men Nobody Wanted

In the spring of 1917, the United States entered World War I. Millions of young men volunteered or were drafted to fight in the trenches of France. Among them were the men of the 15th New York National Guard Regiment — a unit composed almost entirely of Black soldiers from Harlem. They were eager to serve. Their own country did not want them.

The American military was rigidly segregated. Black soldiers were considered unfit for combat by most white officers. They were assigned to labor battalions — digging trenches, loading supplies, building roads. The men of the 15th New York had been promised they would fight. Instead, they spent their first months in the Army doing manual labor at Camp Wadsworth in Spartanburg, South Carolina, where local whites threatened them with violence for wearing the uniform.

When the regiment arrived in France in January 1918, the American Expeditionary Forces under General John Pershing didn't know what to do with them. Pershing generally refused to allow Black soldiers to fight alongside white American units. So he did something that would change the regiment's fate — and the course of the war: he assigned them to the French Army.

The French had no objection to fighting alongside Black soldiers. They had been fighting alongside colonial troops from Africa and the Caribbean throughout the war. The 15th New York was redesignated the 369th Infantry Regiment, equipped with French weapons and helmets, and sent to the front lines. The Germans would soon give them a different name: the Hellfighters.

## 191 Days on the Line

The 369th entered the trenches in mid-April 1918 and did not leave the front lines until the armistice on November 11, 1918. Their 191 consecutive days in combat were more than any other American unit served during the war. They fought in the Meuse-Argonne Offensive — the largest and deadliest operation in American military history, involving more than one million U.S. troops. They held their positions in the Argonne Forest and along the Rhine.

Their record was extraordinary. The 369th never lost a trench. They never had a single man captured by the enemy. They never gave up a foot of ground. These are facts, confirmed by both American and French military records.

The fighting was horrific. Trench warfare on the Western Front meant living in muddy ditches under constant artillery bombardment, going "over the top" into machine gun fire, and enduring poison gas attacks. The 369th suffered approximately 1,500 casualties — roughly one-third of the regiment — killed and wounded during their time at the front.

Two members of the 369th earned particular distinction in the early days of their deployment. On May 15, 1918, Privates Henry Johnson and Needham Roberts were on sentry duty when a German raiding party of approximately 24 soldiers attacked their position. Roberts was badly wounded and unable to stand. Johnson, despite being shot and stabbed multiple times, fought back with his rifle, his bolo knife, and his bare hands. He killed at least four German soldiers and wounded several more, driving off the entire raiding party and rescuing Roberts.

France awarded Henry Johnson the Croix de Guerre with Gold Palm — one of the nation's highest military honors. He was the first American to receive it during the war. The United States did not award Johnson the Medal of Honor until 2015 — 86 years after his death.

## A Regiment Honored — By France

When the war ended on November 11, 1918, France recognized the 369th's extraordinary service. The entire regiment was awarded the Croix de Guerre, a decoration for valor in combat. One hundred seventy individual members of the 369th received the Croix de Guerre or the Legion of Merit. No other American regiment received comparable French recognition.

The Hellfighters had fought with a bravery and tenacity that exceeded what anyone — except perhaps the men themselves — had expected. They had proved what the [54th Massachusetts](/articles/54th-massachusetts-regiment/) had proved 55 years earlier during the Civil War: that Black soldiers, given the chance, could fight as well as anyone.

But there was a bitter irony embedded in their service. While the 369th was earning France's highest honors, the American military was sending a different message. In August 1918, the U.S. Army issued a secret document to the French military titled "Secret Information Concerning Black American Troops." It warned the French not to treat Black soldiers as equals, not to eat with them, not to shake their hands, and not to praise them too highly — because it would give them ideas about equality that would be dangerous when they returned to the United States.

The French largely ignored the document. The American high command did not.

## The Parade — And What Came After

On February 17, 1919, the 369th Infantry Regiment marched up Fifth Avenue in New York City in a victory parade that drew hundreds of thousands of spectators. Led by their legendary bandleader, Lieutenant James Reese Europe — whose jazz-inflected military band had introduced France to American jazz and ragtime — the Hellfighters marched from Lower Manhattan through Midtown and up to Harlem. The crowds were jubilant. Flowers rained down from windows.

It was, for a brief shining moment, a scene of triumph and recognition. And then reality set in.

The men of the 369th returned to an America that was, in many ways, more hostile to Black citizens than the one they had left. The summer of 1919 became known as "Red Summer" — a wave of racial violence that swept across the country. White mobs attacked Black communities in more than two dozen cities. Black veterans, who had fought for democracy in Europe, were targeted specifically. Some were lynched in uniform. The message was unmistakable: the freedoms they had fought for overseas did not apply at home.

Henry Johnson, the hero of the May 1918 raid, returned home with 21 wounds and no disability benefits. The Army refused to acknowledge his injuries or his combat record. He was unable to work, fell into poverty and alcoholism, and died in 1929. He was buried in Arlington National Cemetery, but his valor was not officially recognized by the United States for nearly a century.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The Harlem Hellfighters' story is about proving yourself in the face of contempt. These men were told they couldn't fight, told they weren't good enough, told they didn't belong. They responded by compiling the most distinguished combat record of any American regiment in the war. They didn't argue with their critics — they outperformed them.

That's a lesson that applies far beyond the battlefield. When someone underestimates you — because of your background, your appearance, your accent, your neighborhood — the most powerful response is excellence. Not anger, not bitterness, but relentless, undeniable competence. The Hellfighters didn't need America to believe in them. They believed in themselves.

Their story also carries a harder lesson: that excellence doesn't always guarantee justice. The Hellfighters did everything right and still came home to segregation, violence, and neglect. That's a painful reality, but it's an honest one. Doing the right thing doesn't always produce the right outcome. What it does produce is a record — a truth that can't be erased. The 369th's service record outlasted the prejudice that tried to bury it.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the military services of the United States are among the most diverse institutions in the country. Men and women of every race, background, and identity serve side by side. That reality exists in part because of units like the Harlem Hellfighters and the [54th Massachusetts](/articles/54th-massachusetts-regiment/) before them — units that demonstrated, at the cost of their own blood, that courage has no color.

But the Hellfighters' story also resonates because the gap between service and recognition persists. In 2026, veteran communities continue to advocate for proper acknowledgment of the contributions of soldiers of color, women, and other groups whose service was historically minimized or ignored. Henry Johnson's 86-year wait for the Medal of Honor is not a relic of the past — it's a reminder of how long justice can take, and how important it is to keep demanding it.

The men of the 369th Infantry Regiment were sent to war by a country that didn't want them, assigned to an army that rejected them, and honored by a nation that wasn't their own. They fought anyway. They fought brilliantly. And their story — of courage, of excellence, and of the long struggle for recognition — deserves to be remembered not as a footnote but as one of the defining stories of American history. The veterans of [D-Day](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/) who stormed the beaches a generation later did so in a military that remained segregated — proof that the Hellfighters' fight was far from over.
