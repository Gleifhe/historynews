## The Longest Day

June 6, 1944. The day the Allies invaded Nazi-occupied France. The day they called D-Day.

It was the largest seaborne invasion in history. More than 156,000 troops from the United States, Britain, Canada, and other Allied nations crossed the English Channel and stormed five beaches along the coast of Normandy, France. They were supported by 5,000 ships and 11,000 aircraft.

The invasion was the beginning of the end for Nazi Germany. But for the soldiers who landed on the beaches — especially at Omaha Beach — it was the most terrifying experience of their lives.

## What They Saw

The National WWII Museum in New Orleans holds one of the world's largest collections of oral histories from D-Day veterans. These first-person accounts bring the invasion to life in a way no textbook can.

**John Barnes, 116th Infantry Regiment**, described approaching Omaha Beach in a landing craft:

"The boat started taking fire about 400 yards from shore. Bullets were pinging off the ramp. The guys in front of me were praying. The ramp dropped and the first men out were hit immediately. I stepped into water up to my chest. My rifle was over my head. I could see men falling all around me."

**Bob Slaughter, 29th Division**, remembered:

"The beach was a nightmare. Bodies everywhere. Equipment scattered. Men calling for medics. I found a piece of a wall and pressed my body against it. I thought, 'This is it. I'm going to die here on this beach.'"

## Omaha Beach: The Bloodiest Landing

Of the five D-Day beaches, Omaha was by far the deadliest. The beach was overlooked by high bluffs where the Germans had built bunkers, machine gun nests, and artillery positions. The soldiers landing below were completely exposed.

In the first hour, nearly every officer and sergeant in the first wave at Omaha Beach was killed or wounded. Radio equipment was destroyed. The carefully planned assault fell apart. Soldiers found themselves leaderless, pinned down on a narrow strip of sand under constant fire.

What happened next showed the incredible courage of ordinary soldiers. Small groups of men — sometimes just three or four — began working their way up the bluffs. They didn't wait for orders. They just started climbing.

**Sergeant Leonard Lomell** and his Rangers scaled the cliffs at Pointe du Hoc under fire to destroy German artillery guns that were threatening the landing beaches. Their success owed much to the [codebreakers at Bletchley Park](/articles/enigma-code-bletchley-park/), whose intelligence work had helped the Allies plan every detail of the invasion. When they reached the top, they found the guns had been moved — but they tracked them down in a nearby orchard and destroyed them with thermite grenades.

## The Medics

Some of the most powerful oral histories come from medics — the soldiers whose job was to save lives under fire.

Medics carried no weapons. They wore red cross armbands that were supposed to protect them under the Geneva Conventions. Many German soldiers ignored these rules.

One medic from the National WWII Museum collection recalled:

"I worked on the beach for six hours straight. I ran out of bandages. I ran out of morphine. I used belts as tourniquets. I used shirts as bandages. A soldier grabbed my arm and said, 'Doc, help me.' I looked down and realized he had no legs. I gave him the last of my morphine and stayed with him until he died."

## The French Civilians

It's easy to forget that French people lived in the towns and villages behind the beaches. They had been under Nazi occupation for four years and had been waiting desperately for liberation.

But liberation came with destruction. The Allied bombing and naval bombardment before the landing destroyed many French towns. Thousands of French civilians were killed on D-Day and in the weeks that followed.

French civilians who survived have their own oral histories. One woman from the town of Sainte-Mère-Église remembered:

"We heard the bombs all night. In the morning, we looked out and saw the soldiers. American soldiers. We cried. We ran out and hugged them. They were so young. Some of them looked like children."

## The Cost

By the end of June 6, 1944, the Allies had established a foothold on the beaches of Normandy. But the cost was enormous:

- **Over 4,400 Allied soldiers** were killed on D-Day
- **About 10,000 more** were wounded, missing, or captured
- **Omaha Beach alone** accounted for roughly 2,400 American casualties

## Why the Oral Histories Matter

The soldiers who fought on D-Day are now in their late 90s and beyond. Most have passed away. The oral history collections at the National WWII Museum and the Library of Congress's Veterans History Project preserve their voices for future generations.

These aren't polished stories. They're raw, honest, sometimes painful memories from young men who were asked to do something unimaginably difficult — and did it anyway. Many of those same young men went on to fight in the Pacific, where [the battle of Iwo Jima](/articles/iwo-jima-flag-photo/) produced its own iconic image of sacrifice.

D-Day didn't just happen in textbooks. It happened to real people. Their stories deserve to be heard. And in the aftermath of the invasion, the [Nuremberg Trials](/articles/nuremberg-trials/) would hold accountable the Nazi leaders whose crimes made the sacrifice of D-Day necessary.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The D-Day oral histories teach us something powerful about ordinary people doing extraordinary things. The soldiers who stormed Omaha Beach weren't superheroes. They were teenagers and young men — bakers, farmers, students — who were terrified but kept going anyway. Courage isn't the absence of fear. It's acting in spite of fear. That lesson applies whether you're facing a battlefield or just standing up for someone being bullied at school.

These stories also show the importance of teamwork and stepping up when leadership fails. When the officers were killed and the radios were destroyed, small groups of soldiers didn't wait for someone to tell them what to do. They figured it out themselves. In your own life, there will be moments when nobody is in charge and everything seems chaotic. Those are the moments that define who you are.

Finally, the oral histories remind us to listen to people who've been through hard things. The veterans who shared their stories carried those memories for decades. Listening — really listening — to other people's experiences builds empathy and helps us understand the real cost of the decisions leaders make.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the last D-Day veterans are passing away. Their oral histories are more important than ever because soon there will be no living witnesses to tell us what that day was like. This connects to a bigger challenge we face today: how do we preserve truth in an age of AI-generated content and deepfakes? When anyone can create realistic fake video or audio, firsthand accounts and verified archives become our anchor to reality.

The cooperation that made D-Day possible also has lessons for 2026. The invasion required American, British, Canadian, and other Allied forces to work together despite their differences. Today, international cooperation is being tested by geopolitical tensions, trade wars, and disagreements about how to handle challenges like climate change and AI regulation. D-Day reminds us that the biggest problems require people and nations to work together, even when it's difficult.

The human cost of war described in these oral histories is also a sobering reminder as conflicts continue around the world in 2026. When we see headlines about wars and military operations, it's easy to think in terms of numbers and strategy. The D-Day stories put faces and voices to those numbers. They remind us that behind every statistic is a real person — someone's son, someone's friend — and that the decision to go to war should never be taken lightly.
