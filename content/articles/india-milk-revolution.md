In the 1960s, India had a serious problem. The country of hundreds of millions of people didn't have enough milk. Most families couldn't afford it, and the supply that existed was controlled by middlemen who bought milk cheaply from poor farmers and sold it at high prices in the cities. Farmers were trapped in poverty, children were malnourished, and India had to import dairy products from abroad. It seemed like an impossible situation.

Then came Dr. Verghese Kurien — a young engineer who didn't even like milk. What he did like was solving problems and fighting for the little guy. Over the next three decades, Kurien would lead the largest dairy development program in history, turn India into the world's biggest milk producer, and lift millions of families out of poverty. His weapon? A simple idea called the cooperative.

The story begins in the small town of Anand, in the western state of Gujarat. In 1946, local dairy farmers were being exploited by a government-backed monopoly that set unfairly low prices for their milk. Led by a freedom fighter named Tribhuvandas Patel, the farmers formed their own cooperative — a business owned and run by the farmers themselves. They called it Amul.

Kurien, who had been sent to Anand on a government assignment, was supposed to leave after a few months. Instead, he stayed for the rest of his career. He helped the Amul cooperative grow by introducing modern dairy technology, quality control, and professional management — all while keeping the farmers in charge. The model was revolutionary: farmers pooled their milk, processed it in their own factories, and sold it under their own brand name. The profits went back to the farmers, not to middlemen.

The Amul model was so successful that in 1970, India's National Dairy Development Board launched Operation Flood — an ambitious plan to replicate the Anand cooperative model across the entire country. Kurien led the program, which ran in three phases from 1970 to 1996.

Operation Flood connected millions of small-scale dairy farmers to consumers in India's growing cities. It built a massive cold chain — a network of refrigerated transportation and storage that kept milk fresh from village to city. It set up thousands of village-level cooperatives where farmers could bring their milk twice a day, have it tested for quality, and receive fair payment on the spot.

The results were extraordinary. India's milk production more than quadrupled, from about 22 million tons in 1970 to over 90 million tons by 2000. India surpassed the United States to become the world's largest milk producer — a position it still holds today. More importantly, the program reached about 15 million farm families, most of them small-scale and landless farmers who had never had a reliable income before.

Operation Flood drew on the same principles that powered the [Green Revolution](/articles/green-revolution-borlaug/) — bringing modern technology and better practices to small farmers — and reflected the spirit of self-reliance that [Mahatma Gandhi](/articles/indian-independence-gandhi/) had championed for India.

Women were at the heart of the revolution. In most Indian farming families, women were the ones who cared for the animals and milked them. The cooperatives gave women a way to earn income directly, and many women became leaders in their local dairy cooperatives. This economic empowerment changed social dynamics in villages across India.

Kurien, who became known as the "Milk Man of India," received some of the country's highest honors, including the Padma Vibhushan. He died in 2012, but his legacy lives on in the Amul brand, which is now one of the largest food brands in the world, and in the cooperative model that continues to empower farmers across India and beyond.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Verghese Kurien's story teaches us that real change often starts from the bottom up, not the top down. Instead of waiting for governments or big companies to solve the problem, he helped farmers organize themselves and take control of their own economic destiny. The lesson is clear: empowering people to help themselves is more effective than doing things for them.

Kurien also shows us the power of sticking with something for the long haul. He spent his entire career — over fifty years — working on one problem. In a world that often celebrates quick wins and instant results, his patience and persistence are a powerful reminder that the most important work takes time.

His story also teaches us about the value of fairness. The cooperative model worked because it was fair — every farmer, no matter how small, got a fair price for their milk and had a vote in how the cooperative was run. In any group or organization, fairness builds trust, and trust builds success.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the cooperative model that Kurien championed is being applied to new challenges. Farmer cooperatives in Africa and Southeast Asia are using similar approaches to improve agricultural productivity, and technology is making it easier than ever for small producers to connect with markets.

India's dairy industry continues to grow, and the Amul brand — born from a small village cooperative — is now expanding internationally. The success of Operation Flood is studied in business schools and development agencies around the world as a model for how to create prosperity without destroying traditional ways of life.

Climate change presents new challenges for dairy farmers, including heat stress on animals and changes in feed availability. But the cooperative structure gives farmers a framework for adapting together — sharing knowledge, investing in new technologies, and advocating for supportive policies.

Kurien's vision of empowering the poorest producers through collective action remains as relevant today as it was in 1970. In a world of growing inequality, the cooperative model offers a proven path to shared prosperity — one village at a time.
