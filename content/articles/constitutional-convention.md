On May 25, 1787, fifty-five delegates from twelve states gathered at the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia — the same building where the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/) had been signed eleven years earlier. They had been authorized by Congress to revise the Articles of Confederation, the weak framework that had governed the country since independence. Within days, they decided to throw the Articles out entirely and start over.

## A Country in Trouble

By 1787, the United States was in crisis. The Articles of Confederation, ratified in 1781, had created a government so weak it could barely function. Congress could not levy taxes — it could only request money from states, which routinely ignored the requests. There was no national court system. There was no executive branch. Each state had one vote regardless of population. Amending the Articles required unanimous consent from all thirteen states.

The result was chaos. States imposed tariffs on each other's goods. The national government could not pay its war debts. In 1786, Shays' Rebellion — an armed uprising of indebted farmers in Massachusetts — exposed the government's inability to maintain order. Something had to change.

## The Delegates

The men who gathered in Philadelphia were, on the whole, remarkably accomplished. George Washington, the most famous man in America, presided over the convention. Benjamin Franklin, 81 years old and in failing health, attended and lent his prestige. James Madison, who had spent months studying governments ancient and modern, came with a detailed plan. Alexander Hamilton represented New York. Gouverneur Morris would write the final draft.

Notable absences included Thomas Jefferson, who was serving as ambassador to France, and John Adams, who was ambassador to Britain. Patrick Henry, who had been selected as a delegate, refused to attend, saying he "smelt a rat."

## The Great Debates

The convention met in strict secrecy — windows were nailed shut despite the Philadelphia summer heat, and delegates were sworn to silence. This allowed them to speak freely and change their minds without public pressure.

The most contentious debate was over representation. Large states, led by Virginia, wanted representation based on population. Small states, led by New Jersey, wanted equal representation for each state. The deadlock nearly destroyed the convention. The solution, known as the Great Compromise (or Connecticut Compromise), created a bicameral legislature: the House of Representatives with proportional representation and the Senate with two members per state.

The question of slavery produced another agonizing compromise. Southern states wanted enslaved people counted for representation but not for taxation. Northern states wanted the opposite. The result was the Three-Fifths Compromise, which counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person for both representation and taxation. The convention also agreed to allow the slave trade to continue for twenty more years, until 1808.

These compromises preserved the union but embedded a moral contradiction at the heart of the Constitution that would eventually lead to civil war.

## "A Republic, If You Can Keep It"

On September 17, 1787, thirty-nine of the remaining forty-two delegates signed the Constitution. Benjamin Franklin, looking at the president's chair — which had a carved sun on its back — said he had often wondered during the convention whether the sun was rising or setting. "Now at length I have the happiness to know," he said, "that it is a rising and not a setting sun."

As Franklin left the building, a woman reportedly asked him what kind of government the convention had created. "A republic, madam," Franklin replied, "if you can keep it."

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Constitutional Convention succeeded because the delegates were willing to compromise on details while holding firm on principles. They did not get the perfect government any one of them would have designed alone. They got a workable government that has endured for over two centuries. When you are part of a group trying to solve a hard problem, the goal is not to win every argument. The goal is to build something that works — and that lasts.
