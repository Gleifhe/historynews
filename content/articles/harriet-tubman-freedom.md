On a dark night in September 1849, a young enslaved woman named Harriet Tubman made the most dangerous decision of her life. She left the plantation in Dorchester County, Maryland, where she had been enslaved since birth, and began walking north toward freedom. Traveling alone through swamps and forests, guided by the North Star and helped by a network of safe houses known as the Underground Railroad, she walked nearly 90 miles to reach Pennsylvania — a free state.

But Harriet Tubman didn't stay in safety. Over the next eleven years, she went back to the South again and again — approximately 13 times — to lead others to freedom. She personally guided about 70 enslaved people, including her own parents, brothers, and other family members, along the dangerous route north. She famously said, "I never ran my train off the track, and I never lost a passenger."

Born Araminta Ross around 1822, Tubman endured a brutal childhood. She was hired out to other families as a young child, was whipped regularly, and suffered a devastating head injury when an overseer threw a heavy metal weight at another enslaved person and hit her instead. The injury caused seizures, severe headaches, and episodes of falling asleep without warning for the rest of her life. Despite this disability, she became one of the bravest people in American history.

The Underground Railroad was not a real railroad. It was a secret network of safe houses, hidden routes, and brave people — both Black and white — who helped enslaved people escape to the North and to Canada. "Conductors" like Tubman guided passengers along the route. "Stations" were the homes where they could hide during the day. It was incredibly dangerous work. If caught, conductors could be imprisoned or killed, and escaped people would be returned to slavery and punished severely.

Tubman used clever strategies to avoid capture. She usually traveled on Saturday nights because runaway notices couldn't be posted in newspapers until Monday. She carried a pistol for protection and to discourage anyone in her group from turning back — turning back could endanger everyone. She used disguises, sent coded letters, and sang spirituals whose lyrics contained hidden messages about the route to freedom.

Slaveholders in Maryland were furious and offered a reward of $40,000 (an enormous sum at the time) for her capture. But Tubman was never caught, and she never lost a single person she was leading to freedom.

When the Civil War began in 1861, Tubman joined the Union Army. She served as a nurse, cook, and eventually a spy and scout — the first woman to lead an armed assault during the Civil War. In June 1863, she guided the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people.

After the war, Tubman settled in Auburn, New York, where she cared for her aging parents and continued her activism. She supported the women's suffrage movement and opened a home for elderly African Americans. She lived to be 91, dying on March 10, 1913.

Tubman's courage helped build the foundation for the [Emancipation Proclamation](/articles/emancipation-proclamation/) that would free millions of enslaved people, and her legacy of peaceful resistance echoes through the [March on Washington](/articles/civil-rights-march-on-washington/) a century later.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Harriet Tubman's life teaches us that courage means acting even when you're afraid and even when the odds are against you. She was a small woman with a serious disability, going up against the most powerful institution in the American South. But she didn't let her limitations define her. She found ways to work around every obstacle.

Her story also teaches us about the power of persistence. Tubman didn't make just one trip back to the South — she made thirteen. Each time, the danger was just as real. Each time, she could have decided she had done enough. But she kept going because there were still people who needed help. When we face difficult tasks, Tubman's example encourages us to keep going, one step at a time.

Finally, Tubman shows us that freedom is not just a personal achievement — it's something we should help others achieve too. After reaching her own freedom, she could have lived safely in the North. Instead, she went back for others. True leadership means using your own success and safety to lift others up.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Harriet Tubman's legacy is more visible than ever. The U.S. Treasury has worked to place her portrait on the $20 bill, making her the first African American and the first woman to be featured on U.S. paper currency. This honor reflects a growing recognition that American history includes heroes of all backgrounds.

Tubman's Underground Railroad routes are now part of the National Park Service's Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Historical Park in Maryland and the Harriet Tubman National Historical Park in Auburn, New York. These sites help new generations understand the courage it took to resist slavery and fight for justice.

Her example continues to inspire modern freedom movements around the world. Human trafficking affects millions of people globally, and modern abolitionists — social workers, law enforcement officers, and volunteers — work every day to help victims escape exploitation. Like Tubman, they operate networks of safe houses, provide resources and support, and guide vulnerable people toward safety.

Harriet Tubman proved that one determined person, armed with nothing but courage and conviction, can change the course of history. Her life challenges each of us to ask: what am I willing to risk for what is right?
