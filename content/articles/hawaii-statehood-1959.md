On August 21, 1959, President Dwight Eisenhower signed the proclamation admitting Hawaii as the 50th state of the United States. In Honolulu, sirens wailed, church bells rang, and jubilant crowds flooded the streets. It was a moment of celebration — and the culmination of one of the most extraordinary and contested political transformations in American history.

## The Kingdom

Before it was an American state, Hawaii was a sovereign nation. The Kingdom of Hawaii, unified under King Kamehameha I in 1810, was internationally recognized, maintained diplomatic relations with major powers, and was governed by a constitutional monarchy. It was the most politically sophisticated indigenous state in the Pacific.

American influence grew through the 19th century as missionaries arrived and their descendants became powerful sugar planters. The planter class — predominantly white Americans — accumulated vast landholdings and economic power while pressing for closer ties to the United States, whose markets were essential for Hawaiian sugar exports.

## The Overthrow

On January 17, 1893, a group of American and European businessmen, supported by 162 U.S. Marines from the USS Boston who had been landed at the request of U.S. Minister John Stevens, overthrew Queen Liliuokalani. The Queen surrendered under protest, "to the superior force of the United States of America," expecting that the U.S. government would investigate and restore her throne.

President Grover Cleveland did investigate. His special envoy, James Blount, concluded that the overthrow would not have succeeded without the involvement of American military forces. Cleveland called the overthrow an "act of war" and attempted to restore the Queen. But the provisional government — led by Sanford Dole — refused to step down, and Cleveland lacked the political will to use force.

## Annexation

The Republic of Hawaii, declared in 1894 with Dole as president, immediately sought annexation by the United States. It was not until 1898 — during the Spanish-American War, when Hawaii's strategic value as a Pacific military base became obvious — that Congress passed a joint resolution of annexation. Hawaii became a U.S. territory in 1900.

As a territory, Hawaii was governed by a presidentially appointed governor. Its residents were U.S. citizens but could not vote for president or for voting members of Congress. The sugar plantations — and later pineapple plantations — dominated the economy, relying on imported labor from Japan, China, the Philippines, and Portugal.

## Pearl Harbor and the Path to Statehood

The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, demonstrated Hawaii's strategic importance and tied the islands irrevocably to the American national story. Hawaii was placed under martial law for most of the war. Japanese Americans in Hawaii, who made up roughly 37 percent of the population, were largely spared the mass internment that befell their mainland counterparts — partly because interning a third of Hawaii's population would have collapsed the territory's economy.

After the war, Hawaii's campaign for statehood intensified. Veterans of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team — the all-Japanese American unit that became the most decorated unit in U.S. military history — returned home and entered politics, bringing a new generation of leadership that demanded full citizenship rights.

Opposition to statehood came from multiple directions. Southern Democrats feared that Hawaii's multiracial population would add votes for civil rights legislation. Some mainland politicians questioned whether a non-contiguous territory with a majority-nonwhite population could be a "real" state. Labor opponents worried about Hawaii's powerful union movement.

Statehood finally passed Congress in 1959. In a plebiscite, Hawaiians voted 17 to 1 in favor. On August 21, Eisenhower signed the proclamation, and Hawaii became the 50th state.

## The Sovereignty Movement

For Native Hawaiians, statehood represented the final step in a process that began with the illegal overthrow of their queen. In 1993 — the centennial of the overthrow — Congress passed the Apology Resolution, acknowledging that "the overthrow of the Kingdom of Hawaii occurred with the active participation of agents and citizens of the United States" and expressing "its deep regret to the Native Hawaiian people."

The Hawaiian sovereignty movement continues to advocate for some form of self-determination for Native Hawaiians, ranging from federal recognition as an indigenous nation (similar to Native American tribes) to full independence.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Hawaii's path to statehood forces us to ask hard questions about how "inclusion" can follow exclusion. The United States overthrew a sovereign government, annexed the territory, and then — 61 years later — offered its residents a vote on joining the union that had taken them in the first place. Genuine inclusion requires acknowledging the history that precedes it. When you invite someone to the table, be honest about why they were not there before.
