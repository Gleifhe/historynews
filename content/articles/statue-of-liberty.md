On October 28, 1886, a foggy autumn day in New York Harbor, President Grover Cleveland stood before a crowd of dignitaries, soldiers, and citizens to dedicate the most extraordinary diplomatic gift in history. Rising 151 feet above a massive granite pedestal on what was then called Bedloe's Island, the Statue of Liberty — officially titled *Liberty Enlightening the World* — held her torch high above the harbor that served as America's front door.

## A French Idea

The statue was born over dinner in 1865. Édouard de Laboulaye, a French political thinker and admirer of American democracy, proposed that France give the United States a monument to celebrate liberty and the friendship between the two nations. Laboulaye was motivated partly by idealism and partly by politics — he hoped a monument to American freedom would inspire democratic reform in France, which was then under the authoritarian rule of Napoleon III.

The sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi embraced the project with passion. He envisioned a colossal neoclassical figure — a robed woman holding a torch and a tablet inscribed with the date of the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/): JULY IV MDCCLXXVI. The statue's internal iron framework was designed by Alexandre Gustave Eiffel, who would later build the famous tower in Paris.

## Building Liberty

The statue was constructed in France and shipped to America in 350 individual pieces packed in 214 crates. The copper skin — just 3/32 of an inch thick, about the thickness of two pennies — was shaped using a technique called repoussé, in which craftsmen hammered sheets of copper over wooden molds.

The French paid for the statue; the Americans were responsible for the pedestal. Raising money for the pedestal proved embarrassingly difficult. Congress refused to fund it. Wealthy New Yorkers were indifferent. The project stalled.

Joseph Pulitzer, publisher of the *New York World*, launched a fundraising campaign that shamed the wealthy and appealed to ordinary citizens. He published the name of every donor, no matter how small the contribution. Schoolchildren sent pennies. A woman donated "sixty cents, the result of self-denial." Within five months, over 120,000 people had contributed, raising over $100,000 (roughly $3.2 million in 2026 dollars). The pedestal was completed, and the statue was assembled.

## The New Colossus

In 1883, poet Emma Lazarus wrote a sonnet titled "The New Colossus" to help raise funds for the pedestal. It included the now-famous lines: "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free, / The wretched refuse of your teeming shore. / Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me, / I lift my lamp beside the golden door!"

The poem was largely forgotten after the fundraising campaign. It was not inscribed on the statue until 1903, when a bronze plaque bearing the text was mounted inside the pedestal. Over time, Lazarus's words became inseparable from the statue's meaning, transforming it from a symbol of Franco-American friendship into a universal beacon for immigrants and refugees.

## A Symbol That Grows

For the millions of immigrants who entered the United States through nearby Ellis Island between 1892 and 1954, the Statue of Liberty was literally the first thing they saw of their new country. The statue became an embodiment of the American promise — not just for the people who were already here, but for those arriving with nothing but hope.

The statue was designated a National Monument in 1924 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. After a major restoration completed in 1986, she remains one of the most visited landmarks in the world.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Statue of Liberty was funded not by governments or millionaires but by ordinary people who believed in what it represented. Schoolchildren, immigrants, and working families gave what they could. The lesson is clear: you do not need to make a grand gesture to contribute to something greater than yourself. Small contributions, made with conviction, add up to something monumental.
