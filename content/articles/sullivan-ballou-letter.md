## The Letter That Was Never Sent

On a Sunday evening in July 1861, Sullivan Ballou sat down to write to his wife. He was 32 years old, a lawyer from Smithfield, Rhode Island, and a major in the 2nd Rhode Island Infantry Volunteers. His regiment was camped near Washington, D.C., preparing to march south toward a place called Manassas Junction, Virginia. Everyone knew a battle was coming. It would be the first major engagement of a war that both sides still believed would be short.

Ballou knew he might not survive. And so he wrote a letter — to Sarah, his wife of five years, and to their two young sons, Edgar and Willie. It was a letter about love, about duty, and about the unbearable weight of choosing between the two. He never mailed it. The letter was found among his belongings after his death, and it would become one of the most celebrated documents in American history.

## A Lawyer Goes to War

Sullivan Ballou's path to the battlefield was, in many ways, typical of the men who volunteered in the spring of 1861. He was born in Smithfield, Rhode Island, in 1829. Orphaned young — his father died when he was six, his mother when he was eleven — Ballou was raised by relatives and worked his way through Phillips Academy in Andover, Massachusetts, and then Brown University, graduating in 1852. He studied law at the National Law School in Ballston, New York, and established a practice in Providence.

He married Sarah Hunt Shumway in 1855. By all accounts, it was a deeply affectionate marriage. They had two sons: Edgar, born in 1856, and Willie, born in 1858. Ballou served in the Rhode Island legislature and was elected speaker of the state House of Representatives. He had a promising legal and political career ahead of him.

When the Confederacy fired on Fort Sumter in April 1861, Ballou enlisted as a major in the 2nd Rhode Island. He was not a career soldier. He was a husband, a father, and a civic leader who believed the Union had to be preserved. Like hundreds of thousands of other men on both sides, he left behind everything he loved because he felt he had no choice.

## "My Very Dear Sarah"

The letter Ballou wrote on July 14, 1861, runs to about 600 words. It is at once intensely personal and deeply philosophical — the work of a man who has accepted that he is likely to die and is trying to make sense of that acceptance.

"My very dear Sarah," he began. "The indications are very strong that we shall move in a few days — perhaps tomorrow. Lest I should not be able to write again, I feel impelled to write a few lines that may fall under your eye when I shall be no more."

What followed was a meditation on the competing claims of private love and public duty. Ballou wrote of his devotion to Sarah in terms that were achingly direct: "Sarah my love for you is deathless, it seems to bind me with mighty cables that nothing but Omnipotence could break; and yet my love of Country comes over me like a strong wind and bears me unresistibly on with all these chains to the battle field."

He acknowledged the cruelty of what he was about to do — leaving his wife and children, possibly forever. "I have no misgivings about, or lack of confidence in the cause in which I am engaged, and my courage does not halt or falter. I know how strongly American Civilization now leans on the triumph of the Government and how great a debt we owe to those who went before us through the blood and sufferings of the Revolution."

And then the letter turned back to love: "But, O Sarah! if the dead can come back to this earth and flit unseen around those they loved, I shall always be near you; in the gladdest days and in the darkest nights ... always, always, and if there be a soft breeze upon your cheek, it shall be my breath, as the cool air fans your throbbing temple, it shall be my spirit passing by."

## One Week Later

On July 21, 1861, the 2nd Rhode Island marched into the First Battle of Bull Run — known in the South as First Manassas. It was the first major battle of the Civil War, and both sides expected it to be decisive. Crowds of civilians from Washington, D.C., brought picnic baskets and opera glasses to watch from nearby hillsides, expecting a quick Union victory.

What they witnessed was chaos. The battle seesawed through the morning before Confederate reinforcements arrived by train and overwhelmed the Union flank. The Union retreat turned into a rout. Soldiers, civilians, and wagons clogged the road back to Washington in a panicked mass.

Sullivan Ballou was struck by a cannonball during the fighting. The impact took off his right leg. He was carried to a field hospital, where he died approximately one week later, on July 28, 1861. He was 32 years old.

His body was later disinterred and burned by Confederate soldiers — an act of desecration that outraged the North when it became known. Sarah Ballou, who never remarried, lived until 1917. She was 80 years old when she died, having spent 56 years as a widow.

## A Letter Rediscovered

Ballou's letter might have remained a private family document if not for Ken Burns's 1990 documentary, *The Civil War*. Burns featured the letter prominently in the first episode, read aloud over images of battlefields and period photographs. The combination of Ballou's words and Jay Ungar's haunting fiddle tune "Ashokan Farewell" created one of the most emotionally powerful moments in documentary film history.

The letter resonated with millions of viewers because it gave voice to something universal — the conflict between love and duty, between wanting to stay and knowing you must go. Every soldier who has ever kissed a spouse goodbye before a deployment has felt what Ballou felt. Every family that has waited for news from a war zone understands what Sarah endured.

The men who would later fall at [Gettysburg](/articles/battle-of-gettysburg/) and at dozens of other battles surely wrote similar letters — most of which have been lost. Ballou's survived, and in surviving, it speaks for all of them.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Sullivan Ballou's letter is not just a historical document. It's a model of emotional honesty. In an era when men were expected to be stoic and composed, Ballou wrote with startling vulnerability about his fear, his love, and his sense of duty. He didn't pretend to be unafraid. He didn't hide behind clichés. He said exactly what he felt, knowing these might be his last words.

That kind of honesty takes courage — a different kind of courage from what Ballou showed on the battlefield, but courage nonetheless. Telling the people you love how much they mean to you, especially when it feels uncomfortable or unnecessary, is one of the simplest and most important things a person can do. Ballou's letter endures because he did it when it mattered most.

His letter also reminds us that the people who live through history are not abstractions. They are husbands and wives, parents and children, people with hopes and fears just like ours. When we study the Civil War, it's easy to get lost in strategy and statistics. Ballou's letter brings us back to the human cost — and reminds us that behind every casualty number is a family that was broken.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, military families still face the same impossible choices that Sullivan and Sarah Ballou faced in 1861. Deployments still separate parents from children, spouses from each other, and individuals from the lives they built in peacetime. The technology has changed — soldiers now video-call their families from thousands of miles away — but the emotional weight of goodbye has not.

Ballou's letter also speaks to a deeper question about what we owe to each other and to our communities. In a culture that often emphasizes individual achievement and personal fulfillment, Ballou's willingness to sacrifice everything for a cause larger than himself can feel almost foreign. But the principle he articulated — that some things are worth more than personal happiness — remains as relevant as ever.

Every year on [Memorial Day](/articles/birth-of-decoration-day/), Americans pause to honor the men and women who died in military service. Sullivan Ballou is one of them. His letter reminds us that they were not statistics. They were people who loved, and were loved, and who gave up everything they cherished because they believed the cause demanded it. The least we can do is remember their names.
