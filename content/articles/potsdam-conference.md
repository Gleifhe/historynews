On July 17, 1945, three men sat down at a round table in Cecilienhof Palace in Potsdam, a suburb of Berlin. Harry Truman, president of the United States for barely three months. Joseph Stalin, dictator of the Soviet Union. Winston Churchill, prime minister of Britain (who would be replaced mid-conference by Clement Attlee after losing a general election). Behind them lay a ruined continent. Ahead lay the decisions that would shape the next half-century.

## The Context

The war in Europe was over. Germany had surrendered unconditionally on May 8. The war in the Pacific continued, though Japan was clearly losing. The atomic bomb had been successfully tested at Alamogordo, New Mexico, on July 16 — the day before the conference opened. Truman knew about the bomb. Stalin, through his spies, knew too. Neither acknowledged it directly.

The conference was the third and final meeting of the "Big Three" wartime allies, following Tehran (1943) and Yalta (February 1945). But the dynamics had shifted. Roosevelt was dead. Churchill was about to lose power. Truman, a newcomer on the world stage, faced the most experienced and ruthless leader of the 20th century across the negotiating table.

## The Decisions

**Germany**: The leaders confirmed the division of Germany into four occupation zones and agreed on the principles of denazification, demilitarization, and democratization. They agreed that Germany would pay reparations — primarily to the Soviet Union, which had suffered the greatest destruction — but could not agree on a total amount. Each occupying power would extract reparations from its own zone.

**Eastern Europe**: Potsdam effectively ratified Soviet control of Eastern Europe. Poland's borders were redrawn — shifted dramatically westward, giving the Soviet Union large portions of eastern Poland while compensating Poland with former German territory. Millions of Germans were expelled from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary in what the conference delicately called "orderly and humane" population transfers. The transfers were, in practice, brutal.

**Japan**: The Potsdam Declaration, issued on July 26, demanded Japan's unconditional surrender and warned of "prompt and utter destruction" if Japan refused. The declaration did not mention the atomic bomb specifically. Japan rejected the ultimatum. Nine days later, the United States dropped the atomic bomb on Hiroshima.

## The Lines Are Drawn

Potsdam is often described as the beginning of the Cold War, though the tensions had been building for months. The conference revealed fundamental disagreements about the future of Europe. Stalin wanted a buffer zone of friendly (meaning Soviet-controlled) states on his western border. Truman and Churchill wanted free elections and democratic governments. These goals were incompatible.

The compromises reached at Potsdam were less agreements than acknowledgments of the reality on the ground. The Red Army occupied Eastern Europe. Short of going to war with the Soviet Union — which no one was prepared to do — the Western powers had limited leverage. The conference papered over disagreements that would erupt into open confrontation within two years.

## The American Position

For the United States, Potsdam marked a turning point. At Tehran and Yalta, Roosevelt had worked to maintain the wartime alliance with Stalin, believing that cooperation was possible. Truman arrived at Potsdam with fewer illusions. The possession of the atomic bomb — which Truman obliquely mentioned to Stalin on July 24 — gave the United States confidence that it could pursue its interests from a position of strength.

The decisions at Potsdam established the framework for the American postwar role: the occupation and reconstruction of Germany and Japan, the containment of Soviet expansion, and the construction of a Western alliance system backed by American military and economic power.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Potsdam reminds us that negotiations reveal priorities, not just positions. Truman, Stalin, and Churchill each came to the table with goals shaped by their nations' experiences, fears, and ambitions. The agreements they reached reflected the balance of power, not abstract principles of justice. When you negotiate — for a job, a contract, a compromise in a relationship — understand that the other party's position is shaped by forces you may not fully see. Effective negotiation requires understanding not just what someone wants, but why they want it.
