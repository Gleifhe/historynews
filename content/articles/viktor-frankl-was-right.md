## Nine Words That Survived Auschwitz

In 1942, Viktor Frankl — a 37-year-old Viennese psychiatrist — was deported to the Theresienstadt concentration camp. Over the next three years, he would be moved through four camps, including Auschwitz. His pregnant wife, Tilly, was murdered. His mother was killed in the gas chambers. His father died of starvation and pneumonia. His brother was worked to death. By the time the camps were [liberated in 1945](/articles/liberation-of-auschwitz/), Frankl had lost almost everyone he loved.

He had also made an observation that would change the field of psychology forever.

In the camps, Frankl noticed a pattern. The prisoners who survived longest were not necessarily the strongest or the youngest. They were the ones who held onto a sense of purpose — a reason to endure. Some lived for the hope of seeing a loved one again. Some lived to complete unfinished work. Some lived simply to bear witness, to make sure the world would know what happened. What they shared was not optimism. Many had no rational basis for hope. What they shared was meaning.

After liberation, Frankl wrote *Man's Search for Meaning* in just nine days. Published in 1946, the book distilled his camp experience into a single thesis, borrowed from Nietzsche and proven in the most extreme conditions imaginable: "Those who have a 'why' to live can bear almost any 'how.'"

The book has sold over 16 million copies worldwide. It has been translated into more than 50 languages. In a 1991 Library of Congress survey, readers named it one of the ten most influential books in America. And in 2026, nearly 80 years after Frankl wrote it, his insight sits at the center of how the Department of Veterans Affairs treats the psychological wounds of war.

## Historical Context

Frankl was already a practicing psychiatrist before the war. He had studied under Alfred Adler and Sigmund Freud, the giants of Viennese psychoanalysis, and had developed his own ideas about human motivation before Auschwitz confirmed them. Where Freud argued that humans are driven primarily by pleasure and Adler argued they are driven by power, Frankl proposed a third drive: the will to meaning. Humans, he believed, are fundamentally purpose-seeking creatures. When that search for meaning is frustrated, they suffer psychologically — not because of the absence of pleasure, but because of the absence of purpose.

Frankl called his therapeutic approach "logotherapy," from the Greek *logos*, meaning "reason" or "purpose." Logotherapy does not promise happiness. It does not eliminate suffering. It helps people find a reason to endure suffering — and in that reason, find the strength to keep going.

The [American POWs in the Hanoi Hilton](/articles/hanoi-hilton-pows/) demonstrated Frankl's thesis with devastating clarity. Men like Admiral James Stockdale, who spent over seven years in captivity and endured repeated torture, explicitly credited their survival to a sense of purpose — duty to country, responsibility to fellow prisoners, determination to resist. Stockdale, a student of Stoic philosophy who had read Frankl, later said that the prisoners who didn't make it were often the optimists — the ones who kept expecting rescue by a specific date. When the date passed, their hope collapsed. The survivors were the ones who faced the brutal reality of their situation while maintaining an unshakeable commitment to something larger than their own comfort.

## The Core Lesson: Meaning Is Not Found — It Is Chosen

Frankl's most radical claim was that meaning is not a luxury. It is a necessity. And unlike happiness, which depends on circumstances, meaning can be found in any circumstance — including the worst ones.

He identified three sources of meaning that are available to every human being:

1. **Creative values** — meaning through what you give to the world: your work, your art, your contribution.
2. **Experiential values** — meaning through what you receive from the world: love, beauty, truth, connection.
3. **Attitudinal values** — meaning through the stance you take toward unavoidable suffering.

The third category is the one that matters most in extremity. When you cannot change your circumstances — when you are in a prison camp, a hospital bed, or a situation you cannot escape — you still have one freedom left: the freedom to choose your attitude. Frankl called this "the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way."

This is not a platitude. Frankl watched it save lives and watched its absence kill. Prisoners who lost their sense of meaning — who could not answer the question "Why should I keep going?" — deteriorated rapidly. They stopped eating. They stopped moving. They gave up. Frankl called it "existential vacuum": a state of inner emptiness that is more lethal than physical privation.

## Real-World Examples

The VA's adoption of Frankl's principles began in earnest in the 2000s, as clinicians grappled with the surge of PTSD cases from the Iraq and Afghanistan wars. Traditional treatments — exposure therapy, cognitive processing therapy — focused on reducing symptoms. They worked. But clinicians noticed that some veterans who achieved significant symptom reduction still felt lost. The nightmares had faded, but the emptiness remained. They no longer had a mission.

Meaning-centered interventions, rooted in Frankl's logotherapy, address this emptiness directly. They help veterans answer three questions: What do I value? What am I responsible for? What can I contribute? The answers don't have to be grand. A veteran who finds meaning in mentoring younger veterans, coaching youth sports, or building furniture in a workshop is engaged in the same process Frankl described — giving life a purpose that makes endurance worthwhile.

Research by Steven Southwick and Dennis Charney at Yale, published in their book *Resilience: The Science of Mastering Life's Greatest Challenges*, confirmed Frankl's insight with modern neuroscience and longitudinal data. They studied former POWs, Special Forces soldiers, and civilian trauma survivors and found that a strong sense of purpose was one of the most reliable predictors of resilience and recovery. Purpose didn't prevent trauma. It prevented trauma from becoming the end of the story.

Organizations like The Mission Continues, founded by former Navy SEAL Eric Greitens, are built on Frankl's insight. The organization channels veterans into community service — not as charity, but as purpose. Veterans who serve report a sense of mission and meaning that mirrors what they experienced in uniform. The uniform is gone. The meaning doesn't have to be.

## Personal Growth

Frankl's message is not limited to extreme suffering. It applies to the ordinary difficulties of everyday life — a career setback, a broken relationship, a health scare, a period of uncertainty. His central question is always the same: What is this experience asking of me?

Most people ask a different question when something goes wrong: Why is this happening to me? Frankl would say that question is backwards. Life is not obligated to give you reasons. But you are obligated — to yourself — to find them. Not to justify the suffering. To use it.

The practical application is deceptively simple. When you are going through a difficult period, stop asking what you can get out of the situation and start asking what the situation is asking of you. Maybe it's asking for patience. Maybe it's asking for courage. Maybe it's asking you to help someone else who is going through something worse. The shift from "What do I need?" to "What is needed of me?" is the shift Frankl described — and it is available to anyone, in any circumstance, at any time.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the veteran mental health crisis remains one of the most urgent challenges in American healthcare. The VA has made significant strides in expanding access to care, reducing wait times, and destigmatizing treatment. But the deepest problem many veterans face is not a clinical diagnosis. It is a meaning gap — the loss of the intense, purpose-driven life that military service provided.

Frankl would recognize this immediately. The veteran who has lost their mission is experiencing the same existential vacuum he observed in the camps — not in the same degree, but in the same kind. The solution is the same too: not to eliminate the suffering, but to find a reason that makes the suffering bearable.

Viktor Frankl died in 1997 at the age of 92. He spent his life teaching, writing, and practicing the insight he forged in Auschwitz. His message has not aged because it addresses something timeless in human nature: the need for meaning. He was right in 1946. He is right in 2026. And the veterans who live his insight every day are the proof.
