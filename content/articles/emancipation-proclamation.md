## A War Changes Its Purpose

When the Civil War began in April 1861, President Abraham Lincoln insisted it was about one thing: saving the Union. He was not fighting to end slavery — at least not publicly. Lincoln worried that if he made the war about slavery, the border states (slave states that had stayed in the Union) would leave and join the Confederacy.

But as the war dragged on, Lincoln's thinking changed. Enslaved people were fleeing to Union lines by the thousands. They were building Confederate fortifications and growing food for Confederate soldiers. Slavery wasn't just a moral issue — it was a military one.

By the summer of 1862, Lincoln had made up his mind.

## The Proclamation

On September 22, 1862, Lincoln issued a preliminary proclamation warning the Confederate states: if they did not return to the Union by January 1, 1863, their enslaved people would be "forever free."

The Confederacy ignored the warning.

On January 1, 1863, Lincoln signed the final Emancipation Proclamation. It declared that all enslaved people in the rebel states "are, and henceforward shall be free."

## What It Did — and What It Didn't Do

The Emancipation Proclamation is often misunderstood. Here's what it actually did:

**What it did:**
- Declared free all enslaved people in the Confederate states
- Allowed Black men to serve in the Union Army and Navy
- Transformed the war into a fight for human freedom

**What it did NOT do:**
- It did not free enslaved people in the border states (Missouri, Kentucky, Maryland, and Delaware) that remained in the Union
- It could not immediately be enforced in Confederate territory, since those areas were controlled by the enemy
- It did not permanently abolish slavery — that required the 13th Amendment in 1865

## Black Soldiers Join the Fight

One of the most important provisions of the proclamation was its authorization of Black military service. By the end of the war, approximately 180,000 Black men had served in the Union Army and 18,000 in the Navy.

The most famous Black regiment was the 54th Massachusetts Infantry, whose courageous assault on Fort Wagner in July 1863 was later depicted in the film *Glory*. Black soldiers fought in over 400 engagements and 39 major battles.

## "A Fit and Necessary War Measure"

Lincoln justified the Emancipation Proclamation as a military measure — a "fit and necessary war measure" authorized by his powers as Commander in Chief. He did this deliberately. He knew the proclamation would face legal challenges, and a military justification was harder to overturn than a moral one.

Lincoln himself called the Emancipation Proclamation "the central act of my administration, and the greatest event of the nineteenth century."

## The Document Today

The original Emancipation Proclamation is preserved at the National Archives in Washington, D.C. The document's ink has faded significantly over the years — Lincoln's signature is barely visible. Because of its fragility, the original is displayed only rarely and under carefully controlled lighting conditions.

Despite its faded ink, the words it contains changed the course of American history. The Emancipation Proclamation turned the Civil War into a revolution — and put the United States on the path toward the 13th Amendment, which permanently abolished slavery in December 1865.
