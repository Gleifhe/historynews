## "Retreat? Hell, We Just Got Here"

By the spring of 1918, the war was going badly for the Allies. Russia had collapsed, freeing fifty German divisions to transfer to the Western Front. In late May, the Germans launched a massive offensive along the Chemin des Dames and advanced with terrifying speed toward Paris. French units fell back in disarray. For the first time since 1914, the German army was within striking distance of the French capital.

Into this crisis marched the U.S. 2nd Division, which included the 4th Marine Brigade — the 5th and 6th Marine Regiments with the 6th Machine Gun Battalion. These men had been in France for months but had not yet seen serious combat. That was about to change.

On June 1, the Marines took up positions near the Paris-Metz highway, just west of a dense hunting preserve called Belleau Wood. French soldiers streaming to the rear urged the Americans to retreat with them. According to Marine legend, Captain Lloyd Williams of the 51st Company replied: "Retreat? Hell, we just got here."

## Into the Wheat Fields

The battle opened on June 6, 1918 — a date that would gain even greater significance twenty-six years later on the beaches of Normandy. The Marines were ordered to attack across an open wheat field toward the German-held tree line of Belleau Wood. It was a frontal assault against entrenched machine guns, and it was a slaughter.

The Germans had fortified the wood with machine gun nests positioned to create interlocking fields of fire. They had clear sight lines across the golden wheat. As the Marines advanced in formation across the open ground — some officers walking upright with swagger sticks, in the tradition of the Corps — German Maxim guns opened up at a range of under 400 yards.

First Sergeant Dan Daly, already a two-time Medal of Honor recipient, reportedly rallied his men with the shout: "Come on, you sons of bitches, do you want to live forever?" The line surged forward.

The casualties that first day were catastrophic. The 5th Marines suffered more killed and wounded on June 6 than the entire Marine Corps had suffered in any single day of its 143-year history up to that point. The toll was estimated at over 1,000 casualties in a single afternoon. Yet the Marines reached the wood and began clawing their way inside.

## Three Weeks in Hell

What followed was not a single battle but a grinding, three-week campaign fought tree by tree, boulder by boulder, and trench by trench. Belleau Wood was roughly one square mile of ancient oaks, dense undergrowth, and rocky ravines — perfect terrain for defense and nightmarish for attack. The Germans had constructed bunkers, communication trenches, and concealed firing positions throughout.

The fighting was intensely personal. Rifle fire, bayonets, grenades, and bare fists were the weapons of Belleau Wood. Visibility was often measured in yards. Units became disoriented in the tangle of shattered trees. Men fought in the same positions for days, with the dead and wounded lying where they fell because evacuation was impossible under fire.

The Marines attacked, were driven back, attacked again, lost ground, and attacked again. Between June 6 and June 26, the wood changed hands six times. Each assault produced a new list of casualties. The sweet smell of the wheat fields gave way to the stench of cordite, blood, and death.

Gunnery Sergeant Ernest Janson — later known as Charles Hoffman — earned the Medal of Honor on June 6 for single-handedly bayoneting two German soldiers who were operating a machine gun nest, capturing the position. Private John Kelly received the Medal of Honor for running through machine gun fire to attack a gun position with hand grenades. These were acts of desperation as much as bravery.

## The Cost

When the battle ended on June 26 — the final German defenders cleared by a combined Marine and Army assault — the toll was devastating. Approximately 1,811 Marines were killed in action. Total Marine casualties, including wounded and missing, exceeded 5,000. Some companies had been virtually wiped out and rebuilt with replacements multiple times during the three weeks.

The Germans had also suffered heavily, with an estimated 1,600 killed and over 1,600 captured. But Belleau Wood was not a strategic masterstroke. Military historians have debated whether the wood was worth taking at all. It was a small patch of forest with no particular tactical value. The Marines could have bypassed it and achieved the same objective — blocking the German advance on Paris — with far fewer casualties.

But the battle's value was measured in something other than terrain. It proved that American troops could fight — and fight ferociously — against the best the German army could throw at them. It shattered any remaining German illusion that the Americans were unprepared amateurs.

## "Teufel Hunden"

According to Marine Corps tradition, German intelligence reports after Belleau Wood referred to the Marines as "Teufel Hunden" — Devil Dogs. Whether the name originated from an actual German report or was embellished by American journalists is a matter of historical debate. Linguists have noted that the proper German would be "Teufelshunde." Regardless of its precise origin, the name stuck and became one of the most enduring symbols of the Marine Corps.

The French government renamed the wood "Bois de la Brigade de Marine" — Wood of the Marine Brigade — in honor of the Americans who fought there. It remains one of only a handful of places in the world that carry the Marine Corps name.

The Marines who fought at Belleau Wood were part of a larger American Expeditionary Force that included units like the [Harlem Hellfighters of the 369th Infantry Regiment](/articles/harlem-hellfighters/), African American soldiers who fought with distinction under French command. Together, these units transformed the war and signaled America's emergence as a global military power — a role that would reach its fullest expression on another June 6, when Allied forces stormed the [beaches of Normandy in 1944](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/).

## Personal Growth: The Price of Perseverance

Belleau Wood is a story about perseverance — and about the cost of it. The Marines did not take the wood on the first try, or the second, or the third. They took it because they kept going back. That persistence is admirable, but the story also forces an honest question: at what point does determination become stubbornness? Military historians argue that the frontal assaults were tactically unnecessary. The lesson is not simply "never give up," but "never give up thinking, either."

In your own life, persistence matters enormously. Most meaningful achievements — learning a skill, building a relationship, finishing a difficult project — require doing hard things repeatedly. But persistence without reflection is just repetition. The Marines at Belleau Wood eventually adapted their tactics, using small-unit infiltration and coordinated fire instead of massed charges. The ones who survived learned to be both brave and smart.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Marine Corps still sends recruits to visit the Aisne-Marne American Cemetery near Belleau Wood, where more than 2,000 American war dead are buried. The rows of white crosses are a reminder that the values we celebrate — courage, sacrifice, loyalty — come with a price that is measured in human lives. Understanding that price is not glorifying war. It is respecting the people who paid it, and resolving to make decisions worthy of their sacrifice. Every generation faces its own version of Belleau Wood — a moment when the easy path is retreat and the hard path is forward. The Marines who walked into those wheat fields on June 6, 1918, made their choice. Their example asks us what we would choose.
