On July 1, 1944, while Allied troops were still fighting their way through the hedgerows of Normandy, 730 delegates from 44 nations arrived at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. For three weeks, they negotiated the framework for a new international economic order. The system they created would make the United States dollar the center of global finance — a position it still holds more than 80 years later.

## Why Bretton Woods?

The conference was driven by a shared fear: that the economic chaos of the 1930s must never be repeated. The Great Depression had triggered competitive currency devaluations, trade wars, and protectionist tariffs that strangled international commerce and contributed directly to the rise of fascism. The delegates at Bretton Woods believed that economic instability was the root cause of political extremism and, ultimately, war.

The United States and Britain dominated the negotiations, represented by two of the most brilliant economists of the century: Harry Dexter White of the U.S. Treasury and John Maynard Keynes of Britain. Their visions clashed. Keynes wanted a new international currency managed by a global institution. White wanted the U.S. dollar at the center. White won. America was funding the war and producing half the world's GDP. He who pays the piper calls the tune.

## The System

The Bretton Woods Agreement established three pillars:

**The International Monetary Fund (IMF)** would oversee international exchange rates and provide short-term loans to countries with balance-of-payments problems. Its purpose was to prevent the competitive devaluations that had wrecked the 1930s economy.

**The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (the World Bank)** would provide long-term loans for reconstruction and development — initially for war-ravaged Europe, later for developing nations worldwide.

**The Dollar-Gold Standard** pegged the U.S. dollar to gold at $35 per ounce, and all other currencies were pegged to the dollar. This meant that the dollar became, in effect, the world's money. Every international transaction, every oil purchase, every trade deal ultimately referenced the dollar.

## The Hidden Empire

Bretton Woods did not look like empire. There were no conquered territories, no colonial governors, no occupying armies. But the system gave the United States structural power that traditional empires could only dream of. Because the dollar was the world's reserve currency, the United States could run deficits that would bankrupt any other country. Because the IMF and World Bank were headquartered in Washington and effectively controlled by American voting power, the United States could shape economic policy around the globe.

Countries that needed IMF loans had to accept conditions — open their markets, reduce government spending, privatize state industries. These conditions, known later as "structural adjustment," reshaped economies from Latin America to Southeast Asia along lines that generally favored American commercial interests.

The system was not imposed by force. It was adopted voluntarily by nations that wanted access to the stability and prosperity that the American-led order provided. But voluntary participation in a system designed around American interests was still participation in an American system.

## The System Evolves

In 1971, President Richard Nixon ended the dollar's convertibility to gold — the "Nixon Shock" — effectively ending the original Bretton Woods system. But the institutions survived. The IMF and World Bank remain powerful. The dollar remains the world's reserve currency. More than 80 years after Bretton Woods, roughly 60 percent of global foreign exchange reserves are held in dollars.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Bretton Woods shows that the most durable power is structural, not coercive. The United States did not force the world to use the dollar at gunpoint. It created a system so useful that everyone chose to participate — and that choice gave the United States influence that no army could have achieved. If you want lasting influence in your career, community, or field, do not seek to control others. Build something so valuable that people choose to work within your framework.
