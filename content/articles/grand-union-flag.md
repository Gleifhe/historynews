On January 1, 1776, a new flag rose above Prospect Hill near George Washington's headquarters in Cambridge, Massachusetts. It had thirteen alternating red and white stripes — representing the thirteen colonies — but in the upper left corner, where Americans today expect to see stars on a blue field, there was the British Union Jack. This was the Grand Union Flag, and it perfectly captured the confused state of American identity in early 1776.

## A Nation Between Two Worlds

In January 1776, the colonies were at war with Britain but had not yet declared independence. Many colonists — including some members of the Continental Congress — still hoped for reconciliation with the Crown. They were fighting for their rights as British subjects, not for a new nation. The Grand Union Flag reflected this ambiguity: it announced colonial unity with its thirteen stripes while maintaining allegiance to Britain with the Union Jack.

The flag was sometimes called the Continental Colors or the Congress Colors. No one is entirely sure who designed it or who ordered it made. Like much of the Revolution's early history, the details were not well documented.

## Washington's Flag

George Washington raised the Grand Union Flag as the new banner of the Continental Army. The timing was significant — January 1, 1776, was also the day the Continental Army was officially reorganized under Washington's command. The new flag was intended to signal a new beginning.

There was an awkward misunderstanding. British observers in Boston, seeing the Union Jack on the flag, assumed it was a signal of surrender or submission. Washington was mortified when he learned of the confusion. In a letter, he acknowledged that the flag's design sent a mixed message.

The incident highlighted a problem that would only grow more pressing as 1776 progressed. If the colonies were going to fight Britain, they needed a flag that did not include Britain's cross.

## Six Months of Revolution

The Grand Union Flag served for roughly eighteen months, from January 1776 until June 1777, when the Continental Congress passed the [Flag Resolution](/articles/flag-resolution-1777/) establishing the Stars and Stripes. During that time, the political landscape shifted dramatically. Thomas Paine published *Common Sense* in January 1776, arguing forcefully for complete independence. The Declaration of Independence followed on July 4, 1776. By the time the Stars and Stripes replaced the Grand Union Flag, the idea of reconciliation with Britain was dead.

The Grand Union Flag remains historically significant as a transitional symbol — a snapshot of a moment when Americans were not yet sure who they were becoming.

## A Flag Without a Record

No original Grand Union Flag is known to have survived. Unlike the Star-Spangled Banner, which was deliberately preserved as a relic, the Grand Union Flag was a working military banner that was used, worn out, and discarded or repurposed once the Stars and Stripes replaced it. No congressional resolution authorized the Grand Union Flag — no official record documents who designed it, who ordered it made, or how many were produced. Historians have pieced together its story from letters, ship logs, and contemporary accounts. Washington mentioned the flag-raising at Prospect Hill in his correspondence, and British observers in Boston noted the new banner in their reports. The East India Company's flag — which also featured red and white stripes with the Union Jack — has led some scholars to theorize that the Grand Union Flag was adapted from a merchant ensign rather than designed from scratch. The truth may never be known.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Grand Union Flag reminds us that identity is not always clear-cut, especially during transitions. The colonists were not yet Americans, but they were no longer simply British. They flew a flag that reflected their uncertainty — and then they grew past it. If you find yourself in a period of transition, unsure of who you are becoming, that is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign that you are in the middle of something important. Clarity comes with time and action, not all at once.
