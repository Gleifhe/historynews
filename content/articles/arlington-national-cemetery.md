## A General's Mansion on a Hill

Before it held the remains of more than 400,000 Americans, Arlington was a plantation.

The estate sat on a hilltop overlooking the Potomac River, directly across from Washington, D.C. It had been built by George Washington Parke Custis, the adopted grandson of George Washington, who completed the grand columned mansion — Arlington House — in 1818. Custis filled the house with Washington relics and used the surrounding 1,100 acres as a working plantation, operated by enslaved laborers.

When Custis died in 1857, the estate passed to his daughter, Mary Anna Custis Lee — the wife of Colonel Robert E. Lee, then a U.S. Army officer. Lee managed the estate and, under the terms of Custis's will, was required to free the enslaved people within five years. He did so in January 1863, the same month the Emancipation Proclamation took effect.

But by then, Lee had already made his fateful choice. In April 1861, when Virginia seceded from the Union, Lee resigned his commission in the U.S. Army and took command of Virginia's Confederate forces. The Lees abandoned Arlington, and Mary Lee never returned to live in the house.

## A Deliberate Act of Revenge

When Union forces occupied northern Virginia in May 1861, they immediately seized Arlington. The hilltop was too strategically valuable — it commanded the heights above Washington — to leave in Confederate hands. Troops camped on the grounds, and the mansion became a military headquarters.

The transformation from estate to cemetery was not accidental. It was personal.

In 1862, Congress passed a law allowing the federal government to collect taxes on property in "insurrectionary districts." When Mary Lee sent a cousin to pay the $92.07 tax bill on Arlington, the tax commissioners refused to accept payment from anyone other than the owner in person. Since Mary Lee could not safely travel to Union-held territory, the estate was seized for unpaid taxes and purchased by the federal government for $26,800.

Quartermaster General Montgomery C. Meigs — a former Army engineer who had once worked alongside Lee and now deeply despised him for his treason — saw an opportunity. The Union Army needed burial space. Meigs proposed turning Lee's estate into a military cemetery, ensuring that Lee could never return home.

On May 13, 1864, Private William Henry Christman of the 67th Pennsylvania Infantry became the first soldier buried at Arlington. He was 21 years old and had died of disease. Meigs personally ordered that graves be placed as close to the mansion as possible — including in Mary Lee's rose garden — to make the property permanently unsuitable as a private residence.

## The Cemetery Takes Shape

By the end of the Civil War, more than 16,000 soldiers were buried at Arlington. The graves included both Union and Confederate dead, though Confederates were initially buried in a separate section.

Meigs's intentions went beyond military necessity. He wanted Arlington to serve as a permanent monument to the cost of rebellion. In 1866, he ordered the construction of a large vault near the mansion and filled it with the remains of approximately 2,111 unidentified soldiers recovered from the battlefields of Bull Run and the route to the Rappahannock. The vault's inscription reads: "Beneath this stone repose the bones of two thousand one hundred and eleven unknown soldiers gathered after the war from the fields of Bull Run."

The connection between Arlington and the broader story of the Civil War runs deep. The men buried here fought in battles from [Fort Sumter to Appomattox](/articles/fort-sumter-to-appomattox/), and the cemetery exists because of a war that began when Lee chose the Confederacy over the Union he had sworn to defend.

## The Lee Family Fights Back

After the war, Robert E. Lee never attempted to reclaim Arlington. He died in 1870. But his eldest son, George Washington Custis Lee, sued the federal government, arguing that the tax sale had been illegal because the commissioners had refused his mother's proxy payment.

In 1882, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in *United States v. Lee* that Custis Lee was the rightful owner of the property. The decision put the government in an impossible position: the estate was now covered with thousands of graves.

Congress quickly negotiated a deal. Custis Lee sold Arlington back to the federal government for $150,000 — about $4.8 million in 2026 dollars. The transaction made the cemetery's status permanent and legal.

## Growth Through America's Wars

Arlington has grown with every conflict in American history. After the Civil War came the Spanish-American War, World War I, World War II, Korea, Vietnam, the Gulf War, and the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Each conflict added thousands of graves.

Today, Arlington covers 639 acres and contains more than 400,000 graves. Approximately 30 funerals are conducted each day, and the cemetery is expected to reach capacity by the middle of this century. An expansion project begun in 2020 added approximately 80,000 new burial spaces in the southern portion of the cemetery.

The cemetery's most famous graves include President John F. Kennedy, whose eternal flame has burned since his burial in 1963; his brother Senator Robert F. Kennedy; Supreme Court Justice Thurgood Marshall; boxer Joe Louis; astronauts from the Space Shuttle Challenger and Columbia disasters; and thousands of Medal of Honor recipients.

Arlington is also linked to other sacred sites of American military memory. The [USS Arizona Memorial](/articles/uss-arizona-memorial/) at Pearl Harbor serves a similar purpose in the Pacific — a place where the ship itself became the tomb, entombing 1,177 sailors and Marines who died on December 7, 1941.

## The Rituals of Arlington

Arlington is defined by its rituals. Every headstone is placed with precisely 12 inches between the bottom of one stone and the top of the next. Each stone is aligned to be visible in perfect rows from every angle. Groundskeepers place more than 250,000 flags — one at each headstone — before every Memorial Day, an operation called "Flags In" that requires roughly 1,000 soldiers working for several hours.

The cemetery's most famous ritual is the changing of the guard at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, which has been conducted 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, since 1937. The sentinels walk 21 steps, pause for 21 seconds, and turn — a number chosen to match the highest military honor, the 21-gun salute.

The funerals at Arlington follow precise military protocol. A horse-drawn caisson carries the casket. A riderless horse — boots reversed in the stirrups — follows behind, symbolizing a fallen warrior who will ride no more. A bugler plays "Taps." A folded flag is presented to the next of kin.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Arlington's history carries an uncomfortable truth: sacred ground can be created through acts of spite. Montgomery Meigs didn't establish the cemetery to honor the dead — he did it to punish Robert E. Lee. But over time, the cemetery transcended its origins. What began as revenge became something greater than any one person's grievance.

That's worth remembering when you think about your own life. Sometimes good things come from bad motivations. A project you started to prove someone wrong can become genuinely meaningful. A decision made in anger can lead somewhere important. What matters is not always why something started, but what it becomes.

Arlington also teaches the power of consistency. The guards at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier have maintained their vigil through hurricanes, blizzards, and every national crisis for nearly ninety years. They don't do it for applause — most of their shifts happen when no one is watching. Showing up consistently, especially when it's hard and no one notices, is one of the most respected things a person can do.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Arlington faces its most pressing challenge: space. The cemetery is projected to run out of burial plots within the coming decades, forcing difficult decisions about eligibility criteria. Currently, any honorably discharged veteran can be buried at Arlington, but future restrictions may limit burial to those killed in action, Medal of Honor recipients, and certain other categories.

This creates a profound question: How does a nation honor its dead when the ground runs out? Some advocates push for expansion. Others argue that the solution lies in columbarium walls for cremated remains, which take far less space than traditional burials.

Arlington also remains a flashpoint in debates about which stories get told and which get silenced. The Confederate section of the cemetery, including a large Confederate memorial erected in 1914, has generated controversy in recent years. In 2023, the Naming Commission recommended removing the Confederate memorial, arguing that it contained imagery glorifying the Lost Cause. The memorial was removed in December 2023.

From Lee's plantation to America's most hallowed ground, Arlington's story is the story of the nation itself — complicated, contested, and still unfolding.
