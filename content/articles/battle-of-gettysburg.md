## Two Armies Collide in a Small Town

In the summer of 1863, the Civil War had been raging for over two years. The Confederacy, led by General Robert E. Lee, had been winning battles in Virginia and felt unstoppable. Lee decided it was time to take the war north — into Union territory. He believed a major victory on Northern soil would break the Union's will to fight and convince European nations to support the Confederacy.

Lee's Army of Northern Virginia — about 75,000 men — marched through Maryland and into Pennsylvania. The Union's Army of the Potomac — roughly 93,000 soldiers — moved to intercept them. Neither army planned to fight at Gettysburg. The battle started almost by accident when Confederate soldiers heading into the small town ran into Union cavalry scouts on the morning of July 1, 1863.

What began as a small skirmish quickly exploded into the largest battle ever fought in North America.

## Day One: The Confederates Push Forward

On the first day, Confederate forces pushed Union troops back through the town of Gettysburg. The Union soldiers retreated to high ground south of town — Cemetery Hill and Cemetery Ridge. This position would prove critical.

Union General George Meade, who had taken command of the army just three days earlier, rushed reinforcements to the ridge. By nightfall, the Union held a strong defensive position shaped like a fishhook, stretching from Culp's Hill to a rocky formation called Little Round Top.

Lee saw an opportunity to crush the Union army before it could fully organize. His generals argued about the best approach. General James Longstreet urged Lee to go around the Union position and find better ground. Lee refused. He wanted to attack directly.

## Day Two: Fighting for Every Rock

On July 2, Lee ordered attacks on both ends of the Union line. The fighting was some of the most brutal of the entire war. At Little Round Top, Union Colonel Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain and the 20th Maine Regiment held the extreme left end of the line. When they ran out of ammunition, Chamberlain ordered a bayonet charge downhill into the attacking Confederates. It worked. The line held.

Elsewhere, soldiers fought desperately in a peach orchard, a wheat field, and a maze of boulders called Devil's Den. Thousands fell on both sides. By the end of the second day, the Union position was battered but unbroken.

## Day Three: Pickett's Charge

On July 3, Lee made the decision that would define the battle. He ordered roughly 12,500 Confederate soldiers — led by General George Pickett — to march across nearly a mile of open farmland directly at the center of the Union line on Cemetery Ridge.

The soldiers stepped out into the open under a blazing July sun. Union artillery tore into their ranks. As they got closer, Union infantry rose behind stone walls and poured rifle fire into the advancing lines. The Confederates were cut down in waves. A few hundred reached the Union position — a moment called the "High Water Mark of the Confederacy" — but they were quickly overwhelmed.

Pickett's Charge was a disaster. About half the men who made the attack were killed, wounded, or captured. When Pickett was later asked to rally his division, he reportedly said, "General Lee, I have no division now."

## The Aftermath

The three days of fighting left roughly 51,000 soldiers killed, wounded, captured, or missing — about 23,000 Union and 28,000 Confederate. Lee's army retreated south on July 4, never to invade the North again.

Four months later, President Abraham Lincoln came to Gettysburg to dedicate a cemetery for the fallen soldiers. His speech — the Gettysburg Address — lasted only two minutes but became one of the most important speeches in American history. Lincoln declared that the war was a test of whether a nation "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could survive. Those words connected the battle directly to the ideals of the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/) and the fight to end slavery through the [Emancipation Proclamation](/articles/emancipation-proclamation/).

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Gettysburg teaches us about the cost of stubbornness. Robert E. Lee was a brilliant general, but at Gettysburg he refused to listen to his subordinates and insisted on a frontal assault that his own officers knew would fail. Sometimes the bravest thing you can do is change your mind when the evidence tells you to.

The battle also shows what ordinary people can accomplish under pressure. Joshua Chamberlain was a college professor, not a career soldier. When his men ran out of bullets, he didn't freeze — he improvised. Life will throw situations at you where the playbook runs out. What matters is how you respond in that moment.

Finally, Lincoln's Gettysburg Address reminds us to find meaning in sacrifice. Lincoln didn't just mourn the dead — he gave their sacrifice a purpose. When you go through hard times, it helps to connect your struggles to something larger than yourself.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Gettysburg battlefield remains one of the most visited historical sites in America. But the questions the battle raised are still alive. What are we willing to fight for? How do we honor sacrifice? And how do we hold onto the idea that "all men are created equal" when inequality is still a daily reality?

The story of Gettysburg also connects to modern debates about leadership and accountability. Lee sent thousands of men to their deaths in a charge he was warned against. In 2026, whether in politics, business, or military decision-making, the Gettysburg lesson is clear: leaders who ignore good advice and let ego drive strategy put everyone at risk.

The battlefield itself is also at the center of preservation debates. Development pressures and climate change threaten historical sites across America. Deciding what to protect — and what to let go — is a question communities face every day in 2026.
