## The First Shower

Ask a veteran about coming home from deployment and you'll hear about the flight, the family waiting at the gate, the tears. But ask what surprised them most, and you'll hear something different.

"The shower," said Army Sergeant First Class Maria Torres, who deployed twice to Afghanistan. "I stood in a hot shower for forty-five minutes and cried. Not because I was sad. Because I had forgotten what hot water felt like when you don't have to share it with forty people and there's no time limit."

This is the paradox of homecoming: the extraordinary moment isn't the reunion or the ceremony. It's the ordinary moment — the running water, the cold glass of milk, the silence of a bedroom at night, the weight of a pillow that isn't stuffed into a rucksack. Deployment doesn't just take you away from comfort. It recalibrates your ability to notice it.

Veterans describe this experience so consistently that it has become one of the most reliable findings in transition research. The people who have had the least come home with the most gratitude. And that gratitude, according to decades of research by UC Davis psychologist Robert Emmons, is not just a pleasant feeling — it is a measurable predictor of mental health, physical health, and successful transition.

## Historical Context

The connection between deprivation and gratitude is older than psychology. Stoic philosophers, including Marcus Aurelius and Seneca, practiced deliberate deprivation — sleeping on hard floors, fasting, wearing rough clothing — specifically to cultivate appreciation for what they had. Seneca wrote in his *Letters to Lucilius*: "Set aside a certain number of days during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while: Is this the condition that I feared?"

American military history is filled with accounts of this same transformation. During the [Operation Homecoming](/articles/operation-homecoming-1973/) of 1973, when 591 American prisoners of war returned from North Vietnam after years of captivity — some held for over seven years in conditions of deliberate cruelty — the most common reaction was not anger or despair. It was gratitude.

Captain John McCain, who spent five and a half years as a POW in Hanoi, later described the experience as one that permanently changed his relationship with ordinary life. "I learned in prison that everything I had taken for granted was a gift," he said. Vice Admiral James Stockdale, the senior POW in North Vietnam, developed an explicit philosophy of gratitude rooted in the Stoic tradition, teaching fellow prisoners to reframe deprivation as a teacher rather than a punishment.

The pattern repeated across generations. World War II veterans who survived the Bataan Death March, the winter of the Bulge, or the island-hopping campaign in the Pacific came home with a visceral appreciation for peacetime comforts that shaped the culture of an entire generation. Korean War veterans who endured the subzero conditions at Chosin returned with a reverence for warmth that their families found both touching and bewildering.

## The Core Lesson: Gratitude Is Not Just a Feeling — It's a Practice

Robert Emmons, the world's leading scientific expert on gratitude, has spent over two decades studying its effects. His research, conducted at UC Davis and published in dozens of peer-reviewed journals, demonstrates that gratitude is not merely a nice emotion. It is a cognitive practice with measurable, replicable effects on mental and physical health.

In his landmark 2003 study, Emmons and his colleague Michael McCullough divided participants into three groups. One group wrote weekly about things they were grateful for. Another wrote about things that irritated them. A third wrote about neutral events. After ten weeks, the gratitude group reported 25% higher levels of well-being, exercised an average of 1.5 hours more per week, and reported fewer physical symptoms than the other groups.

Subsequent studies expanded these findings. Grateful people sleep better — an average of thirty minutes more per night. They have lower levels of inflammatory biomarkers. They report stronger social relationships. And critically for veterans, gratitude practice has been shown to reduce symptoms of PTSD and depression.

A 2015 study published in the *Journal of Positive Psychology* examined gratitude specifically in military populations. Researchers found that service members who practiced regular gratitude exercises reported significantly lower rates of depression and PTSD symptoms, and significantly higher rates of [post-traumatic growth](/articles/post-traumatic-growth/) — the phenomenon in which people who have experienced trauma report positive psychological changes as a result.

The mechanism is not magical. Gratitude works because it redirects attention. The human brain has a negativity bias — it naturally pays more attention to threats, losses, and problems than to blessings, gains, and comforts. Gratitude practice deliberately counteracts this bias by training the brain to notice what is good. Over time, this retraining becomes habitual, literally reshaping neural pathways.

## Real-World Examples

**The Cold Water Test.** Army Ranger School is one of the most physically demanding training programs in the U.S. military. Students endure sleep deprivation, caloric restriction, and extreme physical exertion for 62 days. Rangers who graduate consistently report that the experience permanently changed their appreciation for basic comforts. A hot meal. A dry pair of socks. Eight hours of sleep. "After Ranger School, I was grateful for a floor to sleep on," one graduate told researchers at the Army War College. "Before Ranger School, I complained about my mattress."

**Vietnam Veterans' Reunions.** Dr. Jonathan Shay, a psychiatrist who spent decades working with Vietnam veterans at the Boston VA, documented a recurring phenomenon he called "the gratitude of survivors." Veterans who had processed their trauma and maintained social connections did not merely return to baseline functioning — they developed a heightened capacity for appreciating everyday life. "These men notice sunsets," Shay wrote in *Odysseus in America*. "They notice their grandchildren. They notice the taste of coffee. They live with an awareness that most people save for near-death experiences — because they've had near-death experiences."

**Deployment Letters.** Military families often observe the gratitude shift in real time through their service member's letters and calls. Early deployment communications tend to focus on the mission, the conditions, and complaints. As deployment progresses, the communications shift toward appreciation — for the family at home, for the life they left behind, for the things they previously considered unremarkable. This shift is so consistent that military family support organizations use it as a teaching tool, preparing families for the emotional changes deployment produces.

**The Marine Corps' "Count Your Blessings" Tradition.** Several units have incorporated informal gratitude practices into their culture. After particularly grueling training exercises, squad leaders will ask their Marines to name one thing they're grateful for. It's not therapy. It's not soft. It's a hard-nosed recognition that the ability to find something good in a bad situation is a combat-relevant skill.

## Personal Growth: Cultivating Gratitude Without Deployment

The veteran experience demonstrates that deprivation reliably produces gratitude. But the research shows you don't need deprivation — you need practice.

Emmons recommends three evidence-based strategies that anyone can implement.

**The gratitude journal.** Write down three to five things you're grateful for each day. Be specific — "I'm grateful for the way the light came through the kitchen window this morning" is more effective than "I'm grateful for my house." Specificity forces attention. Do this for three weeks and the research predicts measurable improvements in well-being and sleep quality.

**The gratitude letter.** Write a letter to someone who has made a positive difference in your life — a teacher, a friend, a parent, a mentor — and, if possible, read it to them in person. Emmons' research shows that this single exercise produces the largest and most sustained boost in happiness of any positive psychology intervention tested.

**The deprivation exercise.** Choose one comfort — your phone, your morning coffee, your car — and go without it for a day or a week. Not as punishment. As practice. Seneca did this two thousand years ago. Rangers do it for 62 days. You can do it for 24 hours. The purpose is to notice what you have by temporarily not having it.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Americans live in the most materially comfortable society in human history and report among the highest rates of anxiety and dissatisfaction ever measured. The hedonic treadmill — the psychological phenomenon in which people rapidly adapt to improvements in their circumstances and return to baseline happiness — is running faster than ever. The new phone is exciting for a week. The new house is exciting for a month. Then it becomes the background, invisible and unappreciated.

Veterans who return from deployment have, involuntarily, stepped off the treadmill. They have experienced what it means to have nothing — and discovered that nothing teaches you the value of something faster than its absence.

The lesson for 2026 is not that everyone should deploy to a combat zone. It is that gratitude is a skill, not a circumstance. It can be practiced, developed, and strengthened by anyone willing to pay attention. Emmons' research proves it. The veteran experience confirms it. And in an age of unprecedented comfort and unprecedented dissatisfaction, it may be the most important skill any of us can learn.

Start with the shower. Tomorrow morning, stand under the hot water and notice it. Notice the temperature, the pressure, the fact that it exists at all. Somewhere, a veteran is doing the same thing — and remembering a time when it didn't.
