On the night of September 13, 1814, Francis Scott Key stood on the deck of a British truce ship anchored eight miles from Baltimore's Fort McHenry. He was not a soldier. He was a 35-year-old lawyer from Georgetown who had sailed out to negotiate the release of a prisoner. Now he was trapped — forced to watch as the most powerful navy in the world attempted to blast an American fort into submission.

## The War Nobody Remembers

The War of 1812 is often called America's forgotten war, squeezed between the Revolution and the Civil War in the national memory. But in 1814, the conflict with Britain was terrifyingly real. Just three weeks before the attack on Fort McHenry, British troops had marched into Washington, D.C., and burned the White House, the Capitol, and most other government buildings. President James Madison and his wife Dolley had fled the city. The nation was humiliated.

Baltimore was next. The city was a major port and a base for American privateers who had been raiding British merchant ships. The British were determined to take it.

## The Bombardment

The British plan was straightforward: the Royal Navy would bombard Fort McHenry into rubble while ground troops attacked the city from the east. The naval force was formidable — nineteen ships, including bomb vessels armed with heavy mortars that could lob 200-pound explosive shells in high arcs over the fort's walls.

The bombardment began at 6:30 a.m. on September 13 and continued without pause for 25 hours. The British fired between 1,500 and 1,800 shells at the fort. The explosions lit the sky. The ground shook. Rain poured down. Key, watching from miles away, could track the battle only by the flashes of the rockets and the bursts of the shells.

Major George Armistead, the fort's commander, had prepared for this. He had commissioned an enormous flag — 30 feet by 42 feet — from Baltimore flagmaker Mary Pickersgill specifically so that the British would have no trouble seeing it. The flag was so large that Pickersgill and her team had to assemble it on the floor of a local brewery because no room in her house was big enough.

## The Dawn

Through the long night, Key strained to see the flag. During the bombardment, he could catch glimpses of it in the light of explosions. But around 1:00 a.m., the British shifted tactics, attempting a flanking assault by boat. The firing paused. Key could see nothing. He did not know if the fort had fallen.

When dawn finally broke on September 14, Key looked toward the fort and saw Armistead's enormous flag — battered and rain-soaked but still flying. The British attack had failed. Fort McHenry had held. Baltimore was saved.

Overwhelmed, Key began writing on the back of a letter he had in his pocket. "O say can you see, by the dawn's early light, / What so proudly we hail'd at the twilight's last gleaming?" The poem, originally titled "Defence of Fort M'Henry," was published in newspapers and set to the tune of a popular British song. It spread across the country almost immediately.

## From Poem to Anthem

Key's poem became wildly popular, but it did not become the official national anthem until 1931, when President Herbert Hoover signed it into law. For over a century, the United States had no official anthem, though "The Star-Spangled Banner" was widely used at military ceremonies and public events.

The original flag — Mary Pickersgill's enormous banner — survived. It passed through the Armistead family, who occasionally cut pieces from it as souvenirs. Today, the flag measures 30 by 34 feet (reduced from its original 30 by 42) and is preserved at the Smithsonian National Museum of American History in Washington, D.C.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Key wrote his most enduring work during the worst night of his life — terrified, powerless, and unable to see the outcome. He could not control the battle. He could only watch. But when the dawn came and he saw the result, he was ready to respond. Sometimes the most important thing you can do in a crisis is simply endure the darkness and be prepared to act when the light returns.
