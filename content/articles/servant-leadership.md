## The General Who Gave It Back

On December 23, 1783, George Washington did something that no victorious military commander in modern history had ever done. He walked into the Maryland State House in Annapolis, where the Continental Congress was assembled, and voluntarily surrendered his military commission. He gave power back.

Washington could have been king. His army adored him. The nation owed him everything. Several of his officers had openly urged him to seize permanent authority — the new country was unstable, the government was weak, and Washington was the only figure with universal trust. In almost every other revolution in history, the victorious general kept the power he had won.

Washington said no. He bowed to Congress, returned to his farm at Mount Vernon, and went back to being a private citizen. When King George III of England heard that Washington planned to resign, he reportedly said: "If he does that, he will be the greatest man in the world."

Washington understood something that would take nearly two centuries to get a formal name: the most powerful form of leadership is service. The leader exists for the people, not the other way around.

## Historical Context

Robert K. Greenleaf, a retired AT&T executive, published his essay "The Servant as Leader" in 1970. Greenleaf argued that the great leader is first experienced as a servant — someone whose primary motivation is to serve others, and who leads as a consequence of that service rather than as a goal in itself.

Greenleaf's insight was not new. It drew on religious traditions stretching back millennia — Jesus washing his disciples' feet, the Confucian concept of the virtuous ruler, the Buddhist ideal of the bodhisattva who delays personal enlightenment to serve others. But Greenleaf gave the concept a secular, institutional framework that organizations could adopt and teach.

The military, characteristically, had been practicing servant leadership long before Greenleaf named it. The tradition runs deep. In the U.S. Army, officers eat last. This isn't a metaphor — it's a literal practice in most units. The lowest-ranking soldiers go through the chow line first. The commanding officer eats last. The symbolism is deliberate: the leader's needs come after the needs of those they lead.

The Marquis de Lafayette embodied this principle during the American Revolution. As recounted in [his remarkable story](/articles/marquis-de-lafayette/), Lafayette was a French aristocrat who crossed the Atlantic at age 19 to fight for American independence — at his own expense. He served without pay, was wounded at the Battle of Brandywine, and spent the brutal winter at Valley Forge alongside the common soldiers. Lafayette didn't lead from privilege. He led from sacrifice. Washington, recognizing a kindred spirit, treated Lafayette like a son.

West Point, the U.S. Military Academy, has built its entire leadership curriculum around servant leadership principles. Cadets are taught that leadership is not about authority — it's about responsibility. The academy's motto, "Duty, Honor, Country," places duty first for a reason. Your obligation to serve comes before personal glory or ambition. The Simon Center for the Professional Military Ethic at West Point teaches cadets to ask not "What can my people do for me?" but "What can I do for my people?"

## The Core Lesson: Mission First, People Always

The military distills servant leadership into a phrase that captures its dual obligation: [mission first, people always](/articles/mission-first-people-always/). The mission matters — lives depend on it. But the people who execute the mission matter equally, because without their trust, commitment, and well-being, the mission fails.

This creates a productive tension. A purely mission-focused leader burns people out. A purely people-focused leader avoids hard decisions and lets the team down in a different way. The servant leader holds both priorities simultaneously: accomplish the mission *and* take care of your people. When the two conflict — and they will — the servant leader finds a way to honor both rather than sacrificing one for the other.

Greenleaf proposed a simple test for servant leadership: "Do those served grow as persons? Do they, while being served, become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous, more likely themselves to become servants?" This test shifts the evaluation of leadership from outcomes to impact on people. A leader who achieves great results but leaves broken people in their wake is not a servant leader. A leader who develops people who go on to lead and serve others — that's the standard.

The military applies this test constantly. The measure of a good platoon sergeant is not just whether the platoon accomplishes its missions. It's whether the soldiers in that platoon develop into leaders themselves. The best military leaders are known not for their own accomplishments but for the accomplishments of the people they developed. General George C. Marshall, Army Chief of Staff during World War II, selected and mentored the commanders who won the war — Eisenhower, Patton, Bradley, and dozens of others. Marshall's greatest achievement wasn't a battle. It was building a generation of leaders.

## Real-World Examples

Servant leadership has spread far beyond the military and the pages of Greenleaf's essay. Companies like Southwest Airlines, Starbucks, and The Container Store have adopted servant leadership as explicit corporate philosophy. Southwest's co-founder Herb Kelleher was famous for putting employees first — arguing that happy employees create happy customers, which creates happy shareholders. The sequence matters: serve your people, and the results follow.

But servant leadership is most visible in the small, unglamorous acts that never make headlines. The veteran who coaches Little League not because they love baseball but because they see kids who need structure and mentorship. The retired NCO who volunteers at the VFW, organizing meals and transportation for older veterans. The former officer who mentors junior employees at work, spending hours helping them prepare for promotions that won't benefit the mentor at all.

These acts of service are the living legacy of Washington's decision to surrender his commission. Every time a veteran puts someone else's development ahead of their own convenience, they're practicing the same philosophy that Washington demonstrated in Annapolis — the radical, counterintuitive idea that true power is measured by what you give away, not what you accumulate.

## Personal Growth: Leading by Serving in 2026

In 2026, the servant leadership model is more relevant than ever. The traditional command-and-control leadership style is failing in organizations that depend on knowledge workers, creative collaboration, and distributed teams. People who feel micromanaged disengage. People who feel served — who believe their leader genuinely cares about their growth and well-being — perform at their highest level.

You don't need a title to practice servant leadership. You need a mindset shift. Instead of asking "How do I get ahead?" ask "How do I help the people around me succeed?" Instead of measuring your day by your own accomplishments, measure it by how many people you helped accomplish theirs.

Start with listening. Greenleaf identified listening as the first and most important skill of the servant leader. Most people listen to respond. Servant leaders listen to understand. They ask questions. They pay attention to what's not being said. They create space for others to speak first.

Then act on what you've heard. Help without being asked. Share credit generously. Accept blame quickly. Celebrate others' wins publicly and process your own setbacks privately. These habits feel unnatural in a culture that rewards self-promotion, but they build the kind of trust that no amount of authority can manufacture.

Washington's choice in 1783 wasn't a single dramatic act — it was the culmination of a lifetime of putting service above self. He made that choice because it was who he was, not because he was performing for an audience. Servant leadership works the same way. It's not a technique. It's a character trait, built one act of service at a time.
