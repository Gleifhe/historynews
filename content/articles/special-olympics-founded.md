In the early 1960s, Eunice Kennedy Shriver looked at the world and saw something deeply wrong. People with intellectual disabilities were hidden away. Many were locked in institutions, forgotten by society. They were told they could not learn, could not play, and could not compete. Eunice refused to accept that.

She had a personal reason to care. Her older sister, Rosemary Kennedy, had an intellectual disability. Rosemary had been sent to an institution after a failed surgery, and the family rarely spoke about her publicly. But Eunice never forgot her sister. She believed that people like Rosemary deserved the same chances as everyone else — including the chance to play sports.

## A Camp in the Backyard

In 1962, Eunice started a summer day camp at her home in Maryland called Camp Shriver. She invited children and adults with intellectual disabilities to swim, run, and play sports. At a time when most people believed these individuals could not handle physical activity, Eunice proved them wrong. The campers thrived. They laughed, competed, and showed abilities that no one had expected.

The camp was a success, but Eunice wanted more. She dreamed of a national — even international — sports competition for people with intellectual disabilities. She spent years planning, raising money, and convincing skeptics. Many people told her it could not be done. She did it anyway.

## The First Games

On July 20, 1968, about 1,000 athletes from 26 U.S. states, Canada, and France gathered at Soldier Field in Chicago for the first International Special Olympics Summer Games. They competed in track and field, floor hockey, and aquatics. The Chicago Park District donated the venue. Volunteers helped organize everything.

The event was modest compared to the traditional Olympics, but it was revolutionary. For the first time, athletes with intellectual disabilities stood on a world stage. They ran races, won medals, and heard crowds cheer their names. The oath spoken at those first games captured the spirit of the movement: "Let me win. But if I cannot win, let me be brave in the attempt."

Eunice was there, cheering every athlete. She understood that this was about more than sports. It was about dignity. It was about showing the world that every person has value.

## Growth Beyond Imagination

The Special Olympics grew quickly. By 1975, every U.S. state had a chapter. International chapters followed. Countries across Europe, Asia, Africa, and South America joined the movement. The games expanded to include dozens of sports: basketball, soccer, gymnastics, skiing, figure skating, and many more.

Today, the Special Olympics includes more than 5.7 million athletes in over 190 countries. More than one million coaches and volunteers support them. The organization runs year-round training and competitions, not just a single event. It also promotes health screenings, education programs, and leadership development for athletes.

The impact goes far beyond the playing field. Research has shown that communities involved with the Special Olympics become more inclusive. People who volunteer or watch the games change their attitudes about disability. Athletes gain confidence, friendships, and skills that carry over into school, work, and daily life.

## Eunice's Legacy

Eunice Kennedy Shriver passed away in 2009, but her legacy continues to grow. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1984 for her work. In 2025, she was honored on a U.S. quarter as part of the American Women Quarters Program.

More importantly, she changed how the world thinks about ability and disability. Before the Special Olympics, most people saw intellectual disability as a limitation. After Eunice, millions of people saw it as one part of a whole human being — a person who could dream, compete, and succeed.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Eunice Kennedy Shriver's story teaches us that one person with a clear vision can change the world. She did not wait for governments or big organizations to act. She started with a camp in her own backyard. She used what she had and built from there.

Her story also reminds us to look for the people who are being left out. In every school, workplace, and community, there are people who are overlooked or underestimated. The Special Olympics exists because one woman decided that leaving people out was not acceptable.

For anyone who has ever been told they cannot do something, the Special Olympics athletes offer powerful proof: courage matters more than ability. Showing up and trying is already a victory. That lesson applies to all of us, whether we are learning a new skill, starting a new job, or facing a personal challenge.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the push for inclusion continues to grow in schools, workplaces, and communities worldwide. The Special Olympics model — using sports to break down barriers — has inspired similar programs for other marginalized groups. The values Eunice championed connect directly to the broader civil rights movements, including the [March on Washington in 1963](/articles/civil-rights-march-on-washington/), where leaders demanded equality for all people.

The [United Nations](/articles/founding-of-united-nations/) has recognized the Special Olympics as a key partner in promoting the rights of people with disabilities. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, signed by most countries, echoes the same principles Eunice fought for: dignity, inclusion, and equal opportunity.

As the world continues to wrestle with questions about who belongs and who deserves a chance, the Special Olympics stands as living proof that inclusion makes everyone stronger. Eunice Kennedy Shriver showed us that the biggest barriers are not in the body — they are in the mind. And those barriers can be broken.
