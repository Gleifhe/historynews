## The Kid From Patchogue

Michael Patrick Murphy was born on May 7, 1976, in Smithtown, New York, and grew up in the Long Island community of Patchogue. He was, by every account, the kind of person who made others feel noticed. Friends and teachers described him as someone who stood up for kids who were being bullied — not with his fists, but by simply walking over and standing next to them. His nickname, even as a child, was "The Protector."

Murphy was an honors student at Patchogue-Medford High School. He was a lifeguard. He played hockey. He was accepted into several law schools after graduating from Penn State University in 1998. By any measure, he had an easy path ahead of him — a comfortable career, a life far from the mountains of Afghanistan.

Then came September 11, 2001. Murphy watched the Twin Towers fall from Long Island, roughly 60 miles away. Within months, he had abandoned his plans for law school and begun pursuing a commission in the United States Navy with the specific goal of becoming a SEAL.

He graduated from Basic Underwater Demolition/SEAL training (BUD/S) — one of the most demanding military selection processes in the world, with a dropout rate that typically exceeds 75 percent — in 2002. He was assigned to SEAL Delivery Vehicle Team One in Hawaii and later to SEAL Team Ten based in Virginia Beach.

## Historical Context: The War in the Mountains

By 2005, the war in Afghanistan was in its fourth year, and the fighting had shifted from the initial campaign against the Taliban to a grinding counterinsurgency in the country's rugged eastern provinces. The border region between Afghanistan and Pakistan — a landscape of mountains, valleys, and villages accessible only by foot — had become a stronghold for Taliban and al-Qaeda fighters who crossed back and forth across the border with near impunity.

Kunar Province, in Afghanistan's northeast, was among the most dangerous regions. Dense forests, steep ravines, and elevations above 10,000 feet made conventional military operations nearly impossible. Small teams of special operations forces — Army Green Berets, Navy SEALs, and Marine special operators — were tasked with conducting reconnaissance and disrupting enemy networks in terrain that gave every advantage to the defenders.

It was into this terrain that Lieutenant Murphy and his team were inserted on the night of June 27, 2005.

## The Event: Operation Red Wings

Operation Red Wings was a reconnaissance mission targeting a Taliban leader named Ahmad Shah, who was believed to be operating with a force of fighters in the Sawtalo Sar mountain area of Kunar Province. Murphy led a four-man SEAL reconnaissance team tasked with locating Shah's forces and calling in a larger assault force.

The team consisted of Murphy, Petty Officer Second Class Danny Dietz, Petty Officer Second Class Matthew Axelson, and Hospital Corpsman Second Class Marcus Luttrell. They were inserted by helicopter under cover of darkness and moved to an observation position overlooking the village where Shah was believed to be hiding.

On the morning of June 28, the team was discovered by local goat herders. Faced with a decision that would be debated for years — whether to detain or release the herders — the team chose to release them. Within roughly an hour, the SEALs came under attack from a force that Luttrell later estimated at 30 to 40 Taliban fighters occupying the high ground above them.

The team was outmanned and pinned down on a steep, rocky slope. The terrain that made the mountains ideal for reconnaissance also made them a death trap once the team's position was compromised. Taliban fighters fired from above while the SEALs fought their way downhill, tumbling over cliffs and through ravines as they tried to break contact.

Dietz was hit multiple times and killed. Axelson, severely wounded, continued fighting until he was killed. Murphy and Luttrell were both wounded repeatedly as they moved down the mountainside.

It was in this desperate situation that Murphy made the decision that would define his legacy. The team's satellite phone and radio could not get a signal in the deep ravine where they had taken cover. The surrounding mountains blocked the transmission. The only way to reach headquarters and request reinforcements was to move to open ground — directly into the enemy's line of fire.

Murphy knew what stepping into the open meant. He did it anyway.

He walked deliberately into a clearing, fully exposed to enemy fire from multiple positions, and calmly made the call to the SEAL base at Bagram Airfield. He provided his team's coordinates, reported the size of the enemy force, and requested immediate support. He was shot in the back during the call. According to Luttrell, the sole survivor, Murphy finished the call, said "Thank you," and continued fighting until he was killed.

## The Rescue and Its Cost

The call for help triggered a quick reaction force. A CH-47 Chinook helicopter carrying eight Navy SEALs and eight Army Night Stalkers from the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment launched for the rescue. As the helicopter approached the landing zone, it was struck by a rocket-propelled grenade fired by Taliban fighters. The Chinook crashed, killing all 16 men aboard.

In total, 19 Americans were killed on June 28, 2005 — the single deadliest day for Naval Special Warfare since World War II and the deadliest single incident of the entire Afghanistan War at that time.

Marcus Luttrell, severely wounded, evaded the Taliban for nearly a day before being taken in by Pashtun villagers in the village of Sabray, who protected him under the ancient code of Pashtunwali — the obligation to provide hospitality and protection to guests, even at personal risk. He was rescued by American forces on July 2.

## The Medal of Honor

On October 22, 2007, President George W. Bush presented the Medal of Honor to Murphy's parents, Daniel and Maureen Murphy, in a ceremony at the White House. Michael Murphy became the first service member to receive the Medal of Honor for actions in Afghanistan and the first Navy recipient since the Vietnam War.

His citation reads, in part: "By his undaunted courage, intrepid fighting spirit, and inspirational devotion to his men in the face of certain death, Lieutenant Murphy was able to relay the position of his team, and request reinforcements that ultimately led to the rescue of one team member."

The Navy honored Murphy by naming a guided-missile destroyer, the USS Michael Murphy (DDG 112), which was commissioned in 2012. A Veterans Affairs medical center in his hometown area was also renamed in his honor. Like [Pat Tillman](/articles/pat-tillman-chose-service/), Murphy had walked away from a comfortable life to serve after 9/11. And like [Audie Murphy](/articles/audie-murphy-most-decorated/), whose Medal of Honor came from standing alone against overwhelming odds, Michael Murphy's sacrifice reflected the defining quality of the award: placing others above self.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Michael Murphy's story is about a single decision — the decision to step into the open when staying hidden was safer. He weighed his own survival against his teammates' chance of rescue and chose his teammates. That is the essence of leadership: not authority, not rank, but the willingness to accept cost so that others do not have to.

Most people will never face a choice as stark as Murphy's. But the principle translates to every part of life. Leadership means speaking up when staying silent is easier. It means taking responsibility when blame could be deflected. It means making the phone call, having the hard conversation, or doing the uncomfortable thing — not because it is safe, but because someone has to.

Murphy's nickname was "The Protector" long before he became a SEAL. His character on the mountain was the same character he had on the playground. What you do in a crisis is who you have been all along.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Michael Murphy Memorial Park in Patchogue, New York, and the annual Murph CrossFit workout — a grueling routine Murphy created and performed regularly — keep his memory alive across the country. The "Murph" workout is performed each Memorial Day by hundreds of thousands of people worldwide, making it one of the most widely observed acts of veteran remembrance in modern American culture. Murphy's story continues to be taught at the Naval Academy and at SEAL training as a case study in leadership and sacrifice — a reminder that the Medal of Honor is not awarded for success, but for the willingness to give everything for the person beside you.
