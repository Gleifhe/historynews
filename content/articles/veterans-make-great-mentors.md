## The Backbone of the Army Has Always Been a Teacher

In every branch of the U.S. military, there is a rank structure that civilians rarely understand and that the military considers its most valuable asset: the Non-Commissioned Officer corps. NCOs — sergeants, petty officers, staff sergeants, master sergeants — are the bridge between officers who plan and privates who execute. They are, in the Army's own language, "the backbone."

But the NCO's most important job is not giving orders. It is teaching. Every NCO in the American military is, by design and by tradition, a mentor. They teach privates how to shoot, how to march, how to survive. They teach junior NCOs how to lead, how to make decisions under pressure, and how to take responsibility for other people's lives. This is not optional. It is the job.

The result is that the U.S. military produces, every year, hundreds of thousands of men and women who have spent years practicing a skill that civilian organizations desperately need and rarely develop: the ability to mentor someone effectively.

## Historical Context

The American NCO tradition dates to the founding of the Continental Army in 1775, when Baron Friedrich von Steuben — a Prussian officer recruited by Benjamin Franklin — arrived at Valley Forge and found an army that could barely march in a straight line. Von Steuben didn't lecture the officers and hope the training trickled down. He went directly to the sergeants. He trained them personally, then made them responsible for training the soldiers under their command.

It was the birth of the "each one teach one" philosophy that has defined the NCO corps for 250 years. Von Steuben understood what modern organizational research has confirmed: training is most effective when delivered by someone one step ahead of the learner, not ten steps. A sergeant who learned to field-strip a rifle last year is a better teacher of that skill than a colonel who learned it twenty years ago. Proximity creates credibility.

This principle scaled through every American conflict. In World War II, the Army's rapid expansion from 190,000 to over 8 million soldiers was possible only because the NCO corps could train new recruits at a speed that no officer-led system could match. A single experienced sergeant could transform a platoon of civilians into a functioning combat unit in weeks — not because he had a curriculum, but because he had the credibility of shared experience and the authority of demonstrated competence.

The [mission-first, people-always philosophy](/articles/mission-first-people-always/) that defines military leadership is, at the NCO level, primarily a mentorship philosophy. Taking care of your people means developing them. It means investing time in their growth, correcting their mistakes directly, and holding them to standards that stretch them without breaking them.

## The Core Lesson: Great Mentors Are Direct, Accountable, and Invested

What makes veteran mentors exceptional is not mystery. It is three qualities that the military trains relentlessly and that civilian culture often avoids.

**Directness.** Military mentors tell you the truth. Not the comfortable version. Not the version that protects your feelings. The version you need to hear to survive. A sergeant who sees a private making a mistake that could get someone killed does not schedule a feedback session for next Thursday. He corrects it immediately, clearly, and without ambiguity. This directness, which can feel jarring in civilian contexts, is precisely what makes veteran mentors valuable. Research published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* in 2019 found that mentors who delivered candid, specific feedback produced significantly better outcomes than mentors who prioritized relationship warmth over honesty.

**Accountability.** In the military, mentorship is a two-way accountability structure. The mentor is responsible for the mentee's development. If the mentee fails, the mentor has failed. This is not a metaphor — it is literally how the military evaluates NCO performance. A sergeant whose soldiers can't perform is a sergeant who isn't doing his job. This accountability creates an intensity of investment that casual mentorship lacks. The mentor is not offering advice as a favor. The mentor's own reputation depends on the mentee's growth.

**Investment in the person, not the task.** Civilian managers often train employees to complete specific tasks. Military mentors develop people. The goal is not to teach Private Jones how to do one job. The goal is to develop Private Jones into someone who can figure out any job — and eventually teach others. This distinction, between task training and human development, is the difference between a manager and a mentor.

## Real-World Examples

**American Corporate Partners (ACP).** Founded in 2008 by Sidney E. Goodfriend, ACP is a national nonprofit that pairs transitioning veterans and active-duty spouses with corporate professionals for yearlong mentorships. As of 2025, ACP has served over 25,000 protégés through partnerships with more than 100 corporations. The program's outcomes are remarkable: 87% of ACP protégés report that their mentorship was "valuable" or "extremely valuable" to their career transition, and ACP alumni consistently report higher employment rates and career satisfaction than veterans who transition without mentorship support.

What makes ACP significant for this story is the reverse flow. ACP initially designed the program to help veterans receive mentorship. What they discovered is that veterans frequently become the mentors — not just within ACP, but within the companies that hire them. Corporate partners report that veteran employees naturally gravitate toward mentoring junior colleagues, organizing informal training, and holding peers accountable. The military's "each one teach one" habit doesn't switch off at discharge.

**The Pat Tillman Foundation.** The Tillman Scholars program, which supports veterans pursuing education, has found that veteran scholars disproportionately take on mentoring roles at their universities — tutoring other students, leading study groups, and advising younger veterans navigating higher education. The foundation's own data shows that Tillman Scholars average over 100 hours of community service annually, much of it in mentoring roles.

**Team Rubicon.** The veteran-led disaster response organization has built its training pipeline on the NCO model. Experienced veteran volunteers mentor newer volunteers through progressively challenging deployments. Jake Wood, Team Rubicon's co-founder, has described the organization's growth model as fundamentally military: "We don't train people for one disaster. We develop leaders who can handle any disaster. That's the NCO way."

## Personal Growth: Finding and Being a Mentor

The [transition from platoon to purpose](/articles/platoon-to-purpose/) is one of the most difficult challenges veterans face. Mentorship — both receiving and providing it — is one of the most effective bridges.

If you are looking for a mentor, apply the lesson of the NCO corps: find someone one step ahead of you, not ten. The best mentor is not the CEO. The best mentor is the person who recently navigated the challenge you're currently facing. They remember what it felt like. They know the specific obstacles. And they have the credibility of proximity.

If you are a veteran looking to mentor others, recognize that you already have the skills. You have been trained to give direct feedback, to hold people accountable, and to invest in human development over task completion. Those skills are rare in civilian life and desperately needed. Organizations like ACP, SCORE (which pairs mentors with small business owners), and Big Brothers Big Sisters actively recruit veterans because they know what the military produces: natural mentors.

If you are a civilian, the NCO model offers a blueprint for better mentorship in any context. Be direct. Be accountable for your mentee's growth. Focus on developing the person, not just completing the task. And remember Von Steuben's lesson from Valley Forge: the best training comes from someone who has recently been where the learner is now.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the American workforce faces a mentorship deficit. A 2024 survey by the American Society for Training and Development found that 75% of executives credit mentorship as critical to their career development — but only 37% of professionals report having a mentor. The gap is particularly acute for young workers entering a job market shaped by remote work, artificial intelligence, and rapid industry disruption.

Veterans can help close that gap. More than 200,000 service members transition out of the military each year, each carrying years of practiced mentorship skills that most civilian professionals never develop. The question is not whether veterans can mentor. The question is whether civilian organizations are smart enough to let them.

The evidence says yes. Companies that participate in ACP report that veteran employees improve team cohesion, raise performance standards, and naturally develop junior talent. The NCO tradition — 250 years old and counting — remains one of America's most underutilized assets. Each one teach one. It worked at Valley Forge. It works today.
