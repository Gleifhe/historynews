For thousands of years, smallpox was one of the most terrifying diseases on Earth. It killed about 30 percent of the people who caught it. Those who survived were often left with deep scars covering their bodies. The disease didn't care if you were rich or poor, young or old. It struck kings, peasants, and entire civilizations without mercy.

Smallpox killed an estimated 300 million people in the 20th century alone. That's more than all the wars of that century combined. Ancient Egyptian mummies show signs of the disease, meaning it plagued humanity for at least 3,000 years. For most of that time, there was nothing anyone could do about it.

Then, in 1796, a country doctor in England named Edward Jenner made a discovery that would change the world. He noticed that milkmaids who caught cowpox — a mild disease from cows — seemed to never get smallpox. Jenner took fluid from a cowpox sore on a milkmaid's hand and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy named James Phipps. Weeks later, he exposed the boy to smallpox. The boy didn't get sick. Jenner had created the world's first vaccine. The word "vaccine" actually comes from "vacca," the Latin word for cow.

Jenner's discovery spread across the world, but smallpox didn't disappear overnight. For nearly 200 more years, the disease continued to kill millions. Many countries couldn't afford vaccines. Others couldn't keep them cold enough in hot climates. Wars and poverty made it hard to reach everyone.

In 1959, the World Health Organization decided it was time to end smallpox once and for all. They launched a global campaign, but progress was slow at first. Then, in 1967, the WHO intensified its efforts with a new strategy. Instead of trying to vaccinate every single person on Earth, health workers focused on finding outbreaks and vaccinating everyone around the sick person. This was called "ring vaccination," and it worked brilliantly.

Thousands of health workers traveled to the most remote villages on the planet. They crossed deserts, climbed mountains, and navigated dense jungles. In some places, they went door to door, showing pictures of smallpox sores and asking if anyone had seen a person with these marks. Each outbreak was tracked down and surrounded with vaccinations until the disease had nowhere left to go.

The last natural case of smallpox occurred in Somalia in 1977, when a hospital cook named Ali Maow Maalin caught the disease. He survived and later spent his life helping to vaccinate others against polio. On May 8, 1980, the World Health Assembly officially declared that smallpox had been eradicated from the Earth. It remains the only human disease ever completely eliminated.

The eradication of smallpox proved something powerful: when the entire world works together toward a common goal, extraordinary things are possible. Countries that were enemies during the Cold War cooperated on this effort. Rich nations funded the campaign while health workers from dozens of countries did the difficult work on the ground. It was one of humanity's greatest team efforts.

The success of smallpox eradication inspired other global health campaigns, including the ongoing fight against [polio](/articles/polio-vaccine-salk/) and the battle against diseases like malaria and measles. Scientists continue to build on the foundation that Edward Jenner laid more than two centuries ago, using the principles of vaccination to protect billions of people. Understanding how diseases evolve, as [Darwin's work](/articles/darwins-origin-of-species/) helped us appreciate, has been essential to developing better vaccines and treatment strategies.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of smallpox eradication teaches us that the biggest problems can be solved when we refuse to give up. Edward Jenner faced ridicule from other doctors who thought his vaccine idea was foolish. Many people were afraid of vaccination at first. But Jenner kept going because he believed in what the evidence showed him.

This same lesson applies to our own lives. When you face a challenge that seems impossible — a tough class, a difficult relationship, or a personal goal that feels out of reach — remember that the entire planet once faced a disease that had killed for thousands of years. People didn't give up. They found a solution and stuck with it.

The story also teaches the power of small actions. Every health worker who knocked on one more door or vaccinated one more child contributed to the final victory. You don't have to solve the whole problem yourself. You just have to do your part, consistently and with determination.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the world continues to face health challenges that echo the smallpox story. The COVID-19 pandemic showed both how far vaccine science has come and how much work remains in distributing vaccines fairly around the world. The mRNA technology used in COVID vaccines represents the next evolution of the science Jenner started.

Today, scientists are using lessons from the smallpox campaign to work toward eliminating other diseases. Polio is on the verge of eradication, with only a handful of cases reported each year. Efforts to develop vaccines for malaria and other tropical diseases are making real progress.

The smallpox story also reminds us of the importance of global cooperation. In a world where countries sometimes struggle to work together, the eradication of smallpox stands as proof that international teamwork can achieve miracles. As we face challenges like climate change and antibiotic resistance, that lesson has never been more important.
