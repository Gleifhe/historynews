## A Saturday Afternoon at Work

March 25, 1911 was a Saturday. At the Triangle Shirtwaist Company, located on the top three floors of a 10-story building in New York City's Greenwich Village, about 500 workers — mostly young immigrant women from Italy and Eastern Europe — were finishing their workday.

They made "shirtwaists," which were popular women's blouses. The workers sat elbow to elbow at long tables, surrounded by fabric scraps, thread, and tissue paper patterns. The floors were covered with lint and cloth cuttings.

At about 4:40 p.m., someone on the 8th floor noticed a fire in a scrap bin under one of the cutting tables. Within minutes, the entire floor was ablaze.

## Everything Went Wrong

The fire spread with terrifying speed. The floors were covered with flammable material. But what turned a bad fire into a catastrophe was a series of terrible decisions by the factory owners:

**The doors were locked.** The factory owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, had ordered the exit doors locked from the outside during working hours. Why? To prevent workers from stealing fabric or taking unauthorized breaks. When the fire broke out, workers couldn't open the doors.

**The fire escape collapsed.** The building had one fire escape — a flimsy, narrow metal structure on the outside of the building. When dozens of workers tried to climb down it at once, the fire escape buckled and fell apart, sending people plunging to the ground.

**The ladders were too short.** Fire department ladders only reached the 7th floor. The fire was on the 8th, 9th, and 10th floors. Firefighters watched helplessly as people appeared in windows high above the ground.

**The elevator stopped working.** The building had two small elevators. The operators made several heroic trips up and down, saving dozens of workers. But eventually the heat warped the elevator shafts and they stopped working.

## The Most Terrible Sight

With the doors locked, the fire escape broken, the ladders too short, and the elevators dead, workers on the upper floors faced an impossible choice: burn to death or jump.

Dozens jumped. Witnesses on the street below watched in horror as young women — some holding hands in pairs — leaped from the 9th-floor windows. The bodies hit the pavement with a sound that witnesses said they could never forget. Firefighters held out nets, but the falling bodies tore right through them.

The fire lasted only about 30 minutes. By the time it was out, 146 people were dead. Most were young women between 16 and 23 years old. The youngest was 14.

## The Investigation

The investigation reports, preserved in the HathiTrust Digital Library, reveal the full extent of the negligence.

Investigators found that:
- The factory had no sprinkler system
- Fire drills had never been conducted
- The locked exit doors directly caused many deaths
- The building had been inspected and passed — despite obvious fire hazards

The factory owners, Blanck and Harris, were charged with manslaughter. Their trial lasted three weeks. Despite overwhelming evidence, the jury acquitted them. The judge had instructed the jury that they could only convict if the owners knew the specific door was locked at the time of the fire — a nearly impossible standard to prove.

The acquittal outraged the public. Blanck and Harris later paid $75 per victim in a civil settlement — about $2,400 in today's money.

## The Fire That Changed America

The Triangle Fire didn't just kill 146 people — it transformed American labor law. Public outrage forced politicians to act:

- **New York created the Factory Investigating Commission**, which inspected workplaces across the state and recommended sweeping reforms
- **Dozens of new labor laws** were passed in New York within three years, covering fire safety, working conditions, and child labor
- **The International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union** grew dramatically, giving workers more power to demand safe conditions
- **Frances Perkins**, who had witnessed the fire from the street, went on to become the first female Cabinet member and the architect of the New Deal's labor protections

Every time you see a fire exit sign in a building, a sprinkler system on the ceiling, or a door that opens outward — those exist because of what happened at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory on March 25, 1911.
