On June 23, 1945, the Battle of Okinawa ended after 82 days of some of the most savage fighting in the Pacific War. The cost was staggering: an estimated 12,000 Americans killed, over 100,000 Japanese military dead, and — most devastatingly — an estimated 100,000 Okinawan civilians killed, roughly one-quarter of the island's population. Okinawans were caught between two armies, and both sides treated them as expendable.

When the guns fell silent, the United States took control of the island and did not let go for 27 years.

## The Keystone of the Pacific

While the rest of Japan regained sovereignty in 1952 under the San Francisco Peace Treaty, Okinawa was explicitly excluded. The United States retained full administrative control over the Ryukyu Islands (Okinawa and surrounding islands) under Article 3 of the treaty. Japan retained "residual sovereignty" — a legal fiction that acknowledged Japan's claim in principle while denying it in practice.

The reason was strategic. Okinawa is located roughly 400 miles from mainland Japan, 500 miles from Taiwan, and 800 miles from the coast of China. It is the ideal location for projecting military power across the western Pacific. During the Cold War, Okinawa hosted nuclear weapons, intelligence operations, and tens of thousands of American troops. During the Vietnam War, B-52 bombers flew combat missions from Okinawa's Kadena Air Base.

## Life Under the Military

For Okinawans, American rule was a mixed experience. The U.S. Civil Administration of the Ryukyu Islands (USCAR) built schools, roads, and hospitals. It promoted democratic local governance and economic development. But it also seized land — often over the objections of local owners — for military bases, paid below-market compensation, and maintained ultimate authority over the island's political life.

Okinawans elected their own chief executive beginning in 1968, but USCAR retained veto power over local legislation. The U.S. dollar was the official currency. American military courts had jurisdiction over crimes committed by service members — a source of particular resentment when serious offenses, including violent crimes against Okinawans, were seen as receiving lenient treatment.

## Reversion

The movement to return Okinawa to Japan grew through the 1960s, fueled by the Vietnam War, land seizures, and a series of incidents involving American service members. Massive protests drew hundreds of thousands of Okinawans into the streets.

In 1969, President Richard Nixon and Prime Minister Eisaku Sato agreed to return Okinawa to Japan. The reversion took effect on May 15, 1972. Okinawa became a Japanese prefecture again. But the military bases stayed.

## The Burden Continues

As of 2026, approximately 70 percent of U.S. military facilities in Japan are concentrated on Okinawa — an island that constitutes less than 1 percent of Japan's total land area. Roughly 15 percent of Okinawa's main island is occupied by American military installations. About 26,000 American troops are stationed there.

The base presence has been a source of persistent tension. Okinawans have protested noise pollution, environmental contamination, aircraft accidents, and crimes committed by American personnel. A 1995 incident in which three U.S. servicemen assaulted a 12-year-old Okinawan girl triggered the largest protests since reversion and led to the creation of the Special Action Committee on Okinawa (SACO) to address base-related issues.

Plans to relocate Marine Corps Air Station Futenma — located in the densely populated city of Ginowan — have been debated since the 1990s. The proposed relocation to a less populated area in northern Okinawa has been fiercely opposed by many Okinawans who want the base removed from the island entirely, not simply moved.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Okinawa's story is about disproportionate burden. Okinawans bear the costs of the U.S.-Japan security alliance — the noise, the land loss, the risks — far more than other Japanese citizens or Americans. In any organization or community, some people carry more than their share of the collective burden. Recognizing that imbalance — and taking steps to address it — is not just fairness. It is the only way to sustain cooperation over the long term.
