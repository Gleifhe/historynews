## When Misery Becomes a Verb

Somewhere in a rain-soaked patrol base, a Marine stands in ankle-deep mud at 3:00 a.m., shivering under a poncho that stopped being waterproof two days ago. The MRE in his cargo pocket has turned to mush. His socks are wet. His feet hurt. There are still nine hours until the end of the mission. Nothing about this situation can be fixed, improved, or escaped. So the Marine next to him says what Marines have said for decades: "Embrace the suck."

The phrase is crude, profane in its full-length versions, and perfectly precise. It means: this situation is terrible, everyone knows it's terrible, and complaining about it won't change anything. So stop fighting the misery and lean into it. Accept the discomfort. Let go of the fantasy that things should be better. And in that acceptance, find a strange kind of freedom.

"Embrace the suck" entered widespread military slang during the Iraq and Afghanistan wars, though the sentiment behind it is as old as soldiering itself. Today, it has migrated into civilian life as one of the most useful mental frameworks veterans carry with them — a counter to a culture that increasingly believes discomfort is a malfunction rather than a feature.

## Historical Context

The military has always operated in conditions that are, by any civilian standard, miserable. But the willingness to endure misery as a deliberate practice — not just an unfortunate side effect of war — has roots that stretch back centuries.

At [Valley Forge in the winter of 1777-1778](/articles/valley-forge/), George Washington's Continental Army endured conditions that make modern field exercises look comfortable. Soldiers lacked shoes, coats, and adequate food. They built crude log huts and stuffed the gaps with clay and straw. Sentries stood watch barefoot in the snow. Nearly 2,000 men died of disease, malnutrition, and exposure that winter.

The soldiers at Valley Forge didn't have the phrase "embrace the suck." But they lived the concept. They endured not because conditions improved — they didn't — but because they stopped expecting conditions to improve and focused instead on what they could control: discipline, training, and mutual support. Baron von Steuben, the Prussian drillmaster who arrived that winter, transformed the ragged army into a professional fighting force precisely because the shared suffering had burned away every illusion. The soldiers who survived Valley Forge were willing to learn because they had already accepted the worst.

The [Chosin Reservoir campaign in 1950](/articles/chosin-reservoir/) offers another example. When 30,000 Marines and soldiers found themselves surrounded by 120,000 Chinese troops in temperatures reaching 35 degrees below zero, the conditions were beyond miserable — they were lethal. Weapons froze. Morphine froze. Blood froze before medics could administer transfusions. The Marines who fought their way out of that encirclement didn't do so by pretending conditions were acceptable. They did it by acknowledging the catastrophe, setting it aside, and focusing on the next step.

The phrase "embrace the suck" itself became ubiquitous during the early years of the Iraq War, when soldiers and Marines faced 130-degree heat, sandstorms, improvised explosive devices, and extended deployments that stretched months beyond their scheduled return dates. The conditions couldn't be changed. The only variable was attitude. Units that adopted an "embrace the suck" mentality — acknowledging the misery openly, even joking about it — consistently showed better cohesion, morale, and operational effectiveness than units that either suppressed complaints or let them fester into bitterness.

## The Core Lesson: Suffering You Accept Loses Its Power

The psychological mechanism behind "embrace the suck" is well-documented. Researchers in resilience psychology distinguish between primary suffering — the actual discomfort of a situation — and secondary suffering — the additional distress caused by resisting, resenting, or wishing the discomfort away. Secondary suffering is almost always worse than primary suffering. The rain is bad. Being furious that it's raining makes it unbearable.

When a soldier "embraces the suck," they are eliminating secondary suffering. They accept the rain, the cold, the exhaustion, the boredom. They stop spending mental energy on the gap between how things are and how things should be. This frees up cognitive and emotional resources for the things that actually matter: the mission, the team, the next task.

This is not stoic suppression. Veterans who embrace the suck are not pretending to be fine. They are acknowledging the discomfort out loud — often with dark humor — and choosing not to let it control their behavior. The laughter is the mechanism. By joking about the misery, they externalize it. It becomes a shared experience rather than a private burden. The suck doesn't go away. But it stops being the center of gravity.

## Real-World Examples

Veterans carry "embrace the suck" into civilian life with remarkable consistency. Entrepreneurs who served describe using the mindset during the early, unglamorous stages of building a business — the months of cold calls, rejections, and 80-hour weeks that precede any success. They recognize the pattern from their military service: this phase is supposed to be hard. Resenting the difficulty wastes energy. Accepting it and working through it is the only path forward.

In healthcare, nurses and paramedics who are veterans often display an unusual ability to function during the worst shifts — mass casualty events, pandemic surges, understaffed overnight rotations. Colleagues describe them as calm, focused, and occasionally funny at exactly the wrong moment. That dark humor is a direct import from military culture: it's how you acknowledge the suck without letting it win.

Athletic coaches have adopted the concept for training programs. CrossFit, obstacle course racing, and endurance events explicitly borrow from military language and culture. The phrase "embrace the suck" appears on gym walls, race bibs, and training manuals. The underlying principle is the same: the workout is supposed to hurt. Stop expecting it not to hurt. Focus on completing the next rep.

Psychologists who work with veterans have noted that "embrace the suck" functions as an informal cognitive-behavioral technique. It reframes unavoidable discomfort from a threat (something that must be eliminated) to a challenge (something that can be endured and even leveraged). This reframing is associated with lower cortisol levels, reduced anxiety, and improved performance under stress.

## Personal Growth

The civilian world in 2026 offers more comfort than any generation in history has ever experienced. Climate-controlled environments, on-demand entertainment, same-day delivery, and algorithmic feeds designed to minimize friction. These are genuine improvements in quality of life. But they come with a hidden cost: a declining tolerance for discomfort.

"Embrace the suck" is not a rejection of comfort. It's a correction for the belief that discomfort is always a signal that something is wrong. Sometimes discomfort is the path itself. The difficult conversation with a friend, the first months at a new job, the grinding hours of learning a new skill — these experiences are uncomfortable precisely because they involve growth. Avoiding the discomfort means avoiding the growth.

The practical application is simple. The next time you encounter a situation that is genuinely unpleasant but unavoidable — a long commute, a tedious assignment, a painful workout, a hard conversation — try the veteran's approach. Acknowledge that it's bad. Say it out loud if you want. Then stop resisting and start moving through it. You'll find that the thing you were dreading was mostly made up of your dread of it.

## How This Connects to 2026

Veterans transitioning to civilian life in 2026 face a paradox. The skills they developed — tolerance for discomfort, functioning under uncertainty, thriving in austere conditions — are exactly the skills that researchers say are most lacking in the broader population. Studies on resilience consistently show that controlled exposure to manageable hardship builds psychological strength, while chronic avoidance of discomfort builds fragility.

This doesn't mean everyone needs to go through military training. It means the principle behind "embrace the suck" is universally applicable: the willingness to endure short-term discomfort for long-term benefit is one of the most reliable predictors of success in any field. Veterans know this because the military taught them the hard way. The rest of us can learn it by choice — which is considerably less painful than learning it in ankle-deep mud at 3:00 a.m.
