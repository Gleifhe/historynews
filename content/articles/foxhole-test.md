## The Question That Cuts Through Everything

Ask a veteran how they evaluate the people in their life and you'll hear some version of the same answer: "Would I trust this person in a foxhole?"

It's not a metaphor to them. It's a lived experience distilled into a single diagnostic question. A foxhole is a shallow, scraped-out hole in the ground where two or three people crouch while the world explodes around them. There is no exit. There is no pretending. There is only the person next to you and the absolute, non-negotiable need to trust them with your life.

The foxhole test isn't about whether someone is fun at a party, impressive on a résumé, or charming over dinner. It's about whether, when everything goes wrong — when the pressure is real and the consequences are permanent — this person will hold the line or fold. Veterans know the difference because they've seen both.

## Historical Context

The foxhole as both a physical structure and a metaphor for extreme shared experience has been central to military culture since World War I, when trench warfare forced soldiers to live, fight, and die in holes dug into the earth. By World War II, the foxhole had become the defining image of the infantry experience — a cramped, miserable, terrifying place where two soldiers depended on each other for survival.

The phrase "there are no atheists in foxholes" — attributed to military chaplain William Thomas Cummings during the Battle of Bataan in 1942 — captured one aspect of the foxhole experience: the way extremity strips away pretense. But the deeper truth, the one that veterans carried home, was about people, not faith. The foxhole revealed character. It showed you who would share their last canteen of water, who would stay awake on watch, and who would freeze, lie, or run.

At the [Chosin Reservoir](/articles/chosin-reservoir/) in 1950, Marines surrounded by 120,000 Chinese troops in subzero temperatures learned the foxhole test under the most extreme conditions imaginable. Men who had seemed reliable in garrison crumbled under the pressure. Others who had been quiet and unnoticed became rocks — calm, steady, unbreakable. The test was not about who talked the best game. It was about who showed up when showing up might kill you.

This pattern repeated in every American conflict that followed. Vietnam. Desert Storm. Iraq. Afghanistan. The uniforms changed, the weapons changed, the terrain changed. The test did not.

## The Core Lesson: Shared Adversity Creates Bonds That Comfort Cannot

Sebastian Junger, a journalist who spent months embedded with U.S. troops in Afghanistan's Korengal Valley, explored this phenomenon in his 2016 book *Tribe: On Homecoming and Belonging*. His central argument is both simple and radical: humans are wired to bond through shared hardship, and modern society has largely eliminated the conditions that produce those bonds.

Junger observed that soldiers in combat zones often experienced a paradox: they were miserable, exhausted, and terrified, yet they described their time in combat as the most meaningful period of their lives. The reason was not that they enjoyed war. The reason was that war gave them something civilian life could not — a small group of people who depended on each other completely, with zero tolerance for selfishness, dishonesty, or cowardice.

"What people miss isn't danger or killing," Junger wrote. "It's the closeness. It's the brotherhood. It's the feeling that nothing you do is just for yourself."

Research supports this. A 2018 study published in *Frontiers in Psychology* found that shared adversity — from military combat to natural disasters to extreme athletic challenges — activates neurological bonding mechanisms that are far stronger than those produced by shared pleasure. The stress hormone cortisol, when experienced collectively, triggers increased oxytocin production, which strengthens trust and social bonding. In plain language: suffering together connects people more deeply than enjoying things together.

This is why the foxhole test works. It isn't asking whether someone is pleasant. It's asking whether someone has been tested — and whether they passed.

## Real-World Examples

The foxhole test has migrated far beyond the military.

**Business.** Former Marine and venture capitalist Todd Connor, founder of The Bunker Labs, has written extensively about how veterans apply the foxhole test to business partnerships. "I don't care about your MBA," Connor told *Inc.* magazine. "I care about what you do when the deal falls apart, the client leaves, and the money runs out. That's the foxhole." The [buddy system principle](/articles/buddy-system-never-alone/) that underpins military operations applies directly: your partner in a startup is your battle buddy. If you wouldn't trust them in a foxhole, don't trust them with your company.

**First Responders.** Firefighters, paramedics, and police officers apply a version of the foxhole test every shift. When a firefighter enters a burning building, they need to know — not hope, know — that the person behind them will pull them out if the floor collapses. This is why first responder culture, like military culture, values reliability over charisma and actions over words.

**Sports.** Legendary basketball coach Pat Riley described the difference between a teammate and a companion: "A teammate is someone who makes you better under pressure. A companion is someone who makes you comfortable." The foxhole test is Riley's distinction in military language. Championship teams are built on foxhole trust — the kind forged in grueling practices, playoff pressure, and the shared experience of losing.

**Medicine.** Surgical teams, particularly in trauma and emergency settings, develop foxhole-level bonds. Dr. Atul Gawande, in his book *Better*, described how operating room teams that have faced crises together develop a nonverbal communication and mutual trust that teams assembled ad hoc simply cannot replicate.

Paul Zak, a neuroeconomist at Claremont Graduate University, demonstrated in a 2017 study published in *Harvard Business Review* that trust within organizations is the single largest predictor of performance, engagement, and satisfaction. His research showed that high-trust organizations outperform low-trust organizations by 286% in total return to shareholders. The foxhole test is, in essence, a trust detector — and trust, Zak proved, is the foundation of everything that works.

## Personal Growth: Applying the Foxhole Test to Your Own Life

You don't need to have served in combat to use the foxhole test. You just need to be honest.

Look at the people in your inner circle — the five or ten people you spend the most time with. Now ask yourself: if things went badly wrong — a health crisis, a financial disaster, a family emergency — which of these people would show up? Not with a text message. Not with "let me know if you need anything." Show up. At your door. With food, or time, or just their presence.

That's your foxhole list. It's probably shorter than your contact list. That's fine. The foxhole test isn't designed to be passed by everyone. It's designed to help you identify the few people who matter most and invest in those relationships accordingly.

The reverse is equally important: are you someone who would pass the foxhole test? When a friend is in crisis, do you show up or do you send a text? When a coworker is struggling, do you step in or step back? The test works in both directions. If you want foxhole-quality people in your life, you need to be a foxhole-quality person yourself.

Veterans understand this reciprocity instinctively. The [bonds they formed in service](/articles/brotherhood-beyond-uniform/) survive decades because they were forged under conditions that demanded total commitment. You can build the same kind of bonds in civilian life — but only if you're willing to be tested and to show up when others are being tested.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the average American has more social media connections than any generation in history — and reports higher levels of loneliness than any generation on record. The Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness noted that the crisis has only deepened.

The paradox makes perfect sense through the lens of the foxhole test. Social media connections are not foxhole relationships. They are low-cost, low-risk, low-depth interactions that create the appearance of connection without its substance. You can have 2,000 followers and not a single person who would show up at your door in a crisis.

Junger warned about exactly this in *Tribe*: "Modern society has perfected the art of making people feel unnecessary. The crisis isn't that we don't have enough friends. It's that we don't have enough people who need us — and whom we need."

The foxhole test is the antidote. It doesn't ask how many people you know. It asks how many people you trust — and how many people trust you. In 2026, that question has never been more important. Start with the people who've already passed the test. Invest there. Build there. Everything else is noise.
