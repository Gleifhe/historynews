On July 4, 1826, the United States turned fifty years old. Across the country, Americans celebrated the Jubilee — the golden anniversary of the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/). Cannons fired, parades marched, and orators delivered speeches praising the Founders. That same day, two of the most important of those Founders took their last breaths.

## Two Rivals, One Document

John Adams and Thomas Jefferson had been at the center of the American experiment from the very beginning. In June 1776, they had served together on the five-member committee tasked with drafting the Declaration of Independence. Jefferson, the younger man and the more elegant writer, composed the initial draft. Adams championed the document on the floor of Congress, arguing passionately for its adoption. "The Atlas of Independence," Jefferson later called him.

The two men had been friends, then bitter political enemies, then friends again. Their rivalry defined the early republic.

## The Bitter Years

The friendship fractured in the 1790s over fundamental disagreements about the direction of the country. Adams, a Federalist, favored a strong central government and closer ties to Britain. Jefferson, a Democratic-Republican, feared centralized power and sympathized with revolutionary France.

The election of 1800 — one of the nastiest in American history — pitted them directly against each other. Jefferson won. Adams, humiliated, left Washington before dawn on Inauguration Day rather than attend his rival's swearing-in. The two men did not speak for twelve years.

## The Reconciliation

In 1812, at the urging of their mutual friend Benjamin Rush, Adams broke the silence with a letter to Jefferson. "You and I ought not to die before we have explained ourselves to each other," Rush had written to Adams. Adams sent Jefferson a brief, warm note on New Year's Day, along with "two pieces of Homespun" — actually two volumes of his son John Quincy's lectures.

Jefferson responded enthusiastically, and one of the greatest correspondences in American history began. Over the next fourteen years, the two men exchanged 158 letters, covering philosophy, politics, religion, science, agriculture, and memory. They debated the meaning of the Revolution they had both led. They reflected on aging, death, and legacy. The letters reveal two extraordinary minds grappling honestly with the hardest questions about democracy, power, and human nature.

## The Jubilee

By the summer of 1826, both men were failing. Jefferson, 83, was bedridden at Monticello, weakened by a combination of ailments. Adams, 90, was confined to his home in Quincy, Massachusetts. Both had been invited to attend Jubilee celebrations in Washington but were too frail to travel.

Jefferson's last conscious words, spoken on July 3, were reportedly a question: "Is it the Fourth?" He was told it soon would be. He held on through the night and died at approximately 12:50 p.m. on July 4.

Adams, in Quincy, did not know Jefferson had died. His last words, spoken that afternoon, were: "Thomas Jefferson survives." He was wrong by a few hours. Adams died at approximately 6:20 p.m.

## A Nation's Response

When news spread — slowly, by the standards of 1826 — the nation was thunderstruck. That both men had died on the exact fiftieth anniversary of the document they had created together seemed too meaningful to be coincidence. Eulogists and preachers declared it a sign of divine approval. Daniel Webster delivered the most famous eulogy, treating the twin deaths as proof that the American experiment had been blessed from the start.

The coincidence remains one of the most remarkable in American history. James Monroe, the fifth president, would die on July 4, 1831, adding another layer of improbability.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Adams and Jefferson remind us that no disagreement has to be permanent. They were political enemies who said terrible things about each other, spent a decade in bitter silence, and then found their way back to friendship through honest conversation. The relationship that resulted — those 158 letters — was richer for having survived the rupture. If a relationship matters to you, the path back is almost always available. The question is whether you are willing to write the first letter.
