## The Largest Aircraft Ever Built

The LZ 129 Hindenburg was enormous — 804 feet long, longer than three football fields laid end to end. It was the largest aircraft ever built, and it was the pride of Nazi Germany's airship program.

The Hindenburg could cross the Atlantic Ocean in just two and a half days — far faster than any ocean liner. Inside, passengers enjoyed a dining room, a lounge with a grand piano, private cabins with real beds, and promenade decks with windows that could be opened in flight. A one-way ticket from Germany to America cost $400 — about $8,500 in today's money.

The Hindenburg had already made 10 successful round trips across the Atlantic. Its arrival at the Lakehurst Naval Air Station in New Jersey on May 6, 1937 was supposed to be routine.

## 37 Seconds of Horror

The Hindenburg arrived at Lakehurst at about 7:00 p.m. after a delayed flight from Frankfurt, Germany. Thunderstorms in the area forced the airship to circle while waiting for weather to clear.

At 7:25 p.m., the crew dropped mooring lines to ground handlers below. Then, at 7:25 p.m., observers noticed a small flame near the tail of the airship.

What happened next took only **37 seconds**.

The flame spread with terrifying speed. The hydrogen gas that kept the Hindenburg aloft was extremely flammable. The fire raced along the top of the airship, and within seconds the entire tail section was engulfed. The Hindenburg's nose tilted upward as the burning tail crashed to the ground. Then the entire structure collapsed in a massive fireball.

## "Oh, the Humanity!"

Radio reporter Herbert Morrison of WLS Chicago was at Lakehurst to cover the landing. His live broadcast captured the moment of the disaster — and his emotional breakdown became one of the most famous pieces of audio in history:

> "It's burst into flames! It's falling, it's crashing! Get out of the way, please! It's burning, bursting into flames, and it's falling on the mooring mast. Oh, the humanity, and all the passengers!"

Morrison's anguished voice cracking with emotion defined the disaster for the American public. Newsreel cameras also captured the explosion on film, making it one of the first disasters to be extensively documented on camera.

## The Casualties

Of the 97 people aboard (36 passengers and 61 crew), 35 died in the fire. One ground crew member was also killed, bringing the total to **36 deaths**. Remarkably, 62 people survived — many by jumping from the burning wreckage as it hit the ground.

The relatively low death toll surprised many people. Given the speed and intensity of the fire, the fact that nearly two-thirds of those aboard survived was almost miraculous. Many survivors credited their escape to the airship's design — the passenger compartments were located beneath the gas cells, and as the Hindenburg fell, some passengers were able to jump from relatively low heights.

## What Caused the Fire?

The exact cause of the Hindenburg fire has been debated for nearly 90 years:

- **Static electricity**: The leading theory is that an electrical discharge — perhaps caused by the thunderstorm conditions — ignited leaking hydrogen gas
- **Sabotage**: Some investigators suspected sabotage by anti-Nazi agents, but no evidence was ever found
- **The outer covering**: Some researchers have suggested that the airship's outer fabric, which was coated with a compound similar to rocket fuel, may have been the initial source of ignition

Most modern experts believe it was a combination of hydrogen leaking from the gas cells and a static spark that ignited it.

## The End of the Airship Era

The Hindenburg disaster didn't kill the most people or cause the most damage of any transportation accident. But it killed something bigger: **public confidence in airship travel.**

The dramatic film footage and Morrison's emotional broadcast made the disaster vivid and unforgettable. Newspapers across the world — documented in the Newspapers.com archive — ran massive front-page photos of the burning airship.

Within months, commercial airship service was dead. Germany scrapped its remaining airships. The age of lighter-than-air travel was over, replaced by the faster, heavier-than-air airplane.

The Hindenburg remains a cautionary tale about the intersection of technology, hubris, and the power of media to shape public perception.
