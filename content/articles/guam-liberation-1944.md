On July 21, 1944, U.S. Marines stormed the beaches of Guam, beginning a three-week battle to retake the island from Japanese forces that had occupied it since December 1941. The liberation of Guam was a moment of genuine relief for the island's indigenous Chamorro people, who had endured nearly three years of brutality under Japanese military rule. But liberation did not bring independence. Guam would remain what it had been since 1898: an American possession.

## Three Empires

Guam's history is a history of being claimed by empires. Spain colonized the island in 1668 and governed it for over 230 years. When the [Spanish-American War](/articles/spanish-american-war-1898/) ended in 1898, Spain ceded Guam to the United States along with Puerto Rico and the Philippines. The transfer was almost an afterthought — the American captain who arrived to claim Guam found that the Spanish garrison did not even know the two countries were at war.

The U.S. Navy administered Guam from 1899 to 1941. The Chamorro people were U.S. nationals but not citizens. They had no self-government. The naval governor had absolute authority.

## Japanese Occupation

Hours after the attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, Japanese forces attacked Guam. The small American garrison — about 400 Marines and sailors — surrendered after two days. What followed was one of the most harrowing occupations of the Pacific War.

The Japanese renamed the island Omiya Jima (Great Shrine Island) and imposed military rule. Chamorros were forced to learn Japanese, bow to Japanese soldiers, and labor on military construction projects. Thousands were confined in concentration camps. Torture, execution, and sexual violence were commonplace. An estimated 1,000 Chamorros died during the occupation out of a prewar population of about 20,000.

## Liberation and Its Limits

The 1944 liberation was costly — over 1,700 Americans and an estimated 18,000 Japanese were killed in the fighting. For the Chamorro people, July 21 remains the most important date on the calendar, celebrated annually as Liberation Day.

After the war, the Organic Act of Guam (1950) granted Guam's residents U.S. citizenship and a measure of self-government. But the island remained — and remains — an unincorporated territory. Its residents can vote in primary elections but not in general presidential elections. Guam sends a delegate to the U.S. House of Representatives who can participate in debate and committees but cannot vote on the floor.

## The Military Island

Guam's primary value to the United States has always been strategic. The island is the westernmost U.S. territory, located roughly 6,000 miles from San Francisco and 1,500 miles from Tokyo. It hosts major Air Force and Navy installations — Andersen Air Force Base and Naval Base Guam — that together occupy roughly 27 percent of the island's land area.

During the Vietnam War, B-52 bombers launched from Andersen to bomb targets in Southeast Asia. In the 21st century, Guam has become a central hub of American military power in the Pacific, with major expansions underway to accommodate forces relocated from Okinawa.

The military presence brings jobs and economic activity but also raises concerns about land use, environmental contamination, and the militarization of daily life. Many Chamorros feel caught between gratitude for liberation and frustration at being governed by a nation in which they have no full political voice.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Guam's story asks an uncomfortable question: what does citizenship mean if it does not include full political rights? The people of Guam serve in the U.S. military at one of the highest per capita rates of any U.S. jurisdiction. They pay Social Security taxes, serve on juries, and carry U.S. passports. But they cannot vote for the commander-in-chief who may send them to war. When you benefit from someone's contributions, ask whether they benefit equally from yours. Reciprocity is the foundation of legitimate authority.
