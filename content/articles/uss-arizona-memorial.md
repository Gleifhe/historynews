## Seven Minutes That Changed Everything

At 7:55 AM on December 7, 1941, the first wave of Japanese aircraft appeared over Pearl Harbor. The battleship USS Arizona was moored at berth F-7 on Battleship Row, alongside Ford Island. Her crew was just beginning their Sunday morning routine.

At approximately 8:06 AM, a 1,760-pound armor-piercing bomb dropped from a Japanese high-altitude bomber struck the Arizona near her forward ammunition magazines. The bomb penetrated the deck and ignited over a million pounds of gunpowder stored below.

The explosion was catastrophic. A massive fireball erupted hundreds of feet into the air. The entire forward section of the ship was destroyed in seconds. The Arizona sank in less than nine minutes, settling into the mud of Pearl Harbor with most of her crew still trapped inside.

Of the 1,512 men aboard the Arizona that morning, 1,177 were killed — nearly half of all American deaths at [Pearl Harbor](/articles/pearl-harbor-attack/). Many died instantly in the explosion. Others were trapped below decks as the ship sank and the water rushed in. Some survivors later recalled hearing tapping from inside the hull for days afterward — men trying to signal for rescue from compartments that could not be reached.

## The Men of the Arizona

The Arizona's crew came from every corner of the United States. They were farm boys from Iowa, factory workers from Pennsylvania, fishermen from Louisiana, and students from California. The youngest were just 17 years old. Many had joined the Navy during the Depression because there were no other jobs.

Among the dead were 23 sets of brothers — pairs of siblings who had enlisted together and were assigned to the same ship. The Beckers from Kansas. The Dohertys from Texas. The Murdocks from Alabama. Families across America lost two sons in a single morning.

The Arizona's band, a 21-member unit called "U.S.S. Arizona Band Unit Number 22," had won the fleet's Battle of Music competition just days earlier. All 21 musicians were killed. Today, they are the only complete military band to have been lost in action in U.S. history.

## Why the Arizona Was Not Raised

After the attack, the Navy raised and repaired most of the battleships sunk at Pearl Harbor. The USS West Virginia, USS California, USS Tennessee, and USS Nevada all returned to service and fought in later battles. But the Arizona was too badly damaged. Her back was broken, her forward section was destroyed, and her hull was shattered.

The Navy salvaged what it could — guns, ammunition, and equipment — but left the ship where she sank. Over time, a decision was made to leave the Arizona as a permanent memorial and tomb for the men still inside her.

## The Memorial

In 1958, President Dwight D. Eisenhower approved the creation of a memorial over the sunken Arizona. Designed by architect Alfred Preis, the white structure spans the midsection of the submerged ship without touching it. It was dedicated on Memorial Day 1962.

The memorial has three sections. The entry room is open and contemplative. The central assembly area, used for ceremonies, features seven open windows on each wall and a large opening in the floor through which visitors can look down at the sunken ship below. The shrine room at the far end contains a marble wall inscribed with the names of all 1,177 men who died aboard the Arizona.

Survivors of the Arizona are given the option to be interred within the ship's wreckage upon their death. Their cremated remains are placed inside the ship by Navy divers — rejoining their shipmates. As of 2026, the last known living survivors have used or declined this option, as the final Arizona crew members have passed away.

## The Oil Still Flows

More than 80 years after the attack, the Arizona continues to leak oil. About two quarts seep from the wreck each day, rising to the surface in small shimmering drops. Visitors to the memorial can see the oil shimmering on the water.

Some people call these drops "the tears of the Arizona" or "black tears." The oil comes from approximately 500,000 gallons of fuel oil that were still in the ship's bunkers when she sank. Navy researchers and conservationists have studied the wreck extensively to monitor its condition. The hull is slowly deteriorating, and experts predict that within the coming decades, the remaining oil could be released in larger quantities.

Whether to remove the oil or leave the ship undisturbed remains a subject of careful debate. Any intervention could disturb the remains of the 1,177 men still inside — men whose families regard the Arizona as a sacred grave.

## A Place of Remembrance

The USS Arizona Memorial is the most visited attraction in Hawaii, drawing more than two million visitors per year. It is operated by the National Park Service as part of Pearl Harbor National Memorial.

The memorial also serves as a powerful place of reconciliation. Japanese visitors frequently leave flowers and pay their respects. In 2016, Japanese Prime Minister Shinzo Abe visited the memorial alongside President Barack Obama — the first sitting Japanese leader to do so. The visit echoed the spirit of reconciliation seen at other WWII commemorations, from [Iwo Jima](/articles/iwo-jima-flag-photo/) to the beaches of [Normandy](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/).

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The USS Arizona teaches us about the weight of sacrifice. The men aboard that ship did not choose to die that morning. They were eating breakfast, writing letters, and getting ready for a quiet Sunday. Their deaths remind us that freedom has a cost, and that cost is often paid by people who never expected to be called upon.

The decision to leave the Arizona where she sank — rather than raise and scrap her — speaks to the importance of remembrance. Some things should not be cleaned up, moved on from, or forgotten. Some losses deserve permanent markers. In our personal lives, too, there are events and people that deserve to be remembered, not just filed away.

The story of the 23 sets of brothers is especially deeply moving. After the losses at Pearl Harbor, the military changed its policy to avoid assigning siblings to the same ship or unit. One tragedy led to a change that protected future families. Learning from loss — making sure it doesn't happen again — is one of the most meaningful things anyone can do.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the USS Arizona Memorial continues to serve as both a war memorial and a symbol of the costs of unpreparedness. As the last World War II veterans pass away, the memorial becomes the primary way younger generations connect with the events of December 7, 1941.

The memorial also raises questions about how we preserve historical sites. Climate change, rising sea levels, and the natural deterioration of the ship's hull present growing challenges. Balancing preservation with respect for the dead — and the practical realities of managing a site visited by millions — is a challenge that reflects broader conversations about how societies honor their past.

The Arizona's story is also a reminder that behind every statistic is a human being. "2,403 killed at Pearl Harbor" is a number. The 21 musicians, the 23 pairs of brothers, the 17-year-old sailors — those are people. When we remember history in terms of individuals, it becomes impossible to be indifferent.
