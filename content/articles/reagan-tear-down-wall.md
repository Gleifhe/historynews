## A City Still Divided

By 1987, the Berlin Wall had stood for 26 years. It cut through the heart of Germany's largest city, separating families, friends, and neighbors. On one side was West Berlin — free, prosperous, and connected to the democratic world. On the other was East Berlin — controlled by the Soviet-backed communist government, where people could be shot for trying to leave.

President Ronald Reagan was visiting West Berlin on June 12, 1987, as part of a European tour. He was scheduled to give a speech at the Brandenburg Gate, one of the most famous landmarks in Berlin — and right next to the wall itself.

What happened that day became one of the defining moments of the Cold War.

## The Speech Nobody Wanted

Reagan's speechwriter, Peter Robinson, had visited Berlin weeks earlier to prepare. He asked a local woman what she wanted Reagan to say. Her answer was simple: "Tell them to tear down this wall."

Robinson wrote the line into the speech. Then the trouble started.

Officials at the State Department and the National Security Council were horrified. They thought the line was too provocative, too aggressive, too dangerous. It might insult the Soviet leader, Mikhail Gorbachev. It could set back diplomatic progress. They tried to have the line removed — not once, but multiple times. Different versions of the speech were submitted with the line taken out.

Each time, Reagan put it back in. On the morning of the speech, Deputy Chief of Staff Kenneth Duberstein asked Reagan if he was going to deliver the controversial line. Reagan's response: **"I'm the president, aren't I?"**

## The Moment

Standing before a crowd of about 20,000 people, with the Berlin Wall and the Brandenburg Gate as his backdrop, Reagan delivered the words that would define his presidency:

**"General Secretary Gorbachev, if you seek peace, if you seek prosperity for the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, if you seek liberalization, come here to this gate. Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"**

The crowd erupted. On the East Berlin side, the government had set up loudspeakers blasting music to drown out Reagan's words — but many East Berliners heard him anyway.

## The Reaction

Strangely, the speech did not make big headlines at the time. Many American journalists dismissed it as empty rhetoric — tough talk with no real consequences. The New York Times barely covered it. Some commentators called it simplistic or naïve.

The Soviet news agency TASS called it "warmongering" and "openly provocative." East German officials denounced it as an insult. Even some of Reagan's allies in Europe thought he had gone too far.

But ordinary people, especially those living behind the Iron Curtain, heard something different. They heard the leader of the free world demanding their freedom — loudly, publicly, and without apology.

## Two Years Later

On November 9, 1989 — just two years and five months after Reagan's speech — the Berlin Wall fell. East German citizens poured through the checkpoints, and people began tearing the wall apart with hammers and their bare hands.

Reagan was no longer president by then. George H.W. Bush was in office when the wall came down. But many historians credit Reagan's speech with helping to create the political pressure that led to the wall's fall.

Whether Reagan's words actually caused the wall to come down is still debated. The Soviet Union was already weakening. Gorbachev's reforms were already underway. But there is no question that those four words — "tear down this wall" — became the most memorable call for freedom of the Cold War era.

## Why It Still Matters

Reagan's Berlin Wall speech is a reminder that words matter. A single sentence, delivered at the right moment, can capture the hopes of millions of people and change the way the world sees a conflict. The speech is studied in schools, quoted by politicians, and remembered by people who lived through the Cold War as the moment when someone finally said out loud what everyone was thinking.
