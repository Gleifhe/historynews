## Words That Outlived Their Writers

The night before battle, soldiers write letters.

They have done so for as long as wars have been fought and postal systems have existed. In tents and foxholes, in barracks and base camps, in the holds of transport ships and the cargo bays of aircraft, Americans heading into combat have sat down with pen and paper and tried to say the things that matter most — knowing that these words might be the last their families ever receive.

The Library of Congress Veterans History Project, established by Congress in 2000, has collected and preserved more than 100,000 personal narratives from American veterans of every conflict from World War I to the present, including thousands of letters, diaries, and personal papers. Combined with the National Archives' holdings of Civil War and earlier correspondence, these collections form an extraordinary archive of the American experience of war — told not by generals or politicians, but by the men and women who carried the rifles.

## Historical Context: The Tradition of the Last Letter

The practice of writing a final letter before battle is deeply embedded in American military culture. During the Civil War, when mass literacy and a functioning postal system coincided with industrial-scale warfare for the first time, letter writing became a lifeline between soldiers and their families.

The most famous of all American war letters was written on July 14, 1861, by Major Sullivan Ballou of the 2nd Rhode Island Infantry to his wife, Sarah. Ballou was 32 years old, a lawyer, a former speaker of the Rhode Island House of Representatives, and the father of two young sons. He knew his regiment was about to march toward Manassas, Virginia, and he suspected he would not return.

"I have no misgivings about, or lack of confidence in, the cause in which I am engaged," Ballou wrote, "and my courage does not halt or falter." Then he turned to what truly weighed on his heart: "But, my dear wife, when I know that with my own joys I lay down nearly all of yours, and replace them in this life with cares and sorrows — when, after having eaten for long years the bitter fruit of orphanage myself, I must offer it as their only sustenance to my dear little children — is it weak or dishonorable, while the banner of my purpose floats calmly and proudly in the breeze, that my unbounded love for you, my darling wife and children, should struggle in fierce, though useless, contest with my love of country?"

Sullivan Ballou was killed at the First Battle of Bull Run on July 21, 1861. His [letter to Sarah](/articles/sullivan-ballou-letter/) became one of the most celebrated documents in American history, immortalized by Ken Burns in his 1990 documentary *The Civil War*.

## The Event: Voices Across the Wars

What makes the collected letters of American soldiers so remarkable is not any single letter but the consistency of their themes across more than 160 years of conflict. Whether written by a Union private in 1863, a doughboy in 1918, a Marine on Iwo Jima in 1945, a soldier in the Ia Drang Valley in 1965, or a sergeant in Helmand Province in 2010, the letters return again and again to the same subjects: love for family, loyalty to comrades, fear honestly acknowledged, and a determination to do one's duty regardless of the outcome.

During World War II, the volume of military mail was staggering. The U.S. Postal Service handled an estimated 2.5 billion pieces of mail to and from military personnel between 1941 and 1945. The V-mail system, which photographed letters onto microfilm to save cargo space, processed more than a billion pieces alone. For soldiers serving thousands of miles from home, mail call was often the most important moment of the day.

The letters from World War II reveal soldiers grappling with experiences that defied description. After the liberation of concentration camps, some soldiers wrote home about what they had seen. Others could not bring themselves to describe it and wrote only that they had witnessed things they would never speak of. Both responses — the compulsion to tell and the inability to tell — are preserved in the archives.

In Korea, soldiers wrote of bitter cold, desperate fighting, and a sense that their war was being forgotten even as it was being fought. In Vietnam, the letters shifted in tone as the war dragged on — early letters reflected confidence and purpose; later ones expressed disillusionment, exhaustion, and anger. The names of many of these letter writers are now etched on the [Vietnam Veterans Memorial Wall](/articles/vietnam-veterans-memorial-wall/) in Washington, D.C.

The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq produced a new kind of war correspondence. Soldiers still wrote traditional letters, but they also sent emails, recorded video messages, and posted on social media. The Veterans History Project has adapted to collect these digital communications alongside handwritten letters, recognizing that the medium changes but the human need to reach across the distance does not.

## Aftermath: Preserving the Voices

The Library of Congress Veterans History Project relies on volunteers — family members, students, community organizations — to collect and submit veterans' personal narratives. The project accepts letters, diaries, photographs, maps, and audio or video recordings from veterans of all branches and all conflicts from World War I forward. Each submission is cataloged, preserved, and made accessible to researchers and the public through the Library's digital collections.

The importance of this work grows with each passing year. As veterans of World War II and Korea pass away, their letters and papers are often discovered by children and grandchildren going through belongings. Without a systematic effort to collect and preserve these materials, they would be lost — thrown away, stored in attics until they disintegrated, or simply forgotten.

Some of the most powerful letters in the collection were never sent. Soldiers sometimes wrote letters to be delivered only in the event of their death — sealed, addressed, and left with a comrade or chaplain. These "just in case" letters are among the most emotionally raw documents in the archives. Stripped of any need to reassure or protect, they say what the writer truly felt.

## Personal Growth: The Discipline of Saying What Matters

The soldiers who wrote these letters faced a question that most of us will never confront with the same urgency: if these are my last words, what should they be?

Their answers are remarkably consistent, and remarkably instructive. Almost without exception, the final letters of soldiers who did not return are about love. Not about politics, not about strategy, not about the rightness of the cause. About love — for wives, husbands, children, parents, brothers, sisters, and friends. About gratitude for the life they had been given, however short. About the hope that their sacrifice would mean something.

This clarity — the ability to strip away everything that doesn't matter and focus on what does — is something the rest of us can learn from without waiting for a battlefield to teach it. The soldier writing his last letter knows exactly what matters to him. Most of us spend years — sometimes entire lifetimes — avoiding that knowledge.

The practice of writing honestly to the people who matter most does not require a war. It requires only the willingness to be vulnerable. To say "I love you" without qualification. To say "I'm proud of you" without waiting for a special occasion. To say "I'm sorry" without being asked. The soldiers in these letters did not have the luxury of waiting for the right moment. They wrote what they felt because they were running out of moments.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Veterans History Project continues its work, though the urgency has shifted. The project's primary focus has expanded to collecting narratives from post-9/11 veterans, many of whom are now in their thirties, forties, and fifties — old enough to reflect on their experiences, young enough to have decades of life ahead.

The digital age has transformed military correspondence but has not diminished its emotional power. A 2010 email from a soldier in Afghanistan to his daughter, telling her to be brave and do her homework, carries the same weight as Sullivan Ballou's 1861 letter to Sarah. The technology changes; the humanity does not.

In an era when communication is instantaneous and constant, the idea of a final letter — carefully composed, deeply considered, possibly never read — feels almost anachronistic. But the need it serves is timeless. Every person, whether in uniform or not, has things they would want to say if they knew their time was short. The soldiers in these letters remind us that the time is always shorter than we think, and the words always matter more than we realize.

The Library of Congress invites all Americans to contribute to the Veterans History Project. The stories of those who served belong not just to their families but to the nation. Preserving them is an act of remembrance — and a gift to every generation that follows.
