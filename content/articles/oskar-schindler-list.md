In the darkest chapter of the twentieth century, when millions of Jewish people were being murdered across Europe, one unlikely hero stepped forward. Oskar Schindler was a German businessman — a member of the Nazi Party, a drinker, a gambler, and a man who had come to occupied Poland to make money. But something changed inside him when he saw the horror of what was happening to innocent people. By the end of World War II, he had saved more than 1,200 Jewish lives and spent every penny he had doing it.

Schindler arrived in Krakow, Poland, in 1939, shortly after Germany invaded the country. He took over a Jewish-owned enamelware factory and began producing pots, pans, and later ammunition shells for the German army. At first, he hired Jewish workers simply because they were cheaper labor. But as the Nazi regime tightened its grip and began sending Jewish people to concentration camps, Schindler started to see his factory differently. It was no longer just a business — it was a place where people could be protected.

The turning point came when Schindler witnessed a brutal raid on the Krakow ghetto in March 1943. He watched from a hilltop as Nazi soldiers dragged families from their homes, beat old men in the streets, and shot children. The horror of what he saw shook him to his core. From that moment, he dedicated himself to saving as many lives as possible.

Schindler used every trick he could think of. He bribed Nazi officials with black market goods, expensive liquor, and cash. He argued that his workers were "essential" to the war effort and could not be sent away. He added names to his employee list — a document that would later be called "Schindler's List" — including children, elderly people, and others who had no factory skills at all. Every name on that list was a life saved from the death camps.

When the Nazis began closing the camps in Poland and moving prisoners to Auschwitz, Schindler convinced authorities to let him relocate his factory and workers to Brünnlitz in his home region of Czechoslovakia. He spent enormous sums of money to make this happen. At the new factory, he deliberately produced faulty ammunition so that his work would not help the Nazi war machine. He also bought food and medicine for his workers on the black market, spending his entire fortune in the process.

By the time the war ended in May 1945, Schindler had saved 1,200 Jewish men, women, and children. He was completely broke. The workers he had saved gave him a ring made from a gold dental filling, inscribed with a quote from the Talmud: "Whoever saves one life saves the world entire."

After the war, Schindler struggled financially. His businesses in Germany and Argentina failed. But the people he saved — known as "Schindlerjuden" or "Schindler Jews" — never forgot him. They supported him with money and gratitude for the rest of his life. In 1962, the Israeli Holocaust memorial Yad Vashem recognized him as "Righteous Among the Nations," one of the highest honors given to non-Jewish people who risked their lives to save Jews during [the Holocaust](/articles/the-holocaust/).

Schindler died in 1974 at the age of 66. At his own request, he was buried in Jerusalem on Mount Zion — the only former Nazi Party member to be honored with burial there. Today, the descendants of the people he saved number over 8,000.

In 1993, director Steven Spielberg brought Schindler's story to the world with the film *Schindler's List*. The movie won seven Academy Awards and helped ensure that Schindler's courage would never be forgotten. His story was also central to the evidence gathered during the [Nuremberg Trials](/articles/nuremberg-trials/), which brought Nazi leaders to justice after the war.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Oskar Schindler's story teaches us that anyone can choose to do the right thing, no matter their past. Schindler was not a perfect person. He was greedy, he was selfish, and he joined the Nazi Party for personal gain. But when he saw innocent people suffering, he made a choice that changed everything. He decided that human life was worth more than money, comfort, or safety.

This is a powerful lesson for all of us. You don't have to be a saint to make a difference. You just have to be willing to act when you see something wrong. Standing up for others — even when it's scary or costly — is one of the most important things a person can do.

Schindler also shows us that small actions can grow into something huge. He started by protecting a few workers. Over time, his efforts grew until he was saving over a thousand lives. When you start helping others, even in small ways, you never know how far your impact will reach.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the lessons of Schindler's story are more relevant than ever. Around the world, refugees and displaced people need help from ordinary citizens who are willing to stand up. Organizations like UNHCR and local volunteer groups continue Schindler's legacy by offering shelter, food, and safety to those fleeing violence and persecution.

The power of one person's courage to inspire millions is also visible in how we tell stories today. Films, documentaries, and social media help share the stories of heroes like Schindler with new generations. In classrooms around the world, students study the Holocaust and learn that even in the worst circumstances, there is always a choice to do good.

Schindler reminds us that moral courage — the willingness to risk everything for what is right — is not outdated. It is the foundation of every just society. As we face our own challenges in 2026, from social injustice to humanitarian crises, his example calls us to ask: what am I willing to do for others?
