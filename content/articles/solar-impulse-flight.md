On July 26, 2016, a strange-looking airplane touched down in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates. It had enormous wings — wider than a Boeing 747 — covered in more than 17,000 solar cells. It weighed only about 5,000 pounds, less than most cars. And it had just done something no aircraft had ever done before: it flew all the way around the world without using a single drop of fuel.

Solar Impulse 2 was the dream of two Swiss adventurers, Bertrand Piccard and André Borschberg. Piccard was an explorer and psychiatrist who had already made history by completing the first non-stop balloon flight around the world in 1999. Borschberg was an engineer and pilot with a passion for clean energy. Together, they spent more than a decade designing, building, and testing an aircraft that could fly day and night using only the power of the sun.

The journey began in Abu Dhabi on March 9, 2015 and was completed on July 26, 2016 — a total of 17 legs across four continents, two oceans, and three seas. The plane flew 26,744 miles at an average speed of about 45 miles per hour. That is slower than a car on the highway, but speed was not the point. The point was to prove that solar energy could power sustained, long-distance flight.

The flight was anything but easy. Solar Impulse 2 could carry only one pilot at a time, so Piccard and Borschberg took turns flying different legs. The cockpit was tiny — about the size of a phone booth. The pilot had to stay awake for days at a time during ocean crossings, sleeping only in short naps of 20 minutes. During the leg from Japan to Hawaii, Borschberg flew for nearly five days straight, setting a world record for the longest solo flight in history.

During that same Pacific crossing, the aircraft's batteries overheated, causing damage that grounded the plane in Hawaii for nine months of repairs. Many people thought the mission was over. But Piccard and Borschberg refused to quit. They fixed the batteries, waited for the right weather, and continued their journey in April 2016. Four months later, they landed back in Abu Dhabi to a hero's welcome.

The aircraft itself was a marvel of engineering. Its four electric motors were powered by lithium batteries that charged during the day using the solar panels on the wings. At night, the plane slowly descended, gliding on stored energy until sunrise brought new power. The whole system was incredibly efficient, wasting almost nothing.

Over a century earlier, the [Wright Brothers made their first powered flight](/articles/wright-brothers-first-flight/) at Kitty Hawk — a journey of just 120 feet that lasted 12 seconds. Solar Impulse 2 traveled more than 26,000 miles. The progression from those first few seconds of powered flight to a solar-powered trip around the globe shows how far human ingenuity can take us when we dare to dream big.

Piccard and Borschberg were clear about their mission. They were not trying to start a solar airline. They wanted to change how people think about clean energy. "This is not just a first in the history of aviation," Piccard said after landing. "It's a first in the history of energy." Their message was simple: if a fragile solar airplane could fly around the world, then clean energy solutions could be applied to buildings, cars, factories, and every other part of daily life.

After the flight, Piccard launched the World Alliance for Efficient Solutions, a project of the Solar Impulse Foundation dedicated to finding 1,000 practical clean technology solutions that are both good for the environment and profitable. By 2021, the foundation had identified all 1,000, ranging from smart building insulation to water purification systems. The principles behind clean energy innovation echo the same spirit as the [Clean Air Act](/articles/clean-air-act/), which proved that environmental protection and economic progress can go hand in hand.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The Solar Impulse story is about refusing to accept limits. Almost everyone told Piccard and Borschberg that a solar-powered flight around the world was impossible. The plane was too slow. The batteries were too weak. The weather was too unpredictable. But instead of accepting those limits, they worked around them. They found creative solutions to every problem.

In your own life, you will hear people say that something cannot be done. Sometimes they will be right. But often, the biggest barrier is not the obstacle itself — it is the belief that the obstacle cannot be overcome. Piccard and Borschberg spent 13 years working toward a goal that most people thought was crazy. Their persistence paid off. The lesson is clear: do not let other people's doubts become your own.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, solar energy is one of the fastest-growing power sources in the world. Solar panels are cheaper and more efficient than ever. Electric vehicles are on roads everywhere. Many of the technologies that seemed experimental when Solar Impulse 2 flew are now mainstream.

But the spirit of the flight remains important. The world still faces enormous energy challenges. Fossil fuels still dominate global energy use. Climate change is accelerating. Solar Impulse 2 reminds us that bold action and creative thinking are not just nice ideas — they are necessary. Every solar panel installed, every efficient building designed, and every clean technology adopted is a step toward the future that Piccard and Borschberg imagined from their tiny cockpit, 30,000 feet above the Earth.
