On January 10, 1776, a 47-page pamphlet went on sale in Philadelphia for two shillings. It had no author's name on the cover — just the title *Common Sense* and the modest description "Written by an Englishman." Within three months, it had sold over 100,000 copies. By the end of the year, an estimated 500,000 copies were in circulation in a country of 2.5 million people. Adjusted for today's population, that would be equivalent to selling over 65 million copies.

## The Unknown Author

Thomas Paine was an unlikely revolutionary. He was 37 years old, a recent immigrant from England, and a serial failure. He had been a corset maker, a tax collector (fired twice), a grocer, and a teacher. His first marriage ended when his wife died. His second marriage ended in separation. He was broke and directionless when he met Benjamin Franklin in London in 1774.

Franklin, impressed by Paine's intelligence and writing ability, gave him a letter of introduction and suggested he try his luck in America. Paine arrived in Philadelphia in November 1774 and found work editing a magazine. He also found a cause.

## The Argument

*Common Sense* made three arguments that were, in 1776, genuinely radical.

First, Paine attacked monarchy itself — not just King George III, but the entire idea that one person should rule millions by accident of birth. "Of more worth is one honest man to society," Paine wrote, "than all the crowned ruffians that ever lived." This was shocking. Most colonists still considered themselves loyal British subjects with a grievance against Parliament. Paine told them that the king himself was the problem.

Second, Paine argued that reconciliation with Britain was not just unlikely but undesirable. The colonies had outgrown the mother country. "There is something absurd," he wrote, "in supposing a Continent to be perpetually governed by an island." America's destiny was independence, not reform.

Third, Paine laid out a practical vision for an independent American republic — a representative democracy with no king, no hereditary aristocracy, and a written constitution. He made self-government sound not just possible but obvious.

## The Impact

The effect was electric. George Washington reported that *Common Sense* was "working a powerful change in the minds of many men." John Adams, who had his own disagreements with Paine, admitted that "without the pen of the author of *Common Sense*, the sword of Washington would have been raised in vain."

Before *Common Sense*, independence was a fringe position even among Patriots. After it, independence became the mainstream demand. The pamphlet did not cause the [Declaration of Independence](/articles/declaration-of-independence/) — many factors converged — but it created the public consensus that made the Declaration possible.

Paine donated his royalties to the Continental Army. He would go on to write *The American Crisis* ("These are the times that try men's souls"), serve in the Revolution, and play a role in the French Revolution. He died in poverty in 1809, largely forgotten by the nation he helped create.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Paine proved that the right words at the right time can change the course of history. He was not a general, a politician, or a wealthy landowner. He was a failed corset maker with a pen and a clear argument. If you see something that needs to be said, do not wait for someone more credentialed to say it. Clarity and conviction matter more than credentials.
