## They Marched Into History

On the evening of July 18, 1863, roughly 600 men of the 54th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry formed a battle line on the sands of Morris Island, South Carolina. Ahead of them rose Fort Wagner, a heavily fortified Confederate earthwork guarding the approach to Charleston Harbor. They knew what they were about to do. They knew many of them would not survive it.

At the front of the column stood Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, a 25-year-old white officer from a prominent abolitionist family in Boston. Behind him stood the men who had volunteered to prove something that much of white America refused to believe — that Black men could fight, and fight well. In the next hour, they would settle that question with their blood.

## The Fight to Fight

The story of the 54th Massachusetts begins not on the battlefield but in a political argument. When the Civil War broke out in 1861, thousands of free Black men in the North tried to enlist. They were turned away. The prevailing belief among many white Americans — including some in the Union — was that Black men lacked the courage and discipline to serve as soldiers. Frederick Douglass, the formerly enslaved abolitionist, argued passionately against this prejudice. "Once let the black man get upon his person the brass letters, U.S.," Douglass wrote, "and there is no power on earth which can deny that he has earned the right to citizenship."

The [Emancipation Proclamation](/articles/emancipation-proclamation/), which took effect on January 1, 1863, changed the calculus. It not only declared enslaved people in Confederate states "forever free" but also authorized the enlistment of Black men into the Union military. Massachusetts Governor John Andrew, a committed abolitionist, moved quickly. He received authorization to raise a regiment of Black volunteers — the 54th Massachusetts Infantry.

Recruiting began in February 1863. Volunteers came from across the North — from Massachusetts, New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and beyond. Two of Frederick Douglass's own sons, Lewis and Charles, were among the first to enlist. The men were paid less than white soldiers, given inferior equipment, and subjected to skepticism from Union commanders who doubted they would hold under fire. None of that stopped them from volunteering.

Governor Andrew chose Robert Gould Shaw to lead the regiment. Shaw came from a wealthy family deeply involved in the abolitionist movement. He was young, idealistic, and acutely aware of the stakes. If the 54th failed, it would set back the cause of Black military service — and perhaps the cause of racial equality itself — for years. If they succeeded, they could change everything.

## The Assault on Fort Wagner

By July 1863, the 54th had been deployed to the South Carolina coast. The Union was attempting to capture Charleston, the city where the war had begun with the firing on [Fort Sumter](/articles/fort-sumter-to-appomattox/). Fort Wagner, a massive sand-and-palmetto-log fortification at the narrow neck of Morris Island, stood in the way. The fort was defended by approximately 1,700 Confederate soldiers behind thick walls, with artillery positioned to sweep the only approach — a narrow strip of beach.

Union General Quincy Adams Gillmore ordered a frontal assault. The 54th Massachusetts volunteered to lead the charge.

At dusk, the regiment advanced. They moved in a column across 1,200 yards of open beach, directly into a storm of cannon fire and musket balls. Men fell in clusters. The sand turned dark. Shaw climbed to the top of the parapet, sword raised, shouting for his men to follow. Moments later, he was shot through the chest and killed.

The men of the 54th kept fighting. Sergeant William Carney, a formerly enslaved man from Norfolk, Virginia, seized the American flag when the color bearer fell. Despite being shot multiple times — in the chest, the right arm, and both legs — Carney carried the flag through the battle and back to Union lines. He reportedly said, "Boys, I only did my duty; the old flag never touched the ground." Carney would become one of the first Black Americans to receive the Medal of Honor.

The assault failed. The 54th suffered approximately 281 casualties out of roughly 600 men engaged — a casualty rate of nearly 47 percent. Shaw and dozens of his men lay dead on and around the walls of Fort Wagner. The Confederates buried Shaw in a mass grave with his Black soldiers, intending it as an insult. Shaw's father responded that he could imagine "no holier place" for his son to rest than "surrounded by his brave and devoted soldiers."

## A Nation Transformed

The military impact of the assault on Fort Wagner was negligible — the fort held for another two months. But the political and moral impact was enormous.

Newspapers across the North published detailed accounts of the 54th's bravery. The New York Tribune wrote that the assault "made Fort Wagner such a name to the colored race as Bunker Hill has been for ninety years to the white Yankees." Skeptics who had claimed Black men would break and run in battle were confronted with evidence of extraordinary courage under fire.

Recruitment of Black soldiers surged. By the end of the war, approximately 180,000 Black men had served in the United States Colored Troops, making up roughly 10 percent of the Union Army. An estimated 40,000 died in service. President Abraham Lincoln later said that the military contribution of Black soldiers was essential to winning the war. "Take from us and give to the enemy the hundred and eighty thousand colored persons now serving us as soldiers, seamen, and laborers," Lincoln wrote, "and we cannot longer maintain the contest."

The men of the 54th also fought for equal treatment within the Union Army. For over a year, the regiment refused to accept reduced pay — Black soldiers were paid $10 per month while white soldiers received $13 — choosing to serve without pay rather than accept unequal compensation. Congress finally equalized military pay in June 1864.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of the 54th Massachusetts is a story about fighting for the right to fight. These men didn't just face Confederate bullets — they faced the prejudice of their own government, their own army, and much of their own society. They responded not with bitterness but with excellence. They proved their doubters wrong not by arguing but by acting.

That's a powerful model for anyone who has been told they don't belong, that they can't do something, or that they aren't good enough. The men of the 54th didn't wait for permission to be brave. They demanded the chance and then delivered beyond what anyone expected.

There's also a lesson about sacrifice for a cause larger than yourself. Shaw gave up a comfortable life. Carney carried the flag despite being shot repeatedly. The entire regiment served without pay rather than accept inequality. These men understood that some principles are worth more than personal comfort — and that standing up for those principles, even at great cost, can change the world.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, debates about representation, equality, and who gets to serve continue in militaries, workplaces, and institutions around the world. The legacy of the 54th Massachusetts echoes whenever someone breaks a barrier — the first woman in a combat unit, the first person of color to lead an organization, the first member of any marginalized group to enter a space that was once closed to them.

The 54th's story also speaks to the ongoing conversation about how nations remember their history. The Shaw Memorial on Boston Common, sculpted by Augustus Saint-Gaudens, took 14 years to complete and is considered one of the finest works of public art in America. It depicts Shaw and his men marching together — officer and enlisted, white and Black — unified in purpose. In a time when communities across America are debating which stories deserve monuments and which deserve removal, the Shaw Memorial offers a model: honor the people who fought to make the country better, not those who fought to keep it divided.

The men of the 54th Massachusetts were told they couldn't be soldiers. They proved they could be heroes. Their story reminds us that courage doesn't come from the color of your skin or the circumstances of your birth. It comes from the choices you make when everything is on the line — and the 54th made theirs on the sands of Morris Island, 163 years ago.
