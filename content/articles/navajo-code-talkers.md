## A Language the Enemy Could Not Learn

By 1942, the United States military had a serious problem. Japanese intelligence officers were breaking American codes almost as fast as they could be created. Encrypted radio messages that took hours to encode and decode were being intercepted and deciphered, sometimes within minutes. In the brutal island-hopping campaign of the Pacific War, where speed of communication could mean the difference between victory and annihilation, the Americans needed a code that could not be broken.

The solution came from an unexpected source. Philip Johnston, a civil engineer living in Los Angeles, had grown up on the Navajo reservation as the son of a missionary. He was one of the very few non-Navajo people in the world who spoke the language fluently, and he understood something that most military planners did not: the Navajo language was extraordinarily complex, had no written form, and was spoken by fewer than 30 people outside the Navajo Nation who were not of Navajo descent. It was, in effect, already a code.

In February 1942, Johnston demonstrated his idea to Major General Clayton Vogel at Camp Elliott, California. He arranged for Navajo volunteers to transmit and translate messages in a test exercise. The results were dramatic. Messages that took a coding machine 30 minutes to encrypt and transmit were handled by the Navajo speakers in 20 seconds — accurately.

The Marine Corps authorized a pilot program immediately.

## Building the Code

The first 29 Navajo recruits — later known as the "Original 29" — arrived at Camp Pendleton, California, in mid-1942. Their task was not simply to speak Navajo over the radio. They had to create an entirely new military code within their language, because Navajo had no words for modern military terms like "tank," "bomber," or "submarine."

The code talkers developed an ingenious system. They assigned Navajo words to approximately 450 military terms. A tank became "chay-da-gahi" (tortoise). A bomber became "jay-sho" (buzzard). A submarine became "besh-lo" (iron fish). A fighter plane was "da-he-tih-hi" (hummingbird). For spelling out proper nouns and other words not in the code vocabulary, they created a Navajo alphabet where each letter was represented by a Navajo word for an animal or object — and they assigned multiple words to the most common letters so that frequency analysis, a standard code-breaking technique, would be useless.

The code was never written down. Every code talker memorized the entire vocabulary. This meant that even if a code talker were captured, there was no codebook for the enemy to seize. The code existed only in the minds of the men who used it.

## Into the Pacific

The first Navajo code talkers were deployed to Guadalcanal in late 1942. Their impact was immediate. Communications that had previously taken 30 minutes or more could now be transmitted in seconds, with perfect accuracy. Commanders quickly realized that the code talkers were not just fast — they were unbreakable. Japanese cryptographers, who had successfully cracked previous American codes, were completely baffled.

As the war progressed, the Marine Corps expanded the program rapidly. Approximately 400 Navajo men ultimately served as code talkers, and they participated in every major Marine assault in the Pacific from 1942 to 1945 — Guadalcanal, Tarawa, Peleliu, Saipan, Guam, and Okinawa.

Their most celebrated contribution came during the Battle of Iwo Jima in February 1945 — the same brutal fight captured in the [iconic flag-raising photograph](/articles/iwo-jima-flag-photo/) on Mount Suribachi. During the first 48 hours of the invasion, six Navajo code talkers worked around the clock, transmitting and receiving over 800 messages without a single error. Major Howard Connor, the signal officer of the 5th Marine Division, later said: "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima."

The code talkers operated under extraordinary danger. They were front-line communicators, carrying radios into combat alongside infantry marines. They were high-value targets — if the Japanese had captured a code talker and forced him to reveal the code, the entire system would have been compromised. Some code talkers were reportedly assigned bodyguards whose orders included preventing their capture at all costs, though the specifics of this practice remain debated by historians.

## The Irony They Carried

The Navajo code talkers fought for a country that had spent generations trying to destroy the very thing that made them invaluable: their language. For decades before the war, the United States government had operated a system of Native American boarding schools whose explicit purpose was to strip Indigenous children of their language and culture. Navajo children were punished — sometimes beaten — for speaking their own language at these schools. The motto of the boarding school system was chillingly direct: "Kill the Indian, save the man."

Now the United States government was asking these same men to use that language to save American lives. The irony was not lost on the code talkers, but most of them served willingly and with pride. They saw their service as a way to honor the Navajo warrior tradition and to prove the value of their culture to a nation that had tried to erase it.

The code talkers' work was a closely guarded secret, both during and after the war. Like the British codebreakers at [Bletchley Park](/articles/enigma-code-bletchley-park/) who cracked the German Enigma machine, the Navajo code talkers were sworn to secrecy. The military classified the code talker program until 1968 — meaning that for 23 years after the war ended, the code talkers could not tell anyone, including their own families, what they had done. Many returned home to the reservation with no recognition, no parades, and no public thanks.

## Recognition, Delayed but Earned

The declassification in 1968 began a long, slow process of recognition. In 2000, Congress passed the Navajo Code Talkers Recognition Act. In 2001, the Original 29 were awarded the Congressional Gold Medal, and all other Navajo code talkers received Congressional Silver Medals. President George W. Bush presented the medals in a ceremony at the U.S. Capitol.

By then, many of the code talkers had already passed away. As of 2026, the last known surviving Navajo code talker of World War II has died, making the preservation of their story a matter of historical record rather than living memory.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The Navajo code talkers demonstrate that the qualities others try to take from you may turn out to be your greatest strengths. The U.S. government tried to eliminate the Navajo language, and then the Navajo language helped win the war. If you have ever been made to feel that something about you — your background, your language, your way of thinking — is a weakness, the code talkers' story is a reminder that those judgments are often wrong.

Their story also teaches the value of quiet service. The code talkers served in silence for decades, knowing that their contributions might never be acknowledged. They served anyway. Not every act of courage comes with immediate recognition.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Indigenous languages around the world continue to face the threat of extinction. UNESCO estimates that a language disappears approximately every two weeks. The Navajo Nation has invested in language preservation programs, but fluency rates among younger Navajo have declined significantly. The code talkers' story is a powerful argument for why linguistic diversity matters — not just culturally, but practically. A language is not just a way of speaking. It is a way of thinking, and the world is poorer every time one disappears.
