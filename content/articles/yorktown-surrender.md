On the afternoon of October 19, 1781, a British drummer boy climbed atop a parapet at Yorktown, Virginia, and began to beat a signal for parley. The sound could barely be heard over the cannon fire. An officer appeared beside him, waving a white handkerchief. The guns fell silent. After weeks of siege, bombardment, and daring nighttime assaults, General Charles Cornwallis was asking to surrender.

The war that had begun six years earlier with shots fired at [Lexington and Concord](/articles/lexington-concord/) was, for all practical purposes, over.

## The Trap

The Yorktown campaign was a masterpiece of strategic coordination between American and French forces. In the summer of 1781, Washington's Continental Army was encamped outside New York City, watching the main British army under General Henry Clinton. Washington wanted to attack New York but lacked the forces to do it.

Then came word that changed everything. The French Admiral Comte de Grasse was sailing from the Caribbean with a fleet of 28 warships and 3,000 troops, heading for the Chesapeake Bay. Meanwhile, Cornwallis had marched his army of roughly 8,000 men into Yorktown, a small tobacco port on the Virginia peninsula, to establish a naval base.

Washington saw the opportunity. If de Grasse could control the Chesapeake and block the British navy from reinforcing or evacuating Cornwallis, the army at Yorktown would be trapped. Washington and the French General Comte de Rochambeau began a 450-mile march south from New York to Virginia — one of the most remarkable feats of the war.

## The March

The march south required deception. Washington left a small force outside New York with fake encampments and bogus dispatches (designed to be intercepted) to convince Clinton that an attack on the city was imminent. By the time Clinton realized the truth, the allied army was too far south to catch.

Washington and Rochambeau's combined force of approximately 17,000 American and French troops reached Yorktown in late September. De Grasse's fleet had already arrived and fought off a British relief squadron at the Battle of the Capes on September 5 — a naval engagement that was arguably the most important in American history. The French controlled the sea. Cornwallis was surrounded.

## The Siege

The allies began formal siege operations on September 28. Over the next three weeks, they dug trenches (parallels) that moved progressively closer to the British fortifications. French and American artillery — including heavy siege guns brought by de Grasse — pounded the British positions day and night.

On the night of October 14, two critical British redoubts (small fortifications) were captured in dramatic nighttime assaults. American forces under Colonel Alexander Hamilton stormed Redoubt No. 10 with bayonets, taking it in less than ten minutes. French troops captured Redoubt No. 9 simultaneously.

With his defenses crumbling, his supplies exhausted, and no relief in sight, Cornwallis attempted a desperate escape across the York River on the night of October 16. A violent storm scattered his boats, and the attempt failed.

## The Surrender

On October 19, the British army marched out of Yorktown to lay down their arms. According to tradition, the British band played "The World Turned Upside Down" — though some historians question whether this actually happened. Cornwallis himself did not attend, claiming illness. He sent his deputy, Brigadier General Charles O'Hara, who attempted to surrender his sword to Rochambeau rather than Washington. Rochambeau directed him to Washington, who in turn directed him to his own deputy, Major General Benjamin Lincoln — a deliberate mirror of Cornwallis's snub.

The surrender at Yorktown did not formally end the war — the Treaty of Paris would not be signed until 1783 — but it ended major combat operations. When news reached London, British Prime Minister Lord North reportedly exclaimed, "Oh God, it is all over!"

## What This Means for You in 2026

Yorktown was won not by one brilliant stroke but by the convergence of preparation, alliance, and timing. Washington had spent years building relationships with the French, maintaining his army through terrible conditions, and waiting for the right moment. When the opportunity came, he was ready to act decisively. Success often looks sudden from the outside but is built on years of groundwork. Keep preparing, keep building your alliances, and when the moment comes, move fast.
