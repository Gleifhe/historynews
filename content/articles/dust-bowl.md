## The Sky Turned Black

On April 14, 1935 — a day that became known as "Black Sunday" — the worst dust storm in American history swept across the Great Plains. A wall of black dirt, thousands of feet high, rolled across Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, and Colorado at 60 miles per hour. Day turned to night. People couldn't see their hands in front of their faces.

It wasn't a one-time event. Throughout the 1930s, massive dust storms — called "black blizzards" — hit the Great Plains over and over again. The topsoil of America's most productive farmland was literally blowing away.

## How Did This Happen?

The Dust Bowl was a man-made disaster. For decades, farmers had plowed up millions of acres of native grassland across the Great Plains to plant wheat. During World War I, wheat prices were high, and farmers plowed even more.

The problem was that the native grass had deep roots that held the soil in place. Without the grass, the topsoil had nothing to anchor it. When a severe drought hit in 1930, the exposed soil dried out and turned to dust.

The wind did the rest.

## The Numbers Were Staggering

- **100 million acres** of farmland were damaged
- **Dust storms** carried dirt all the way to Washington, D.C., and ships in the Atlantic Ocean
- **2.5 million people** left the Great Plains during the 1930s
- **Thousands died** from "dust pneumonia" — a condition caused by inhaling fine dirt particles

## Dorothea Lange and the Camera

Some of the most powerful photographs ever taken came from the Dust Bowl. The Farm Security Administration (FSA) hired photographers like Dorothea Lange, Arthur Rothstein, and Walker Evans to document the crisis.

Their photographs — now in the Library of Congress — show families living in tents, children with dirt-caked faces, and farmhouses half-buried in sand dunes. Lange's famous "Migrant Mother" photograph, showing a worried woman with her children at a pea-pickers' camp in California, became one of the most recognized images in American history.

## The Okies

Hundreds of thousands of families packed everything they owned into old cars and trucks and headed west to California. They were called "Okies" — even though many came from Texas, Kansas, and Arkansas, not just Oklahoma.

California didn't welcome them. Many communities turned migrants away. Police set up blockades at the state border. Workers competed desperately for low-paying farm jobs. John Steinbeck's novel *The Grapes of Wrath* told their story and shocked the nation.

## The Government Responds

President Franklin D. Roosevelt's administration took action:

- The **Soil Conservation Service** was created in 1935 to teach farmers how to prevent erosion
- The government paid farmers to plant trees in "shelterbelts" — rows of trees that blocked the wind
- New farming techniques like contour plowing and crop rotation were promoted
- The **Civilian Conservation Corps** planted over 200 million trees across the Plains

By the early 1940s, rain returned and the worst of the Dust Bowl was over. But the landscape — and the lives of millions of people — had been permanently changed.

## Why It Still Matters

The Dust Bowl remains the worst ecological disaster in American history. It showed what can happen when humans ignore the natural environment in pursuit of short-term profit.

Today, scientists warn that climate change and unsustainable farming practices could create new dust bowls — not just in the United States, but around the world. The photographs in the Library of Congress serve as a powerful reminder of what's at stake.
