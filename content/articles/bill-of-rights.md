On December 15, 1791, Virginia became the eleventh state to ratify ten amendments to the United States Constitution, providing the three-fourths majority needed to make them law. These ten amendments — the Bill of Rights — guaranteed freedoms that the original Constitution had not explicitly protected: speech, religion, press, assembly, the right to bear arms, protection from unreasonable searches, the right to a jury trial, and more.

## The Missing Piece

When the [Constitutional Convention](/articles/constitutional-convention/) finished its work in September 1787, several delegates refused to sign the document because it lacked a bill of rights. George Mason of Virginia — who had written Virginia's Declaration of Rights in 1776 — was the most vocal critic. He warned that without explicit protections, the new federal government could become as tyrannical as the British Crown.

During the ratification debates, the absence of a bill of rights became the Anti-Federalists' strongest argument. States like Massachusetts, Virginia, and New York agreed to ratify only on the condition that a bill of rights would be added promptly.

## Madison's Reluctant Masterpiece

James Madison initially opposed a bill of rights. He argued that the Constitution's structure — with its separation of powers, checks and balances, and limited enumerated powers — was sufficient protection against tyranny. He also worried that listing specific rights might imply that unlisted rights did not exist.

But Madison was a pragmatist. He recognized that a bill of rights was politically necessary to secure support for the new government. He also came to see that explicit protections could serve as a powerful check on majoritarian tyranny — the danger that a democratic majority might trample the rights of minorities.

In June 1789, Madison introduced a set of proposed amendments to the First Congress. He drew from state constitutions, the Magna Carta, the English Bill of Rights of 1689, and hundreds of suggestions submitted by state ratifying conventions. Congress debated, revised, and eventually approved twelve amendments. Ten were ratified by the states.

## The Ten Amendments

The Bill of Rights covers a remarkable range of protections in just 462 words:

The First Amendment protects freedom of religion, speech, press, assembly, and petition. The Second Amendment protects the right to bear arms. The Third Amendment prohibits the forced quartering of soldiers in private homes. The Fourth Amendment guards against unreasonable searches and seizures. The Fifth Amendment protects against self-incrimination and double jeopardy and guarantees due process. The Sixth Amendment ensures the right to a speedy public trial. The Seventh Amendment preserves the right to a jury trial in civil cases. The Eighth Amendment prohibits excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishment. The Ninth Amendment states that listing specific rights does not deny other rights retained by the people. The Tenth Amendment reserves unenumerated powers to the states or the people.

## A Living Document

For its first 130 years, the Bill of Rights applied only to the federal government. States could — and did — restrict speech, establish churches, and conduct unreasonable searches with impunity. It was not until the 14th Amendment (1868) and a series of Supreme Court decisions in the 20th century that the Bill of Rights was "incorporated" against state governments, extending its protections to all Americans.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Bill of Rights exists because people demanded it. The Constitution was a brilliant framework, but its authors initially left out the most important part — explicit protection for individual rights. It took citizens pushing back, refusing to accept "trust us" as an answer, to secure those protections. When something important is missing from a system you are part of — a policy, a contract, a plan — do not assume goodwill will cover the gap. Write it down.
