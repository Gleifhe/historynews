## The War That Spanned a Generation

On October 7, 2001 — less than a month after the [September 11 attacks on the Pentagon](/articles/pentagon-on-911/) and the World Trade Center — the United States launched Operation Enduring Freedom in Afghanistan. The stated mission was to dismantle al-Qaeda, the terrorist organization responsible for the 9/11 attacks, and to remove the Taliban government that had given al-Qaeda sanctuary. American forces, working alongside Afghan allies and coalition partners, toppled the Taliban regime within weeks.

But the war did not end. It continued for nearly 20 years — through four presidential administrations, multiple strategy shifts, and a generation of Americans who grew up knowing nothing but a nation at war. When the last U.S. military aircraft lifted off from Hamid Karzai International Airport in Kabul on August 30, 2021, the War in Afghanistan became the longest war in American history, surpassing the Vietnam War by several years.

## Historical Context: Why Afghanistan

Afghanistan had been a crossroads of conflict for centuries. The Soviet Union had fought a devastating war there from 1979 to 1989, losing approximately 15,000 soldiers before withdrawing in what was widely regarded as a strategic defeat. The power vacuum left by the Soviet withdrawal eventually allowed the Taliban, a fundamentalist Islamic movement, to take control of most of the country by 1996. Under Taliban rule, Afghanistan became a haven for al-Qaeda and its leader, Osama bin Laden, who used the country as a base to plan the September 11 attacks.

The American public overwhelmingly supported military action in the wake of 9/11. A Gallup poll taken in early October 2001 showed 90 percent of Americans approved of military action in Afghanistan. The initial campaign was swift and successful. Working with the Northern Alliance, a coalition of Afghan opposition groups, U.S. Special Forces and airpower drove the Taliban from power in roughly two months. Osama bin Laden fled to the mountains of Tora Bora near the Pakistani border, escaping capture in December 2001.

That escape marked the beginning of what would become a long and grinding counterinsurgency campaign. The Taliban regrouped across the border in Pakistan and began launching attacks back into Afghanistan. What had started as a focused mission against al-Qaeda gradually expanded into a broader effort to build a stable Afghan state — a mission that proved far more difficult and costly than anyone had anticipated.

## The Event: Twenty Years of Sacrifice

The scale of the war is difficult to comprehend without the numbers. Over 20 years, approximately 800,000 Americans served in Afghanistan. Of those, 2,461 U.S. service members were killed, including 1,922 who died as a result of hostile action. More than 20,700 were wounded in action. Among the fallen were service members from every branch — Army, Marines, Navy, Air Force, and Coast Guard — and from every state.

The heaviest years of American casualties were 2010 and 2011, during the troop surge ordered by President Barack Obama. At the peak, approximately 100,000 U.S. troops were deployed in Afghanistan simultaneously. The surge aimed to reverse Taliban momentum and create enough stability for Afghan security forces to take over. American casualties during those two years accounted for roughly a third of the war's total.

The war claimed lives in every conceivable way. Improvised explosive devices — IEDs — were the single deadliest threat, responsible for the majority of American casualties. Ambushes, sniper fire, rocket attacks, vehicle accidents, helicopter crashes, and insider attacks by Afghan forces turning their weapons on their American trainers — every deployment carried risk from multiple directions.

Among those who gave their lives was [Pat Tillman](/articles/pat-tillman-chose-service/), the NFL safety who walked away from a $3.6 million contract to enlist as an Army Ranger after 9/11. Tillman was killed in Afghanistan in April 2004 at age 27. His story became a symbol of both the highest form of service and the painful complexities of the war, after the military initially misrepresented the circumstances of his death.

The war also produced extraordinary acts of valor. Sergeant First Class Jared Monti, Staff Sergeant Robert Miller, Sergeant First Class Leroy Petry, Captain William Swenson, and Sergeant Ryan Pitts were among the service members who earned the Medal of Honor for actions in Afghanistan. Their citations describe acts of courage under fire that echo the traditions of earlier American wars — throwing back grenades, charging enemy positions, and refusing to abandon wounded comrades.

## The Aftermath: Withdrawal and Collapse

By 2020, the United States had entered negotiations with the Taliban, and in February of that year, the two sides signed an agreement in Doha, Qatar, that committed the U.S. to a full withdrawal. The withdrawal was completed under the next administration in August 2021, culminating in a chaotic evacuation from Kabul as the Taliban rapidly retook control of the country.

On August 26, 2021 — just days before the final withdrawal — a suicide bombing at Abbey Gate outside the Kabul airport killed 13 U.S. service members and approximately 170 Afghan civilians. The 13 Americans killed ranged in age from 20 to 31. They were the last American casualties of the war.

The fall of Kabul raised difficult questions. Was the sacrifice worth it? Did the mission succeed? The answers depend on how success is defined. Al-Qaeda's ability to operate from Afghanistan was severely degraded. Osama bin Laden was killed by U.S. Navy SEALs in Pakistan in 2011. Millions of Afghan children, especially girls, received educations that would have been impossible under the Taliban. But the Taliban's return to power in 2021 reversed many of those gains, and the speed of the Afghan government's collapse stunned observers worldwide.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The War in Afghanistan teaches a lesson about endurance — not the triumphant kind depicted in movies, but the quiet, unglamorous endurance of people who kept showing up. For 20 years, men and women deployed to a country most Americans could not find on a map. They did their jobs, took care of the soldiers next to them, and came home — or did not.

The war also teaches humility about the limits of good intentions. The Americans who served in Afghanistan overwhelmingly believed in their mission. Many built schools, trained Afghan soldiers, and formed genuine bonds with the Afghan people. The outcome was not what they hoped for, and that does not diminish their service. Doing the right thing and getting the result you wanted are not always the same thing.

For anyone struggling with a situation that feels futile or a goal that seems unreachable, the veterans of Afghanistan offer a model: you control your effort and your integrity, not the outcome. That is enough.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, five years after the withdrawal, the legacy of the Afghanistan War continues to shape American policy and society. The Department of Veterans Affairs estimates that more than 1.9 million veterans of the post-9/11 wars are eligible for VA health care. The PACT Act of 2022, which expanded health care benefits for veterans exposed to burn pits and other toxic substances, was a direct response to the health challenges facing Afghanistan and Iraq War veterans. Mental health care, traumatic brain injury treatment, and suicide prevention among veterans remain urgent national priorities — a reminder that the costs of war do not end when the last plane leaves.
