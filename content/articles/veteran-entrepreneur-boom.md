## They Didn't Wait for Orders — They Started Companies

Walk into any American city and you'll find them: the veteran who opened a construction firm, the former medic who launched a healthcare staffing company, the retired officer who built a cybersecurity startup. They're everywhere, and there are far more of them than most people realize.

According to the U.S. Small Business Administration, approximately 2.52 million businesses in America are owned by veterans. That's not a rounding error — it's an economic force. Veterans start businesses at roughly twice the rate of their civilian peers, and their businesses tend to be larger, employ more people, and survive longer than average.

This isn't a coincidence. It's a direct consequence of what the military teaches — and what it demands.

## Historical Context: Veterans Have Always Built

The connection between military service and entrepreneurship is as old as the republic. After the Revolutionary War, veterans used their land grants to establish farms, trading posts, and frontier businesses that pushed the American economy westward. After the Civil War, veteran entrepreneurs helped drive the industrial boom of the Gilded Age.

But the modern veteran entrepreneurship surge has a specific origin: the [GI Bill of 1944](/articles/gi-bill-1944/). By sending 8 million World War II veterans to college and providing low-interest home and business loans, the GI Bill didn't just create the American middle class — it created a generation of business owners. Veterans used their education and their loans to open shops, start firms, and build the small-business economy that became America's backbone.

The pattern accelerated after Vietnam, when veterans — often shut out of corporate America by anti-war stigma — started their own businesses out of necessity. Many succeeded spectacularly. FedEx founder Fred Smith, a Marine veteran of Vietnam, famously used his military logistics training to build one of the world's largest companies. He wasn't the exception; he was the most visible example of a widespread phenomenon.

Today's post-9/11 generation is repeating the pattern with new intensity. Programs like the SBA's Boots to Business, the IVMF's Entrepreneurship Bootcamp for Veterans, and private accelerators like Bunker Labs are channeling military talent into startups at unprecedented scale.

## The Core Lesson: Military Training Is Business Training

Why do veterans make such effective entrepreneurs? The answer isn't patriotic sentiment — it's practical skill transfer. The military teaches exactly the skills that entrepreneurship demands, and it teaches them under conditions that civilian business schools can't replicate.

**Risk assessment.** Every military operation involves evaluating threats, weighing options, and making decisions with incomplete information. That's exactly what a startup founder does every day. Veterans don't freeze when facing uncertainty — they've been trained to act decisively with the best information available.

**Team building.** A platoon leader learns to build a cohesive team from diverse individuals, often under extreme stress. That's the same challenge facing every startup founder who needs to hire well, build culture, and keep people motivated when the company is burning cash and the product isn't working yet.

**Execution under pressure.** The military's defining characteristic is performance under pressure. Deadlines in business are stressful; deadlines in combat are lethal. Veterans who've operated under the ultimate pressure find business deadlines challenging but manageable. They don't panic. They execute.

**Mission planning.** Military operations require detailed planning with contingencies for everything that can go wrong. That discipline translates directly into business planning — market analysis, financial modeling, scenario planning, and pivot strategies.

**Comfort with hierarchy and structure.** Veterans understand organizational structure instinctively. They know how to build chains of command, delegate authority, establish standard operating procedures, and scale operations — all critical skills for growing a business beyond the founder.

## Real-World Examples

The numbers tell the story, but individual examples bring it to life.

**USAA**, now a Fortune 500 financial services company with over 13 million members, was founded in 1922 by 25 Army officers who couldn't get auto insurance because insurers considered military officers too high-risk. Those officers pooled their resources, started their own company, and built an institution that now manages over $200 billion in assets.

**GoDaddy** was co-founded by Bob Parsons, a Marine veteran of Vietnam who was wounded in combat and received the Purple Heart. Parsons has spoken extensively about how Marine Corps discipline and resilience shaped his approach to business — particularly his willingness to take risks that more cautious founders wouldn't consider.

**Rethreaded**, a Jacksonville, Florida social enterprise, was founded by Kristin Keen, an Army veteran who creates employment for survivors of human trafficking. Her military logistics background helped her build a manufacturing operation that turns donated T-shirts into new products while providing job training and economic independence.

**Black Rifle Coffee Company**, founded by Green Beret veteran Evan Hafer, grew from a small e-commerce operation into a publicly traded company. Hafer has credited his Special Forces training — particularly the ability to build teams and operate in ambiguous environments — as the foundation of the company's rapid growth.

These aren't isolated stories. The Census Bureau's Annual Business Survey consistently shows that veteran-owned businesses are concentrated in industries that mirror military specialties: construction, logistics, security, healthcare, and technology.

## Personal Growth: The Entrepreneurial Mindset Is Trainable

You don't need military service to think like a veteran entrepreneur. The skills that make veterans successful in business are learnable by anyone willing to adopt the mindset.

**Bias toward action.** Veterans are trained to act, not deliberate indefinitely. In business and in life, imperfect action beats perfect inaction. If you've been sitting on a business idea, a career change, or a personal project, the veteran approach is simple: start. Adjust as you go. Waiting for perfect conditions means waiting forever.

**Embrace calculated risk.** The military doesn't teach recklessness — it teaches *calculated* risk. Assess the threat, identify your resources, plan for contingencies, then move. Every meaningful achievement requires risk. The goal isn't to eliminate risk; it's to understand it well enough to move forward confidently.

**Build your team before you need it.** Veterans know that you can't assemble a team in the middle of a firefight. The same applies to business and life. Build relationships, develop your network, and invest in people before you need their help.

**After-action reviews.** The military's practice of reviewing every operation — what worked, what didn't, what to do differently — is one of the most powerful learning tools ever developed. Apply it to your own life: after every project, meeting, or significant event, ask three questions. What went well? What went wrong? What will I do differently next time?

As the research on [finding purpose after service](/articles/platoon-to-purpose/) shows, entrepreneurship provides veterans with more than income. It provides mission, identity, and the satisfaction of building something that matters.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the veteran entrepreneur boom shows no signs of slowing. The SBA's Boots to Business program has graduated tens of thousands of transitioning service members, and new programs are launching every year. The rise of remote work and e-commerce has lowered barriers to entry, making it easier than ever for veterans to start businesses from anywhere.

But the bigger story is what veteran entrepreneurship reveals about the American economy. Small businesses — the kind veterans disproportionately create — account for nearly half of U.S. private-sector employment. When veterans start businesses, they don't just employ themselves. They employ their neighbors, invest in their communities, and strengthen the economic fabric of their towns and cities.

The approximately 2.52 million veteran-owned businesses in America aren't just an economic statistic. They're proof that the skills the military builds — discipline, leadership, risk management, and the relentless drive to accomplish the mission — are exactly the skills that build a strong economy.

America invests heavily in training its service members. Veteran entrepreneurship is the return on that investment — and the returns keep compounding.
