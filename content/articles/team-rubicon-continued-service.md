## The Earthquake That Changed Everything

On January 12, 2010, a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck Haiti, killing an estimated 220,000 people and leaving 1.5 million homeless. Within hours, images of the devastation flooded news broadcasts worldwide. Governments mobilized. Aid organizations scrambled. And in a living room in Los Angeles, a recently discharged Marine named Jake Wood watched the coverage and thought: *I can help.*

Wood had served two combat tours — one in Iraq, one in Afghanistan. He'd been trained in trauma medicine, logistics, and operating in chaotic environments. He knew how to move fast, triage the wounded, and organize operations where nothing was organized. Sitting on a couch in California while people died in Port-au-Prince felt wrong.

So he did something about it. Wood called William McNulty, a fellow veteran, and within 72 hours they'd assembled a team of eight — veterans and medical professionals — and were on a plane to Haiti. They had no organizational backing, no funding, and no plan beyond showing up and helping. They called themselves Team Rubicon, named after the river Julius Caesar crossed when he committed to an irreversible course of action.

That improvised disaster response team would grow into one of the most remarkable veteran service organizations in American history.

## Historical Context: The Long Tradition of Veterans Serving After Service

Veterans deploying to help in disasters wasn't entirely new. After the 1900 Galveston hurricane, military veterans in Texas organized volunteer crews to help with recovery. The Civilian Conservation Corps of the 1930s, while not exclusively veteran, drew heavily on military organizational models and employed thousands of veterans in conservation and disaster recovery work.

But Team Rubicon represented something fundamentally different: a systematic, scalable model for channeling veteran skills into civilian disaster response. Wood recognized what researchers at Syracuse University's IVMF would later confirm — that veterans don't just need jobs after service. They need [purpose](/articles/platoon-to-purpose/). And disaster response provided exactly the kind of high-stakes, team-based, mission-driven work that veterans are uniquely trained to perform.

The timing was critical. By 2010, hundreds of thousands of post-9/11 veterans were returning from Iraq and Afghanistan. Many were struggling with the transition to civilian life. The [stories of those who served in Afghanistan](/articles/afghanistan-war-fallen/) were still fresh in the nation's consciousness. Veterans were coming home to a country that thanked them for their service but often didn't know how to use their skills. Team Rubicon offered an answer.

## The Core Lesson: Skills Don't Expire at Discharge

The insight at the heart of Team Rubicon is deceptively simple: military skills are exactly what disaster zones need.

When a hurricane flattens a town or a wildfire sweeps through a community, the resulting environment looks a lot like a combat zone. Infrastructure is destroyed. Communication is disrupted. People are injured, displaced, and frightened. Chaos is the default state. And the organizations that respond — local fire departments, FEMA, the Red Cross — are often overwhelmed.

Veterans are trained for exactly this environment. An infantry squad leader knows how to organize a team, establish priorities, and execute under pressure. A combat medic knows how to triage patients when there are more casualties than resources. A military logistics specialist knows how to move supplies into areas where roads are destroyed and communication is unreliable.

Team Rubicon's model harnesses these skills systematically. When a disaster strikes, the organization activates its volunteer network, deploys trained teams to the affected area, and operates alongside — not in competition with — existing emergency management agencies. Veterans conduct damage assessments, clear debris, perform home repairs, distribute supplies, and provide medical support.

The work is physically demanding, logistically complex, and emotionally taxing — which is precisely why veterans excel at it.

## Real-World Examples

The growth of Team Rubicon from eight volunteers in Haiti to over 160,000 by 2025 is a story told in deployments.

**Hurricane Sandy (2012)** was Team Rubicon's breakout moment on American soil. When the superstorm devastated the northeastern United States, Team Rubicon deployed hundreds of volunteers to New York and New Jersey. They performed home assessments, removed debris, and helped families begin recovering — often reaching communities that larger organizations hadn't yet reached. The response demonstrated that veteran volunteers could operate at scale in a major domestic disaster.

**Hurricane Harvey (2017)** pushed the organization further. When catastrophic flooding hit Houston, Team Rubicon deployed over 1,600 volunteers who conducted more than 3,000 work orders — mucking out flooded homes, removing waterlogged drywall, and preventing mold damage that would have made homes permanently uninhabitable. The operation lasted months, not days.

**COVID-19 (2020)** required Team Rubicon to adapt its model for a pandemic. Volunteers shifted to running mobile testing sites, supporting food banks, and assisting overwhelmed hospitals with logistics. The organization demonstrated that its model wasn't limited to natural disasters — it could respond to any large-scale crisis.

**Turkey-Syria Earthquake (2023)** marked one of Team Rubicon's largest international deployments, with teams supporting search and rescue operations in the aftermath of the devastating 7.8-magnitude earthquake that killed over 50,000 people.

Across all these operations, a consistent finding emerged: veterans who deployed with Team Rubicon reported significant improvements in their own well-being. They felt useful. They felt connected. They felt like they had a mission again.

## Personal Growth: Finding Purpose Through Service

Jake Wood has spoken openly about his own post-service struggles. After leaving the Marines, he felt adrift. He had a job, an apartment, and a civilian life — but he didn't have purpose. Haiti changed that. "I didn't start Team Rubicon to help veterans," Wood has said. "I started it to help Haiti. The veteran impact was a discovery."

That discovery — that serving others is the most reliable path to healing yourself — is one of the most well-documented findings in psychology. Research consistently shows that volunteering reduces depression, increases life satisfaction, and improves physical health. For veterans specifically, the effect is amplified because service reconnects them with the identity and purpose that the military provided.

The lesson extends far beyond the veteran community:

**Your skills are needed somewhere.** Whatever you're good at — organizing, building, teaching, listening, fixing — there's a community that needs exactly that skill. The gap between feeling useless and feeling essential is often just one phone call or one volunteer application.

**Action defeats despair.** When Wood felt stuck after leaving the Marines, he didn't solve his problems by thinking about them. He solved them by flying to Haiti. Sometimes the best response to personal difficulty is to stop focusing on yourself and start focusing on someone who has it worse.

**Small starts lead to big things.** Team Rubicon began with eight people and a credit card. Wood didn't wait until he had a perfect plan, a board of directors, or a million dollars in funding. He acted with what he had. The organization scaled because the model worked, but it only worked because someone was willing to start small.

**Community is built through shared hardship.** The strongest bonds in the military are forged under difficult conditions. Team Rubicon replicates this dynamic — volunteers who spend a week mucking out flooded houses together form bonds that last. Purpose and connection aren't things you find; they're things you build by doing hard things alongside good people.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Team Rubicon's model is more relevant than ever. Climate change is increasing the frequency and severity of natural disasters worldwide. FEMA's resources are stretched thin. Communities are looking for new models of disaster response — and veteran-led volunteer organizations have proven they can fill critical gaps.

The organization's growth also points to a broader truth about the post-9/11 veteran generation. This is a generation that volunteered for service during wartime, deployed to some of the most dangerous places on earth, and came home determined to keep serving. They didn't choose the military for a paycheck; they chose it because they wanted to matter. Team Rubicon gives them a way to keep mattering.

Congress has recognized the model's value. The STORM Act (Strengthening and Training Opportunities for Veteran Responders and Managers) was designed to integrate veteran volunteers more formally into the national disaster response framework, acknowledging that veterans represent an undertapped resource for emergency management.

Jake Wood's improvised trip to Haiti in 2010 proved something that should have been obvious all along: the skills America builds in its veterans don't expire when they take off the uniform. Those skills — leadership, resilience, teamwork, and the willingness to run toward danger — are exactly what the world needs. Team Rubicon just gave them a place to use them.
