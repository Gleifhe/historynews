## Five Brothers, One Ship

In the small city of Waterloo, Iowa, the Sullivan family was not wealthy, not prominent, and not particularly different from thousands of other working-class American families in the early 1940s. Thomas and Alleta Sullivan had five sons — George, 27; Francis (Frank), 26; Joseph (Red), 24; Madison (Matt), 23; and Albert, 20. The brothers were close, the kind of tight-knit group that stuck together through everything.

On January 3, 1942 — less than a month after the Japanese attack on [Pearl Harbor](/articles/pearl-harbor-attack/) — the five Sullivan brothers walked into the Navy recruiting office in Waterloo and enlisted together. They had one condition: they wanted to serve on the same ship. The Navy had an informal policy discouraging siblings from serving together on the same vessel, precisely to prevent one family from suffering a catastrophic loss. But the policy was not strictly enforced, and the brothers insisted. The Navy relented.

The decision would haunt the military for generations.

## The USS Juneau

The brothers were assigned to the USS Juneau (CL-52), a light cruiser that had been commissioned in February 1942. The Juneau was fast and well-armed, designed for anti-aircraft defense and surface combat. By the fall of 1942, the ship was operating in the Solomon Islands as part of the fierce naval campaign around Guadalcanal — one of the bloodiest and most important battles of the Pacific War.

The Guadalcanal campaign was a desperate fight. The Japanese and Americans were both pouring ships, planes, and men into the area around the island, trying to control a strategic airstrip. Naval engagements around Guadalcanal were frequent, chaotic, and extraordinarily violent. Ships were sunk in nearly every engagement.

On the night of November 12–13, 1942, the Juneau took part in the Naval Battle of Guadalcanal, one of the most intense surface engagements of the war. In the early morning hours, a Japanese torpedo struck the Juneau on the port side, rupturing one of the ship's fire rooms. The cruiser was badly damaged but still afloat, and the crew worked to keep her operational.

Later that morning, as the damaged Juneau limped away from the battle area in formation with other surviving ships, a Japanese submarine — the I-26 — fired a spread of torpedoes. One struck the Juneau directly in her forward fire room, in approximately the same location as the previous night's hit. The ship's forward ammunition magazine detonated.

The USS Juneau exploded and sank in approximately 20 seconds. The blast was so massive that the captain of the nearby USS Helena reported a mushroom cloud of smoke and debris rising hundreds of feet into the air. The other ships in the formation, fearing further submarine attack, did not stop to search for survivors.

Of the approximately 700 men aboard the Juneau, only 115 made it into the water alive. They floated for eight days before rescue aircraft spotted them. By then, exposure, dehydration, injuries, and shark attacks had killed most of the survivors. Only 10 men were ultimately rescued.

All five Sullivan brothers died. George Sullivan is believed to have survived the initial sinking and spent several days in the water before dying. The exact circumstances of each brother's death remain unknown.

## A Mother Gets the News

The news reached the Sullivan family in stages. On January 12, 1943 — two months after the sinking — three Navy officers and a doctor arrived at the Sullivan home in Waterloo. Thomas Sullivan met them at the door.

"I have some news for you about your boys," one of the officers said.

"Which one?" Thomas asked.

"I'm sorry. All five."

Alleta Sullivan, the boys' mother, later wrote to the Bureau of Naval Personnel, asking for information about her sons' deaths. She received condolence letters from President Franklin Roosevelt and numerous military officials. The family's loss became one of the most widely publicized tragedies of the war.

The Sullivans became a symbol of sacrifice on the home front. Alleta and Thomas Sullivan traveled the country promoting war bonds, and the Navy named two ships in honor of the brothers — the destroyer USS The Sullivans (DD-537), commissioned in 1943, and a second USS The Sullivans (DDG-68), commissioned in 1997. The first was one of the few Navy ships named with the article "The" in its name.

## The Sole Survivor Policy

The loss of the Sullivan brothers was the catalyst for a fundamental change in U.S. military policy. While the War Department had already discouraged siblings from serving together, the Sullivan tragedy demonstrated that informal guidelines were not enough. The military formalized what became known as the Sole Survivor Policy, which was later codified in Department of Defense Directive 1315.15.

The policy provides that a service member may be eligible for separation or reassignment if they are the last surviving child in a family where other siblings have died or been captured in military service. The policy does not absolutely prohibit siblings from serving together, but it creates a framework for protecting families from total loss.

The Sole Survivor Policy remains in effect in 2026. It was famously dramatized in the 1998 film *Saving Private Ryan*, though the film's plot is fictional and draws on several historical cases beyond the Sullivans, including the Niland brothers.

## The Broader Cost

The Sullivan brothers' story is one of the most heart-wrenching in American military history, but they were not the only family to lose multiple sons. The Borgstrom family of Thatcher, Utah, lost four sons between 1944 and 1945. The Niland family of Tonawanda, New York, believed they had lost three of four brothers on or near D-Day, though one was later found alive in a Japanese prison camp.

These cases, and [firsthand accounts from D-Day](/articles/d-day-oral-histories/), reveal the staggering human cost of a war that touched nearly every family in America. By the end of World War II, more than 400,000 American service members had been killed.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The Sullivan brothers' story is, at its core, about the bond between family members and the unimaginable cost of loss. It is also about the consequences of well-intentioned decisions. The brothers' insistence on serving together came from a place of love and loyalty — they did not want to be separated. But that decision placed the entire family's future on a single ship.

There is a lesson in this about risk. Loyalty and togetherness are virtues, but spreading risk is wisdom. In your own life — whether you are investing, making career decisions, or planning for the future — it is worth asking whether you have concentrated too much in one place.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Sole Survivor Policy continues to shape how the U.S. military manages the service of family members. The policy reflects a hard-won understanding that the cost of war is not borne only by those who fight — it is borne by the families who wait at home. The Sullivan brothers' sacrifice reminds us that behind every casualty statistic is a family, a hometown, and a community that will never be the same.
