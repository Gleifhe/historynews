On March 31, 1917 — five days before the United States entered World War I — the American flag was raised over the Danish West Indies for the first time. The United States had paid Denmark $25 million for the three islands of St. Thomas, St. Croix, and St. John, motivated by a single strategic concern: if the United States did not buy them, Imperial Germany might seize them. The islands guarded the eastern approach to the Caribbean and the Panama Canal. That strategic logic would prove even more relevant in the next world war.

## The Purchase

The idea of buying the Danish islands was not new. The United States had tried to acquire them in 1867 and again in 1902, but political opposition in either Washington or Copenhagen had blocked both attempts. The threat of German submarine warfare in the Caribbean during World War I finally created enough urgency.

The islanders were not consulted. The population — predominantly descendants of enslaved Africans brought to work the islands' sugar plantations under Danish colonial rule — had no voice in the transfer of sovereignty. They went from being Danish subjects to being American nationals overnight.

## Naval Rule

From 1917 to 1931, the U.S. Navy governed the islands under a military administration. The naval governor had near-absolute authority. Residents were made U.S. citizens in 1927 but had no vote in federal elections and no meaningful self-government. The Organic Act of 1936 provided a limited civil government, but the governor was still appointed by the president.

## The War

During World War II, the Virgin Islands' strategic value was realized. German U-boats prowled the Caribbean in 1942 and 1943, sinking hundreds of Allied merchant ships carrying oil and supplies. The islands hosted naval facilities that supported anti-submarine patrols and convoy escort operations.

Virgin Islanders served in the military — though, like Black Americans on the mainland, they served in segregated units and faced discrimination. The islands' economy, already struggling, was further strained by wartime rationing and the disruption of trade.

The war brought increased military construction, employment, and federal spending to the islands. But it also reinforced the pattern that had defined the Virgin Islands' experience as a U.S. territory: the islands' value to the United States was strategic, not civic. They were positioned, fortified, and used. Their residents' rights were secondary.

## After the War

The postwar period brought gradual political reform. The first Black governor of the Virgin Islands, William Hastie, was appointed in 1946. The Revised Organic Act of 1954 created a more robust local government. In 1970, Virgin Islanders gained the right to elect their own governor.

But the fundamental status has not changed. As of 2026, the U.S. Virgin Islands remain an unincorporated territory. Their residents are U.S. citizens who cannot vote for president. They send a non-voting delegate to Congress. Like Puerto Rico and Guam, the islands exist in a political gray zone — American enough to be governed, but not American enough to have a full voice.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Virgin Islands remind us that strategic value is not the same as equal partnership. The United States bought these islands for their geography, not for their people. When you value someone primarily for what they can do for you — rather than for who they are — the relationship will always be unequal. Genuine partnership requires valuing the other party as an end in themselves, not merely as a means to your own goals.
