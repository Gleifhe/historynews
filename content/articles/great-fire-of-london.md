## A Fire That Started in a Bakery

In the early hours of Sunday, September 2, 1666, a fire broke out in Thomas Farriner's bakery on Pudding Lane in London. Fires were common in 17th-century London — the city was packed with wooden buildings, and open flames were used for cooking and heating. Nobody panicked at first.

They should have.

The summer of 1666 had been unusually hot and dry. London's tightly packed wooden buildings were like kindling. A strong east wind pushed the flames from building to building faster than anyone could react.

By the time Londoners realized this fire was different, it was already out of control.

## Samuel Pepys: Eyewitness

One of the most valuable records of the Great Fire comes from Samuel Pepys (pronounced "Peeps"), a government official who kept a famous diary. His entries during the fire provide a vivid, almost cinematic account.

On September 2, Pepys wrote:

> "Everybody endeavouring to remove their goods, and flinging into the river or bringing them into lighters that lay off; poor people staying in their houses as long as till the very fire touched them, and then running into boats, or clambering from one pair of stairs by the waterside to another."

Pepys watched from across the Thames as the fire consumed block after block. He rushed to warn the King, Charles II, who ordered buildings to be demolished to create firebreaks. But the Lord Mayor resisted: "Lord, what can I do? I am spent: people will not obey me."

## Four Days of Destruction

The fire burned from Sunday, September 2, through Wednesday, September 5. The numbers are staggering:

- **13,200 houses destroyed** — about 80% of the city's housing
- **84 churches burned**, including the original St. Paul's Cathedral
- **44 livery halls** (guildhalls for trades and professions) were destroyed
- **About 100,000 people** were made homeless — roughly one-sixth of London's population

Remarkably, the official death toll was very low — only about 4 to 6 people were recorded as dying. Historians believe the real number was higher, especially among the poor whose deaths went unrecorded.

## Why Couldn't They Stop It?

London in 1666 had no organized fire department. There were no fire engines in the modern sense — just hand-operated pumps that could barely produce a trickle of water.

The main firefighting technique was pulling down buildings in the path of the fire to create gaps — firebreaks — that the flames couldn't cross. But this required demolishing people's homes and businesses, and many property owners refused.

By the time the authorities began demolishing buildings aggressively on the King's orders, the fire had grown too large. Only when the wind died down on Wednesday evening did the fire finally burn itself out.

## Rebuilding a Better City

Here's where the story of the Great Fire becomes inspiring. London didn't just rebuild — it rebuilt better.

**New building rules** required:
- Buildings to be made of brick or stone instead of wood
- Streets to be wider to slow the spread of fire
- Roofs to be tiled, not thatched

**Christopher Wren**, one of England's greatest architects, was given the job of rebuilding London's churches. He designed 51 new churches, including the magnificent new St. Paul's Cathedral, which still stands today as one of London's most iconic landmarks.

**Fire insurance** was invented. Nicholas Barbon created the first fire insurance company in 1681. Before the Great Fire, insurance for buildings simply didn't exist.

**London's first fire brigades** were formed. Insurance companies hired teams of firefighters to protect properties they insured — the beginning of organized fire service in England.

## Why This Story Still Matters

The Great Fire of London is a story about how disaster can lead to progress. The medieval city of London — cramped, filthy, and dangerously flammable — was replaced by a more modern, safer, and more beautiful city.

The building codes developed after the Great Fire influenced construction standards around the world. The concept of fire insurance spread to other countries. And the idea that cities should plan for fire safety — rather than just react to fires — became a fundamental principle of urban planning.

BBC History's coverage of the Great Fire, combined with Pepys's diary (available free online) and images from the Library of Congress, gives us a complete picture of one of the most transformative disasters in history.
