Before the internet, if you wanted to look something up, you needed an encyclopedia. These were expensive sets of books — sometimes 20 or 30 volumes — that only some families could afford. Libraries had them, but you had to travel there. The information was written by experts, edited by publishers, and updated only when a new edition came out, sometimes years later.

Then, on January 15, 2001, two men changed everything. Jimmy Wales, an internet entrepreneur, and Larry Sanger, a philosopher, launched a website called Wikipedia. Their idea was radical: create a free encyclopedia that anyone in the world could read, edit, and improve. No subscription. No paywall. No permission needed.

Many people thought it was a terrible idea. How could you trust an encyclopedia written by random strangers? Wouldn't it be full of errors and nonsense? The critics had a point — at first. But what happened over the following years proved that the wisdom of crowds, combined with good systems and dedicated volunteers, could produce something remarkable.

## How Wikipedia Works

Wikipedia runs on a simple but powerful model. Anyone can create an account and start writing or editing articles. Every edit is recorded, and other editors can review, correct, or reverse changes. Articles must cite reliable sources. There are rules about neutrality, verifiability, and notability.

Behind the scenes, a vast community of volunteers keeps everything running. As of 2026, Wikipedia has more than 300,000 active editors — people who contribute regularly without any pay. They write articles, fix errors, fight vandalism, and debate what should and should not be included. It is one of the largest volunteer efforts in human history.

The Wikimedia Foundation, a nonprofit organization based in San Francisco, manages the servers and technology. It runs almost entirely on donations from readers. There are no ads on Wikipedia. In a world where most websites are designed to grab your attention and sell you something, Wikipedia remains focused on one goal: sharing knowledge freely.

## Growth and Impact

The growth of Wikipedia has been staggering. The English-language edition alone has more than 6.8 million articles. But Wikipedia exists in over 300 languages, from Afrikaans to Zulu. The total number of articles across all languages exceeds 60 million. It is by far the largest encyclopedia ever created — hundreds of times bigger than the famous Encyclopedia Britannica.

Wikipedia is one of the most visited websites in the world, with billions of page views every month. Students use it for homework. Journalists use it for background research. Doctors have been found consulting it for medical information. It is often the first result when you search for almost anything on Google.

The site has also become an important tool for preserving knowledge. Volunteers have written articles about small towns, endangered languages, local history, and obscure scientific topics that no traditional encyclopedia would ever cover. In this way, Wikipedia captures the full breadth of human knowledge — not just the parts that publishers think will sell books.

## Criticisms and Challenges

Wikipedia is not perfect, and it has never claimed to be. Studies have found that its accuracy on scientific topics is comparable to traditional encyclopedias, but errors do exist. Some articles are biased. Coverage of topics related to women, minorities, and the developing world has historically been weaker because the editor community has been predominantly male and Western.

The site also faces ongoing battles with vandalism and misinformation. Some people deliberately insert false information, and bots and volunteer editors must constantly patrol for these changes. In politically sensitive topics, edit wars can break out as people with opposing views fight over the wording of articles.

Despite these challenges, Wikipedia has developed systems to address most of these problems. Featured articles go through rigorous review. Controversial topics are often locked so that only experienced editors can make changes. And the community continues to work on closing the gaps in coverage.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Wikipedia's story teaches us that ordinary people can do extraordinary things when they work together. No single person could write 60 million articles. But millions of people, each contributing a little bit of their knowledge and time, built something that rivals the greatest libraries in history.

This is a powerful lesson for anyone who feels too small to make a difference. You do not need to be an expert to contribute something valuable. You just need to share what you know, be willing to learn, and respect the contributions of others.

Wikipedia also teaches us about the value of free knowledge. In a world where so much is behind paywalls and locked away, Wikipedia stands as proof that information can be shared freely and still be reliable. When knowledge is open, everyone benefits — especially people who cannot afford expensive textbooks or subscriptions.

## How This Connects to 2026

Wikipedia was made possible by the [invention of the World Wide Web](/articles/invention-of-world-wide-web/) in 1989. Tim Berners-Lee's vision of an open, connected information system became the foundation for Wikipedia's model of shared knowledge. The [role of the internet during 9/11](/articles/internet-on-911/) also showed how digital platforms became essential tools for sharing information during times of crisis — a role Wikipedia continues to play today.

In 2026, Wikipedia faces new challenges from artificial intelligence. AI tools can generate text quickly, raising questions about the future of human-written reference material. But Wikipedia's strength has never been just about the text — it is about the community of people who care about getting things right. That human commitment to accuracy, neutrality, and openness is something that no algorithm can replace.

As misinformation spreads faster than ever, Wikipedia's model of transparent, source-based, community-reviewed knowledge is more important than it has ever been. Twenty-five years after its launch, Wikipedia remains one of the internet's greatest gifts to humanity.
