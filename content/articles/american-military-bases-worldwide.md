When World War II ended in 1945, the United States found itself in possession of something no nation in history had ever had: a global network of military bases spanning every continent and every ocean. During the war, the United States had built or acquired over 30,000 installations worldwide. Most were closed after the war, but hundreds remained — and many are still operational in 2026.

## How It Happened

The base network grew in stages. Before World War II, the United States maintained significant overseas bases only in the Philippines, Panama, Hawaii, and a handful of other territories. The war changed everything.

The Destroyers-for-Bases Agreement of 1940 gave the United States 99-year leases on naval and air bases in Bermuda, Newfoundland, the Bahamas, Jamaica, Antigua, Saint Lucia, Trinidad, and British Guiana — in exchange for 50 aging destroyers transferred to Britain. The deal, negotiated before the United States entered the war, established the template: military access in exchange for security guarantees.

During the war itself, the United States built bases across North Africa, Western Europe, the Pacific Islands, and Asia. After victory, the question was whether to bring the troops home and close the bases — as the United States had done after World War I — or maintain a permanent forward presence.

## The Decision to Stay

The Cold War answered the question. The Soviet Union's consolidation of control over Eastern Europe, the Communist victory in China in 1949, and the Korean War in 1950 convinced American policymakers that the United States needed to be permanently positioned around the world to contain Soviet expansion.

The result was a constellation of alliances and basing agreements: NATO in Europe (1949), the U.S.-Japan Security Treaty (1951), ANZUS with Australia and New Zealand (1951), bilateral agreements with South Korea, the Philippines, Thailand, and dozens of other nations. Each alliance came with military bases.

## The Numbers

As of 2026, the Department of Defense maintains approximately 750 military installations in roughly 80 countries and territories. The largest concentrations are in Germany (roughly 35,000 troops), Japan (roughly 54,000), South Korea (roughly 28,000), and Italy (roughly 12,000). But American military personnel are also stationed in Djibouti, Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait, Australia, Singapore, Honduras, and dozens of other nations.

The combined overseas base network covers an estimated 800,000 acres of foreign soil — an area larger than the state of Rhode Island. Annual spending on overseas bases is estimated at $50 to $100 billion, though exact figures are difficult to determine because costs are spread across multiple budget categories.

## Benefits and Costs

Proponents argue that forward-deployed forces deter aggression, reassure allies, enable rapid crisis response, and maintain the international order that has prevented great-power war since 1945. The base network allows the United States to project military power anywhere in the world within hours.

Critics argue that the bases breed resentment, provoke adversaries, entangle the United States in conflicts that do not serve its interests, and impose significant costs on host communities — including crime, environmental contamination, noise pollution, and the displacement of local populations. The bases also represent a form of sovereignty compromise for host nations, which must accommodate a foreign military presence on their territory.

## Empire by Another Name?

The United States does not call its base network an empire. It describes the arrangement as a system of alliances and partnerships. But scholars have noted that the practical effect — the ability to project military power globally, the economic leverage that comes with security guarantees, the political influence that accompanies a permanent military presence — is functionally imperial.

The distinction matters politically but less so strategically. The United States maintains military capabilities in more countries than any empire in history, including the British Empire at its peak. Whether that constitutes an empire depends on your definition — but the scale is unprecedented.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The base network reminds us that infrastructure determines capability. The United States did not become a global power by wanting to be one — it became one by building the physical infrastructure (bases, ports, airfields, logistics networks) that made global reach possible. In your own life and career, infrastructure matters more than intention. Build the systems, habits, and relationships that make your goals achievable, and capability will follow.
