Here is a fact that might surprise you: the world has been getting better. Not in every way, and not for every person, but in one of the most important measures of human well-being — extreme poverty — the progress over the past few decades has been nothing short of extraordinary.

In 1990, about 1.9 billion people lived in extreme poverty. That means they survived on less than $1.90 per day (adjusted for what money can actually buy in different countries). They struggled to afford food, clean water, shelter, and basic medicine. About 36 out of every 100 people on Earth lived this way.

By 2015, that number had fallen to fewer than 740 million people — under 10% of the world's population. More than 1.1 billion people had been lifted out of the worst poverty. Every day during that 25-year period, an average of about 130,000 people escaped extreme poverty. It was the largest and fastest reduction in human suffering in recorded history.

## How Did This Happen?

There is no single answer. The decline in poverty came from many factors working together.

**Economic growth** played the biggest role. Countries in Asia, Africa, and Latin America grew their economies through trade, investment, and industrialization. When economies grow, jobs are created, wages rise, and families can afford more.

**China's transformation** was the largest single factor. In 1990, about 750 million Chinese people lived in extreme poverty — more than the entire population of Europe at the time. Through economic reforms, investment in infrastructure, and opening up to global trade, China lifted hundreds of millions of its citizens out of poverty in just two decades. By 2015, China's extreme poverty rate had dropped from about 66% to under 1%.

**India** also made major progress. Economic reforms in the 1990s boosted growth, and government programs targeting rural poverty helped millions of families. Between 2005 and 2015 alone, India lifted more than 270 million people out of extreme poverty.

**International cooperation** set clear goals and focused attention. In the year 2000, world leaders gathered at the United Nations and agreed on eight Millennium Development Goals (MDGs). The very first goal was to cut extreme poverty in half by 2015. This target was met five years ahead of schedule, in 2010.

**Aid and investment** from wealthier nations helped build schools, clinics, roads, and water systems in the poorest countries. Programs like vaccines, mosquito nets, and agricultural improvements saved lives and gave families a foundation to build on.

## What Changed for Families

Behind the statistics are real human stories. A farmer in Bangladesh who gained access to better seeds and a small loan could grow more food and send her children to school. A factory worker in Vietnam earned enough to move his family from a tin shack to a concrete house with electricity. A mother in Ethiopia could walk to a clinic instead of watching her child die from a treatable disease.

Education expanded dramatically during this period. The number of children out of school dropped by nearly half. More girls than ever before attended primary school. When children are educated, they earn more as adults, have healthier families, and contribute more to their communities. Education became one of the strongest tools against poverty.

Health improvements also played a major role. Childhood deaths from preventable diseases fell sharply. Access to clean water and sanitation improved. When people are healthier, they can work more, earn more, and invest in their futures.

## The Work Is Not Done

Despite this incredible progress, extreme poverty has not been eliminated. In 2026, an estimated 700 million people still live on less than $2.15 per day (the updated poverty line). Most of them are in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Conflict, climate change, and the economic damage from the COVID-19 pandemic have slowed progress in recent years.

Inequality has also grown. While billions of people escaped the worst poverty, the gap between the richest and poorest has widened in many countries. Having enough to survive is not the same as having a fair chance to thrive.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The story of global poverty reduction teaches us that big problems can be solved — but only with sustained effort over time. There was no single magic solution. Progress came from millions of small actions: a teacher showing up every day, a doctor vaccinating children, a government building a road, a family planting better crops.

This is how most meaningful change happens in our own lives too. We rarely solve our biggest challenges overnight. We solve them through consistent, daily effort. Each small step adds up.

The poverty data also teaches us to pay attention to good news. The world is full of real problems, but if we only focus on what is going wrong, we lose sight of what is going right — and we lose the motivation to keep trying. Progress is possible. The numbers prove it.

## How This Connects to 2026

The fight against poverty continues today, guided by the UN Sustainable Development Goals, which replaced the Millennium Development Goals in 2015. The [United Nations](/articles/founding-of-united-nations/) remains at the center of global efforts to coordinate aid, set targets, and hold countries accountable.

Health breakthroughs like the [eradication of smallpox](/articles/smallpox-eradication/) showed that global cooperation can defeat even the most stubborn threats. The same spirit drives efforts in 2026 to expand vaccines, improve nutrition, and build resilience against climate shocks.

The story of 1.1 billion people escaping extreme poverty is proof that humanity can make the world better when it chooses to act. The question for 2026 is not whether progress is possible — it is whether we will keep choosing to pursue it.
