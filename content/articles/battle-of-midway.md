## Six Months After Pearl Harbor

On the morning of December 7, 1941, the Japanese attack on [Pearl Harbor](/articles/pearl-harbor-attack/) had destroyed or damaged eight American battleships and killed over 2,400 Americans. In the six months that followed, Japan had swept across the Pacific with breathtaking speed — conquering the Philippines, Malaya, the Dutch East Indies, Burma, and dozens of island chains. The Japanese Empire now stretched from the borders of India to the mid-Pacific, and its military appeared unstoppable.

But the Japanese high command knew something that most Americans did not: the attack on Pearl Harbor had missed its most important targets. The three American aircraft carriers of the Pacific Fleet — the Enterprise, the Lexington, and the Saratoga — had not been in port that Sunday morning. They were still afloat, and as long as they were, the Pacific remained contested.

Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, the architect of the Pearl Harbor attack, understood that Japan had a narrow window of advantage. American industrial capacity would eventually overwhelm Japan's, so Yamamoto needed a decisive battle that would destroy the remaining American carriers and force a negotiated peace. He chose Midway Atoll — a tiny pair of islands roughly 1,300 miles northwest of Hawaii — as the place to force that battle.

## The American Advantage: Intelligence

What Yamamoto did not know was that the Americans could read his mail. Navy cryptanalysts at Station Hypo in Pearl Harbor, led by Commander Joseph Rochefort, had partially broken the Japanese Navy's JN-25 code. By mid-May 1942, Rochefort's team had determined that the Japanese were planning a major operation against a target designated "AF."

Rochefort believed AF was Midway. To confirm it, he arranged for the garrison at Midway to send an uncoded radio message reporting that the island's water distillation plant had broken down. Within days, the Americans intercepted a Japanese message reporting that "AF" was short on fresh water. The confirmation was definitive.

Admiral Chester Nimitz, commander of the Pacific Fleet, now had something invaluable: knowledge of where and approximately when the Japanese would attack. He ordered every available carrier to Midway. The Enterprise and Hornet, under Rear Admiral Raymond Spruance, and the Yorktown, under Rear Admiral Frank Jack Fletcher, would be waiting.

The Yorktown had been badly damaged at the Battle of the Coral Sea in May and was estimated to need three months in drydock. Pearl Harbor's repair crews did it in 48 hours. The ship sailed for Midway with welders still working below decks.

## The Battle: June 4, 1942

The Japanese fleet that approached Midway was enormous — four fleet carriers (Akagi, Kaga, Soryu, and Hiryu), two battleships, three cruisers, and a host of destroyers and support ships, carrying over 250 aircraft. Vice Admiral Chuichi Nagumo commanded the carrier strike force — the same force that had attacked Pearl Harbor.

At dawn on June 4, Nagumo launched his first strike against Midway itself, sending 108 aircraft to bomb the island's defenses. Meanwhile, American search planes located the Japanese carriers.

What followed was one of the most dramatic sequences in naval history.

The first American attacks were catastrophic failures. Torpedo bombers from the three American carriers — flying low, slow, and without fighter escort — attacked the Japanese carriers in three separate waves. The results were devastating, but not for the Japanese. Torpedo Squadron 8, from the Hornet, lost all 15 of its aircraft. Only one pilot, Ensign George Gay, survived, floating in the ocean and watching the rest of the battle from the water. Torpedo squadrons from the Enterprise and Yorktown suffered similarly appalling losses.

Of the 41 torpedo bombers that attacked, only six returned. They did not score a single hit.

But their sacrifice was not meaningless. The repeated torpedo attacks forced the Japanese carriers to maneuver violently, preventing them from launching their own aircraft. More critically, the attacks pulled the Japanese fighter cover — the deadly Zeros — down to sea level to intercept the torpedo planes.

When the dive bombers arrived, the sky above the Japanese carriers was undefended.

## Five Minutes That Changed the War

At approximately 10:22 a.m. on June 4, 1942, dive bombers from the Enterprise and Yorktown — 37 SBD Dauntless aircraft led by Lieutenant Commander Wade McClusky and Lieutenant Commander Maxwell Leslie — dove on the Japanese carriers from 14,000 feet.

The Japanese flight decks were crowded with aircraft being refueled and rearmed. Aviation fuel hoses snaked across the decks. Bombs and torpedoes sat stacked in the open. The carriers were at their most vulnerable.

In approximately five minutes, three of the four Japanese carriers — the Akagi, Kaga, and Soryu — were transformed into floating infernos. Bombs ripped through their flight decks and detonated the ordnance and fuel below. The fires were uncontrollable. All three carriers would sink within hours.

The fourth carrier, the Hiryu, survived the initial attack and launched two counterstrikes against the Yorktown, hitting the American carrier with bombs and torpedoes. The Yorktown was eventually abandoned and sank on June 7. But American aircraft from the Enterprise located the Hiryu later that afternoon and hit it with four bombs. The Hiryu burned through the night and was scuttled the next morning.

In the span of a single day, four Japanese fleet carriers — the core of Japan's naval aviation power — were destroyed. Japan also lost approximately 3,000 men and over 200 aircraft, including many of its most experienced pilots.

The United States lost the Yorktown, the destroyer Hammann, approximately 145 aircraft, and 307 men.

## Why Midway Mattered

The Battle of Midway did not end the war — three more years of brutal fighting remained, including the campaigns that would rely on the [Navajo code talkers](/articles/navajo-code-talkers/) for critical communications across the Pacific. But Midway fundamentally shifted the balance of power. Before Midway, Japan held the strategic initiative. After Midway, Japan was on the defensive for the rest of the war.

Japan could not replace its losses. The four carriers sunk at Midway represented the core of the striking force that had dominated the Pacific since Pearl Harbor. American shipyards, by contrast, were already building the fleet of Essex-class carriers that would overwhelm Japan by 1944. Midway was the moment when industrial capacity began to tell.

The battle also demonstrated the ascendance of the aircraft carrier over the battleship as the decisive weapon of naval warfare. Not a single surface ship fired at an enemy vessel during the entire battle. The war was fought and won by aircraft operating from flight decks miles apart.

## The Cost of Courage

The American victory at Midway was not guaranteed. It depended on intelligence, luck, and the extraordinary courage of men like the torpedo bomber crews who knew they were flying into near-certain death. The pilots of Torpedo Squadron 8 knew their aircraft were slow, knew they had no fighter cover, and knew the odds were against them. They attacked anyway.

Ensign Gay, the sole survivor, later recalled that his squadron leader, Lieutenant Commander John Waldron, told his men before launch: "If there is only one plane left to make a final run-in, I want that man to go in and get a hit."

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

Midway teaches that preparation and knowledge can overcome even enormous disadvantages. The American fleet was outnumbered and outgunned, but the intelligence work of Rochefort's codebreakers gave Nimitz the information he needed to position his forces perfectly. In your own life, preparation — studying the problem, understanding the terrain, knowing what you are up against — is often more valuable than raw strength.

The battle also shows that individual sacrifice can change the course of events in ways the individual may never see. The torpedo bomber crews did not know that their attacks were drawing the Japanese fighters away from the dive bombers above. They could not know that their deaths would create the opening for victory.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the Pacific remains one of the most strategically significant regions on Earth. The U.S. Navy continues to operate from bases and alliances forged during and after World War II, and the lessons of Midway — the importance of intelligence, the vulnerability of concentrated forces, and the decisive role of naval aviation — remain central to military strategy. The battle is studied at every naval academy in the world as a case study in how intelligence, courage, and timing can overcome material disadvantage.
