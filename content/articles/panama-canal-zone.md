On November 18, 1903, just fifteen days after Panama declared independence from Colombia — an independence movement actively supported by the United States — Secretary of State John Hay and Philippe-Jean Bunau-Varilla (a French engineer acting as Panama's representative, despite not being Panamanian) signed a treaty granting the United States control of a ten-mile-wide strip of land across the Isthmus of Panama "in perpetuity."

The Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty gave the United States rights over the Canal Zone "as if it were the sovereign" — a phrase that effectively made the Zone American territory in all but name. Panama received $10 million and an annual payment of $250,000. No Panamanian official was present at the signing.

## Building the Canal

The [construction of the Panama Canal](/articles/construction-of-panama-canal/) was one of the great engineering feats in history. Between 1904 and 1914, the United States carved a 50-mile waterway through mountains, jungle, and swamp, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Over 75,000 workers — many recruited from the Caribbean, particularly Barbados and Jamaica — labored in brutal conditions. An estimated 5,600 workers died from disease and accidents during the American construction period (over 20,000 had died during the earlier French attempt).

The canal opened on August 15, 1914, and immediately transformed global shipping. A voyage from New York to San Francisco that had required a 13,000-mile journey around South America was reduced to 5,000 miles.

## The Zone

The Canal Zone was, in effect, a small American colony embedded in the heart of another country. It had its own government, police force, court system, schools, hospitals, and post offices. American "Zonians" — the Canal Zone's permanent residents — lived in manicured communities with subsidized housing, commissaries, and social clubs. They lived an American suburban life in the tropics, separated from Panamanian society by fences, checkpoints, and a vast economic divide.

Panamanians who worked in the Zone were paid less than American employees for the same work — a dual wage system known as the "gold roll" (for Americans) and "silver roll" (for Panamanians and West Indians). The racial and economic hierarchy was explicit and unapologetic.

## The Military Dimension

The Canal Zone's military significance was enormous. During World War II, the United States maintained 67,000 troops in and around the Zone. Fourteen military installations — including Fort Clayton, Fort Sherman, Albrook Air Force Base, and Howard Air Force Base — defended the canal and served as staging areas for operations across Latin America.

The U.S. Army School of the Americas, based in the Zone from 1946 to 1984, trained Latin American military officers in counterinsurgency, intelligence, and military operations. Many graduates later became dictators or were associated with human rights abuses in their home countries, earning the institution the nickname "School of Assassins."

## The Handover

Panamanian resentment of American control of the Zone erupted periodically, most dramatically in the Flag Riots of January 1964, when Panamanian students attempted to raise their flag alongside the American flag in the Zone. American Zonians resisted. In the violence that followed, 22 Panamanians and 4 American soldiers were killed. Panama severed diplomatic relations with the United States.

The crisis led to negotiations that culminated in the Torrijos-Carter Treaties of 1977, signed by Panamanian leader Omar Torrijos and President Jimmy Carter. The treaties provided for the gradual transfer of the canal and the Zone to Panama, with full Panamanian control taking effect on December 31, 1999.

The handover was controversial in the United States. Ronald Reagan campaigned against the treaties, declaring, "We bought it, we paid for it, it's ours, and we're going to keep it." But the Senate ratified the treaties by one vote.

On December 31, 1999, the Panama Canal and all former Zone territory passed to Panamanian control. The transition was peaceful, and Panama has operated the canal efficiently and profitably ever since.

## What This Means for You in 2026

The Panama Canal Zone lasted 96 years — long enough to seem permanent, but not long enough to be. The lesson of the Zone is that arrangements built on unequal power can endure for generations but will eventually be challenged by the people they disadvantage. If you are in a position of advantage, ask whether the arrangement is sustainable — whether it is built on genuine mutual benefit or simply on the other party's inability to resist. Sustainable partnerships require fairness, not just force.
