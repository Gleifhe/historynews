## The War That Divided America

The Vietnam War was the longest and most controversial conflict in American history up to that point. The United States entered the war to stop communist North Vietnam from taking over democratic South Vietnam. Over two decades, more than 58,000 Americans and an estimated 2 million Vietnamese died.

By 1973, massive anti-war protests at home forced the United States to sign a peace agreement and pull its combat troops out of Vietnam. But the fighting between North and South Vietnam continued. Without American military support, South Vietnam's army began to collapse.

By early 1975, North Vietnamese forces were sweeping south at a pace that stunned everyone. City after city fell. The end was coming — and it was coming fast.

## The Final Days

In April 1975, the North Vietnamese army was closing in on Saigon, the capital of South Vietnam. U.S. Ambassador Graham Martin refused to order a full evacuation for weeks, insisting that the situation could still be saved. His delay made the final evacuation far more desperate and chaotic than it needed to be.

Thousands of South Vietnamese who had worked with the Americans — translators, military officers, government officials — were terrified of what would happen when the communists took over. They begged for help getting out. Many had been promised they would be protected.

On April 29, with North Vietnamese rockets falling on the city's airport, President Gerald Ford finally ordered **Operation Frequent Wind** — the helicopter evacuation of Saigon. It would become the largest helicopter evacuation in history.

## Chaos on the Rooftops

Over the next 18 hours, helicopters flew nonstop from the U.S. Embassy and other locations across the city to aircraft carriers waiting offshore. Pilots flew mission after mission through the night, often in dangerous conditions.

At the embassy, thousands of people — Americans and Vietnamese — crowded into the compound, climbing walls and pushing through gates. Marines used rifle butts to keep the crowds from overwhelming the helicopters. People passed babies and children over the walls to strangers, hoping they would get on a flight.

One of the most famous images from that day shows a line of people climbing a ladder to reach a helicopter on a rooftop. For years, this was incorrectly described as the U.S. Embassy roof. It was actually the top of an apartment building used by the CIA. The photograph, taken by Hubert van Es, became the defining image of America's defeat in Vietnam.

## The Ships and the Sea

The evacuation didn't end at the aircraft carriers. In the days surrounding the fall of Saigon, tens of thousands of Vietnamese fled the country by boat, ship, and any vessel they could find. South Vietnamese navy ships, fishing boats, and cargo vessels headed out to sea, packed with desperate refugees.

Some South Vietnamese helicopter pilots flew their families to the American fleet. When the carrier decks ran out of space, crews pushed millions of dollars' worth of helicopters overboard to make room for more incoming flights. Photographs show Huey helicopters being shoved off the side of the carrier USS Midway into the South China Sea.

## Left Behind

Despite the massive effort, tens of thousands of South Vietnamese who had been promised evacuation were left behind. When the last American helicopter lifted off from the embassy roof at 7:53 a.m. on April 30, roughly 420 Vietnamese were still inside the compound, waiting for flights that would never come.

Hours later, a North Vietnamese tank crashed through the gates of South Vietnam's Presidential Palace. The war was over.

## Why It Still Matters

The fall of Saigon shaped American foreign policy for a generation. The term "Vietnam Syndrome" described the country's reluctance to send troops into foreign conflicts. The images of those desperate rooftop evacuations became a warning about the costs of war and the consequences of broken promises.

For the Vietnamese refugees who escaped — the "boat people" who fled in the months and years that followed — the fall of Saigon was the beginning of a dangerous new chapter. Many settled in the United States, Australia, France, and Canada, building new lives while carrying the memory of everything they had lost.
