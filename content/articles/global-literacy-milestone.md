Here is a fact that might surprise you: for most of human history, almost nobody could read. As recently as 1820, only about 12 percent of the world's population was literate. Books existed, but they were luxuries that only the wealthy and powerful could access. The vast majority of people — farmers, workers, women, and children — lived their entire lives without ever reading a single word.

Today, that number has flipped dramatically. More than 86 percent of the world's adults can read and write. Among young people aged 15 to 24, the rate is even higher — over 91 percent. This is one of the most remarkable achievements in human history, and it happened mostly within the last 60 years.

## The Rise of Literacy

The transformation began accelerating after World War II. In 1960, the global adult literacy rate was only about 42 percent. That meant more than half the world's adults could not read a newspaper, sign their name, or help their children with homework. The situation was especially dire in Africa, South Asia, and parts of Latin America, where literacy rates were below 30 percent.

Several forces drove the change. Newly independent countries in Africa and Asia made education a top priority, building schools and training teachers at a rapid pace. The United Nations, through its agency UNESCO, launched global literacy campaigns and helped countries develop education systems. International organizations invested billions of dollars in schools, textbooks, and teacher training.

The results have been extraordinary. South Asia saw some of the most dramatic improvements. India's literacy rate rose from about 28 percent in 1960 to over 77 percent today. Sub-Saharan Africa's rate more than doubled. Countries like South Korea, which had a literacy rate of about 22 percent after the Korean War, achieved near-universal literacy within a single generation.

The impact of these gains has been profound. Literate people earn higher incomes, live longer, and raise healthier children. Literate women have fewer children and are better able to protect their families from disease. Literate communities are more democratic, more economically productive, and more peaceful. The connection between literacy and human flourishing is one of the most well-documented findings in social science.

## The Tools That Made It Possible

Several key innovations helped drive the literacy revolution. The [printing press](/articles/gutenberg-printing-press/), invented by Gutenberg in the 1400s, made books affordable for the first time. But it was the expansion of public education systems — first in Europe and North America, then around the world — that truly brought reading to the masses.

In the digital age, technology has opened new pathways to literacy. Mobile phones have brought reading material to people in remote villages. Online platforms like [Wikipedia](/articles/wikipedia-launched/) provide free information in hundreds of languages. E-readers and tablets make books accessible to people who live far from any library or bookstore. Radio and television literacy programs reach millions of adults who missed the chance to learn as children.

UNESCO's Education for All initiative, launched in 1990, set ambitious global goals for literacy and education. While not all targets have been met, the campaign has helped focus international attention and resources on the problem. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, adopted in 2015, include a specific target for universal literacy by 2030.

## The Challenge That Remains

Despite the remarkable progress, about 775 million adults worldwide still cannot read or write. Two-thirds of them are women. The problem is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where poverty, conflict, and cultural barriers continue to limit access to education.

Even in wealthy countries, functional illiteracy — the inability to read well enough to navigate daily life — affects millions of people. In the United States, approximately 21 percent of adults read below a fifth-grade level, which makes it difficult to fill out a job application, understand a lease, or read a prescription label.

These numbers remind us that the work is not finished. Literacy is not just about recognizing words on a page. It is about giving people the power to understand the world around them, make informed decisions, and participate fully in society.

## What We Can Learn (Personal Growth)

The global literacy story teaches us that progress is real, even when it is slow and uneven. In a world full of bad news, it is easy to feel like things are getting worse. But the fact that billions more people can read today than could read just 60 years ago is proof that sustained effort makes a difference.

This is an important lesson for our personal lives, too. Growth often happens gradually. You might not notice day-to-day improvement in a skill you are practicing or a habit you are building. But over weeks, months, and years, the progress adds up. Just as the world's literacy rate climbed slowly but steadily from 42 percent to 86 percent, your own growth happens one small step at a time.

The literacy story also reminds us not to take reading for granted. The ability to read is one of the most powerful tools you have. It gives you access to the ideas of every thinker, scientist, and storyteller who ever lived. It allows you to learn anything, go anywhere in your imagination, and understand perspectives very different from your own. Every time you pick up a book, you are exercising a privilege that most humans throughout history never had.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, the global literacy movement faces new challenges and opportunities. Artificial intelligence is creating tools that can help people learn to read more quickly and in their own languages. Translation technology is breaking down language barriers that once limited access to knowledge. Digital libraries are making millions of books available for free to anyone with an internet connection.

At the same time, new forms of literacy are becoming essential. Digital literacy — the ability to navigate the internet, evaluate sources, and protect your privacy online — is increasingly important in a world where misinformation spreads rapidly. Media literacy — understanding how news and information are created and shared — is vital for healthy democracies.

The goal of universal literacy is closer than ever, but it has not been reached. Every child who learns to read, every adult who attends a literacy class, and every community that builds a school is part of this ongoing story. It is one of humanity's greatest achievements — quiet, gradual, and still unfolding.
