## The Most Honest Writing in America

Somewhere in the archives of the Library of Congress, there are more than 110,000 collections of personal narratives from American veterans — letters, diaries, photographs, and recorded oral histories spanning every conflict from World War I to the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. They were gathered through the Veterans History Project, established by Congress in 2000 to preserve the firsthand accounts of those who served.

Read enough of them and a pattern emerges that no historian could fabricate. Across a century of warfare, across every branch and every theater, the letters home say the same thing. Not "I'm brave." Not "the cause is just." Not "I'm proud to serve." Those sentiments appear, but they are not what dominates. What dominates — overwhelmingly, unmistakably — is this: I miss you. I love you. You are what I'm fighting for.

War, it turns out, is the most effective clarity machine ever invented. It burns away everything that doesn't matter and leaves only what does.

## Historical Context

The tradition of soldiers writing letters home is as old as literacy itself. Roman legionaries stationed on Hadrian's Wall scratched messages on wooden tablets asking for socks and news from home. Civil War soldiers wrote by candlelight in muddy trenches, knowing the letter might arrive after they were already dead. Sullivan Ballou's [letter to his wife Sarah](/articles/sullivan-ballou-letter/) — written one week before he was killed at the First Battle of Bull Run — remains one of the most powerful love letters in the English language: "If the dead can come back to this earth and flit unseen around those they loved, I shall always be near you."

What makes the Veterans History Project different from earlier collections is its scale and its democracy. It doesn't collect only the letters of officers or the famous. It collects the words of privates, corporals, nurses, mechanics, and cooks. It records the voices of people who never expected anyone to listen. And because of that breadth, the patterns it reveals are not literary — they are statistical.

Andrew Carroll, founder of the Legacy Project, spent years reading war letters from every American conflict. In his book *War Letters*, he noted that the overwhelming majority of personal correspondence from combat zones focuses not on the war itself but on the people back home. Soldiers write about their children's birthdays they're missing. They write about the meals their mothers used to cook. They write about the ordinary Tuesdays they took for granted.

"Nobody writes home about strategy," Carroll observed. "They write about what they'd give to be sitting on the porch with the people they love."

## The Core Lesson: Danger Clarifies Value

Psychologists have a term for what happens when mortality becomes real: it's called mortality salience. Terror Management Theory, developed by researchers Sheldon Solomon, Jeff Greenberg, and Tom Pyszczynski in the 1980s, demonstrates that when people are reminded of their own death, their priorities shift dramatically. Superficial concerns — status, money, career advancement — fade. What rises to the top is connection: family, love, belonging.

Veterans don't need a psychology textbook to understand this. They lived it. A soldier lying in a foxhole at night, listening to incoming artillery, does not think about his quarterly performance review. He thinks about his daughter's laugh. He thinks about the last thing he said to his wife and whether it was kind enough. He thinks about his father and wishes he had said "I love you" more often.

This is not sentimentality. It is clarity purchased at an extraordinary price.

The [last letters written by soldiers who knew they might not survive](/articles/letters-home-last-words/) are the purest distillation of this principle. When a person has only one page and possibly only one hour, they do not waste words on trivia. They write what matters. And what matters, in letter after letter after letter, is the same: people.

## Real-World Examples

The pattern holds across every conflict.

**World War II.** Private First Class Richard Romero wrote to his mother from Normandy in 1944: "Mom, I don't care about the car or the house or any of that. I just want to sit at your kitchen table one more time." He survived the war and, according to his oral history in the Veterans History Project, spent every Sunday at that kitchen table for the rest of his mother's life.

**Korea.** Corporal James Cardinal wrote home from the frozen hills near the Chosin Reservoir: "I used to think I needed to make something of myself. Now I know I just need to make it home to you." The simplicity of the statement is its power. War had edited his ambitions down to a single line.

**Vietnam.** Army nurse Lieutenant Sharon Lane, one of the few American women killed by hostile fire in Vietnam, wrote to her family regularly about the soldiers she treated. Her letters, preserved by the Vietnam Women's Memorial Foundation, focused relentlessly on the connections between her patients and their families — the photographs they carried, the names they called out in pain.

**Iraq and Afghanistan.** In the post-9/11 era, soldiers could communicate by email and video call, but the impulse remained identical. Staff Sergeant Marc Golczynski, killed in Iraq in 2007, left a letter for his wife that read in part: "My time in the Army has taught me one thing above all others — nothing is guaranteed except the love we give while we're here."

These are not isolated anecdotes. They are representative samples from an archive of over 100,000 collections. The consistency is the finding.

## Personal Growth: What Veterans Know That Civilians Forget

You do not need to go to war to learn what war teaches. But you do need to pay attention.

The veterans whose letters fill the Library of Congress discovered, under the worst possible circumstances, a truth that most civilians sense but rarely act on: the people in your life are not a backdrop to your achievements. They are the achievement. The promotion, the degree, the house, the car — none of it means anything if you have no one to share it with. And none of it will comfort you when the stakes are real.

The practical application is simple but difficult. It means putting down the phone during dinner. It means saying "I love you" today, not when you get around to it. It means choosing the recital over the meeting, the conversation over the email, the person over the task. It means living as if your time is limited — because it is.

Robert Waldinger, director of the Harvard Study of Adult Development — the longest-running study of happiness in history — confirmed what veterans have been writing in letters for a century: "Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period." The study, which has tracked participants for over 85 years, found that the quality of a person's relationships at age 50 was the single best predictor of health at age 80. Not cholesterol. Not career success. Not income. Relationships.

Veterans know this in their bones. The question is whether the rest of us can learn it without the foxhole.

## How This Connects to 2026

In 2026, Americans spend an average of over seven hours per day on screens. Social media has created the illusion of connection while often eroding its substance. Loneliness has been declared a public health epidemic by the U.S. Surgeon General, with health effects comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes a day.

Against that backdrop, the letters in the Veterans History Project read like dispatches from a world that understood something we are losing. Those soldiers, separated from everyone they loved by oceans and war zones, valued human connection with a ferocity that our hyperconnected age struggles to match.

The lesson is not that we need a war to teach us what matters. The lesson is that we already know what matters — we've known for a century, and it's written in 110,000 collections at the Library of Congress. We just need to act like we know it.

Start today. Write the letter. Make the call. Show up. The veterans did it from foxholes and field hospitals with pencil stubs and borrowed paper. You can do it from your living room.
