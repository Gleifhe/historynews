In 1958, a junior at Lancaster High School in Ohio sat at his kitchen table with an old 48-star flag, a sewing machine, and an assignment that would make history. Robert G. Heft had been told by his history teacher, Stanley Pratt, to create a project related to government. Most students wrote reports. Heft decided to design the flag that the United States would use when Alaska and Hawaii became states.

## The B-Minus

Heft spent twelve hours cutting apart the existing flag and rearranging the stars into a pattern that could accommodate fifty. He settled on alternating rows of six and five stars — five rows of six and four rows of five. When he turned the project in, Pratt was unimpressed. He gave Heft a B-minus. "It lacks originality," the teacher reportedly said.

Heft was indignant. He told Pratt he would prove the design was good enough for Congress. Pratt agreed to raise the grade if the design was accepted.

## The Campaign

Over the next two years, Heft wrote to his congressman, Walter Moeller, and to anyone else who might listen. Alaska became the 49th state on January 3, 1959, and a 49-star flag was adopted. But everyone knew Hawaii was close behind, and the 49-star flag would be short-lived.

Heft's design was one of approximately 1,500 submissions received by Congress for the new flag. But Heft had something the other designers did not — a persistent congressman willing to champion his entry. Moeller personally presented Heft's design, and it was selected.

On July 4, 1960, the 50-star flag was raised for the first time over Fort McHenry in Baltimore — the same fort where Francis Scott Key had watched the [Star-Spangled Banner fly under bombardment](/articles/star-spangled-banner/) 146 years earlier. President Eisenhower presided over the ceremony. Seventeen-year-old Robert Heft was there.

His teacher changed his grade to an A.

## The Longest-Serving Flag

Heft's design has outlasted every previous version of the American flag. The 48-star flag served from 1912 to 1959 — 47 years. As of 2026, Heft's 50-star flag has been in service for 66 years and counting, making it the longest-serving design in the flag's history.

Heft went on to become a teacher himself and later a mayor and a college professor. He spent much of his life traveling the country telling his story. He kept the original flag he sewed at his kitchen table and displayed it at events. He estimated it was flown over 88 state capitols and embassies before his death in 2009.

## The Competition Heft Never Knew

Heft's design was not the only 50-star arrangement considered. The U.S. Army's Institute of Heraldry had been quietly developing its own proposals for months before Congress acted. Among the roughly 1,500 submissions were staggered diamond patterns, circular star arrangements, and designs that placed stars in the shape of a larger star. The Institute ultimately recommended the same alternating rows of six and five that Heft had sewn at his kitchen table — a coincidence that strengthened his claim. President Eisenhower personally selected the final design from three finalists on August 21, 1959, through Executive Order 10834, which also specified precise proportions, color shades, and star placement down to the fraction of an inch. Heft's hand-stitched prototype had gotten the pattern right, even if his measurements were not exact.

## What This Means for You in 2026

Robert Heft was a teenager with a sewing machine and a mediocre grade. He did not accept the verdict. He believed in his work and found a way to put it in front of the people who could recognize it. A B-minus is not a final answer — it is one person's opinion. If you have done the work and you believe in it, do not let a single grade, rejection, or criticism stop you. Find the right audience.
